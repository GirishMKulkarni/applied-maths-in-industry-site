What optimization is: the best plan under limits

This is the idea behind the whole optimisation block of the course. Read it once and Session 20 will feel easy. No proofs here, just the picture.

The problem it solves

You almost always have limits. A fixed budget. A fixed number of delivery vans. Only so many hours in a shift. So much space in a warehouse. Inside those limits there are usually many plans you could choose, and they are not equally good. One meal plan is cheaper than another. One delivery schedule burns less fuel. One production mix earns more profit.

Optimisation is the art of picking the single best plan the limits allow. Not a prediction of what will happen, but a decision about what to do.

The one idea

Every optimisation problem has the same three pieces. Once you can spot them in a paragraph of plain business English, you can model almost anything.

  • The things you choose. How many servings of dal. How many vans on each route. How many tables to build this week. These are the knobs you can turn. We call them the decision variables.
  • The one number you care about. Total cost, total profit, total distance. You want to push this number as low or as high as it will go. This is the objective.
  • The rules you must obey. The budget. The van count. "At least 50 grams of protein." "Ship no more than the warehouse holds." Each rule is a constraint.

The whole job is: turn the knobs to make the one number as good as possible, without ever breaking a rule.

The picture: corners of a shape

Here is the surprise that makes it beautiful. Draw a simple problem with two decisions, say tables across the bottom and chairs up the side. Each rule ("no more than 6 assembly hours") is a straight line, and it allows everything on one side of that line. Stack a few rules and the plans that obey all of them form a shape with straight edges, like a cut gemstone seen from above.

Every point inside that shape is an allowed plan. We call the shape the feasible region. Now here is the good part: when the objective is a plain weighted sum (no squares, no curves), the best plan is never hiding somewhere in the middle. It always sits at a corner of the shape.

Why? Picture the profit as a gentle slope tilting the whole floor. A marble placed anywhere rolls downhill in one steady direction and never settles in the open middle. It comes to rest jammed into a corner. A straight objective behaves exactly like that slope, so the best plan is always pinned at a corner.

That is a huge simplification. A shape has infinitely many points inside it but only a handful of corners. So the computer never has to search the whole region. It just checks the corners and keeps the best one.

A tiny example

Suppose a thali should give you at least 50 g of protein and at least 12 mg of iron, and you can build it from dal (₹8 a serving, lots of protein) and spinach (₹6 a serving, lots of iron). Too much dal is dear; too much spinach misses the protein. Somewhere between them sits the cheapest plate that still clears both targets. That "cheapest plate that clears every target" is a linear program, and it is exactly the first thing you will solve in the lab.

You do not need calculus or proofs

You do not find the best corner by hand, and you do not prove anything. You write down the three pieces (choices, objective, rules) in a few readable lines, and a free tool called a solver does the searching and hands you the answer. Your skill is the modelling, turning a business paragraph into those three pieces. The solver is just a very fast, very reliable calculator for corners.

Why industry cares

This is one of the most valuable skills a numbers person can bring to a company. Refineries blend crude into petrol with it. Indian Railways builds timetables with it. Flipkart and Delhivery decide which warehouse serves which city with it. Airlines roster their crews with it. The sums are enormous: a one-percent better blend at a refinery is worth crores a year. Prediction tells a business what will happen; optimisation tells it what to do, and that second answer is the one people pay the most for.
