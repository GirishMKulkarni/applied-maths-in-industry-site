Graphs and DAGs

This is a ten-minute, picture-first look at the one maths idea hiding under Git. You do not need it to finish Session 4. It is here for anyone who is curious why a "commit history" is really a well-known mathematical object.

A graph is dots and arrows

Forget the graphs with x and y axes from school. In this part of maths a graph is something simpler: a bunch of dots, with lines or arrows joining some of them.

  • The dots are called nodes (or vertices). A node can be anything: a city, a person, a web page, a task.
  • The lines are called edges. An edge says "these two are connected".

The Delhi Metro map is a graph: stations are nodes, tracks are edges. Your list of friends on a social app is a graph: people are nodes, a friendship is an edge.

"Directed" means the arrows point one way

Sometimes a connection has a direction. On the metro you can go both ways, so those edges have no arrow. But some connections only go one way.

Think of "who follows whom" on a social app. You following a cricketer does not mean the cricketer follows you. So we draw an arrow from you to the cricketer, not a plain line. A graph whose edges are arrows is a directed graph.

"Acyclic" means you can never loop back

Now add one rule: follow the arrows, and you can never come back to where you started. No round trips. That "no loops" property is the word acyclic (a-cyclic, "no cycle").

Picture a family tree drawn with arrows from each person to their parents. You can walk from yourself up to a grandparent, but you can never follow parent-arrows and arrive back at yourself. Time only goes one way. A family tree is acyclic.

Put the three words together and you get a directed acyclic graph, or DAG: dots joined by one-way arrows, with no way to loop back to the start.

Why industry cares

DAGs are everywhere once you notice them, because so many real things are "steps that depend on earlier steps and never circle back":

  • A build system: to make the final report you first need the cleaned data, which first needs the raw file. Arrows point to what a step depends on. No loops.
  • A spreadsheet: cell C depends on cell B, which depends on cell A. If it looped, Excel would throw a "circular reference" error, which is just "your graph has a cycle".
  • Project scheduling: task "paint the wall" must come after "plaster the wall".
  • Git, which is where Session 4 meets one. Each save point (a commit) points back to the save point it grew from, its parent. Time only moves forward, so the arrows never form a loop. Your whole project history is a DAG, and every Git command (log, branch, merge, undo) is just tidy bookkeeping on that graph.

The smallest example

Three commits, made one after another:

   C1  <-----  C2  <-----  C3
 (first)     (second)    (newest)

Read the arrows as "grew from". C3 grew from C2, which grew from C1. It is directed (arrows have a direction) and acyclic (you can never get back to C1 by following arrows forward). That is the entire mathematical object. When you make a branch, one node simply gets two arrows coming out of it, and the picture fans out.

Where you'll use this

Session 4 (Git and GitHub) names the DAG as its "math object", in the optional box. The same dots-and-arrows idea returns whenever one thing depends on another: task schedulers, data pipelines, and dependency graphs later in the course.
