Optimization: objectives and constraints

A lot of the second half of this course is about one kind of question: out of all the choices you are allowed to make, which one is best? That question has a tidy shape, and once you see the shape you can read almost any optimization problem. Read this before the operations-research sessions. No proofs, just the picture.

The shape of every optimization problem

Three pieces. That is all.

  • Decision variables. The knobs you are allowed to turn. How many tonnes to ship. Which warehouse to open. Whether to put this parcel on the van. These are the things you get to choose.
  • An objective. The single number you are trying to make as good as possible. Sometimes you want it as big as possible (profit, value delivered) and sometimes as small as possible (cost, distance, time). "Best" always means best on this one number.
  • Constraints. The rules you must not break. The van holds only 500 kg. You cannot spend more than the budget. Every customer must be served by someone. These fence off the choices that are actually allowed.

Put in words: turn the knobs to make the objective as good as possible, without breaking any rule. A choice that breaks no rule is called feasible. The best feasible choice is the optimum, the answer we are after.

A tiny example

A snack stall makes samosas and vada pav. Each samosa earns ₹6 profit, each vada pav earns ₹8. The stall has only so much oil and only so many hours before the evening rush.

  • Decision variables: how many samosas, how many vada pav.
  • Objective: total profit, 6 × samosas + 8 × vadapav, as large as possible.
  • Constraints: oil used must fit the stock; hours used must fit the time.

Every combination that respects the oil and time limits is feasible. Somewhere among them is the mix that earns the most. Finding that mix by hand is fiddly. Finding it with a solver is one short program.

What a solver does

You do not search for the best choice yourself. You describe the three pieces to a solver, a ready-made program whose whole job is to find the best feasible choice for you. In this course the solver is free and open-source (PuLP hands your model to a solver called CBC), and you never see the clever search it runs inside. You write the goal and the rules in plain Python; the solver returns the numbers.

The skill you are building is not the search. It is the modelling: taking a messy business situation and writing it down as a goal, some rules, and the knobs you control. Do that faithfully and the solver does the rest.

When the knobs only click into whole notches

So far imagine the knobs turn smoothly, so an answer like "ship 2.4 tonnes" is fine. That smooth world is linear programming, and it is the easy case: the best answer sits neatly at a corner of the allowed region and the solver finds it almost instantly.

But some decisions are not smooth. You cannot open half a warehouse, hire 3.7 drivers, or put 0.6 of a parcel on a van. Those knobs only click into whole numbers, and very often into just two settings, 0 or 1, a plain yes/no. Add that "must be whole" demand and you have an integer program.

It sounds like a small change. It is not. The tidy corner answer from the smooth version is usually a fraction, and you cannot simply round it off, because rounding can break a rule or leave value on the table. Coping with whole-number decisions is genuinely harder, and it is exactly what the integer-programming session is about.

Why industry cares

This shape fits an enormous amount of real decision-making. Which projects to fund inside a budget. Where to place fulfilment centres. How to route a fleet of delivery vans. How to build a portfolio that earns the most for a given risk. All of them are "make this number best without breaking these rules", and all of them are solved by describing the three pieces and letting a solver work.

Where you'll use this: Session 20 (linear programming), Session 21 (integer programming and routing), and Session 22 (convex optimization). The same three pieces — variables, objective, constraints — come back every time.
