Distance between two points

The whole of clustering leans on one small idea: how far apart are two things? Once you can put a number on "how far", the computer can find which things sit close together and group them. Read this before Session 11 and clustering will feel easy. No proofs here, just the picture.

Two customers as two dots

Take two customers from a shop. Describe each by two numbers: how many times they visited this month, and how much they spent. Now each customer is a dot on a chart, visits across the bottom, spend up the side.

Asha  →  (12 visits, ₹8,000)
Ravi  →  (3 visits,  ₹1,500)

Two customers who behave alike sit near each other on the chart. Two very different customers sit far apart. "Similar" just means "close", and "different" means "far". That is the entire idea a clustering method uses: it keeps asking "which dots are close?" and puts the close ones in the same group.

Straight-line distance

So how far apart are two dots? The natural answer is the length of the straight line joining them, the distance you would measure with a ruler on the chart. If two dots are at the same spot the distance is zero; the further apart they drift, the bigger the number.

For dots on a flat chart this is the school "Pythagoras" distance: take the gap along the bottom, take the gap up the side, and combine them into one length. You do not compute it by hand. numpy and scikit-learn do it for you. All you need to carry is the picture: distance is how far apart two dots are, and small distance means similar.

The same idea keeps working when each thing has more than two numbers. A customer described by three numbers (recency, frequency, money spent) is a dot in three dimensions, and one described by fifty numbers is a dot in fifty dimensions. You cannot picture fifty dimensions, but the computer measures the distance between two such dots exactly the same way. Nothing new happens; there are just more numbers to combine.

Why you must put every column on the same scale first

Here is the trap that catches everyone the first time. Suppose one column is age in years (roughly 20 to 60) and another is income in rupees (roughly 20,000 to 90,000). When you combine the gaps into a distance, the income gap is in the tens of thousands and the age gap is in the tens. The income completely drowns out the age. Two people the same age but ₹100 apart look "close"; two people ₹100 apart but thirty years apart still look "close". Age has effectively vanished from the distance.

The fix is to put every column on the same footing before measuring anything. The standard move is to standardise each column: shift it so its average is 0 and stretch or squeeze it so its spread is 1 (this rescaled value is called a z-score). After that, a one-step change in age counts the same as a one-step change in income, and every column gets a fair vote. In the notebooks this is one line, StandardScaler, and skipping it is the number one reason clustering gives nonsense. Scale first, always.

Other ways to measure "far" (optional, skip on a first read)

Straight-line distance (its proper name is Euclidean distance) is the default and the only one you need for Session 11. Two others exist for when they fit better:

  • Manhattan distance adds up the gaps one column at a time, like walking a city grid where you cannot cut across the block. It is steadier when a stray column misbehaves.
  • Cosine distance ignores how big the numbers are and looks only at the direction they point. It is the right choice for text and recommendations, where a blockbuster and a niche film can still count as "similar" if the same kind of people like both.

You can do all of Session 11 knowing only the straight-line one. Keep the others in your back pocket.

Why industry cares

Every "find the similar ones" task is a distance underneath. Group customers who behave alike so you can treat each group differently. Group localities by rent and amenities so a property site can suggest "areas like this one". Flag a payment that sits far from a customer's usual behaviour as possible fraud. All of it is the same move: measure how far apart, then act on what is close and what is far.
