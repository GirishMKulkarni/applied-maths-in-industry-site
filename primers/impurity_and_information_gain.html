Impurity and information gain

This is a ten-minute, picture-first look at the one idea hiding inside a decision tree: how the tree decides which question to ask. You do not need it to finish Session 10. It is here for anyone curious how a tree learns.

A tree is a flowchart of yes/no questions

Think about how a loan officer might decide, on paper, whether to approve a loan. "Is the monthly income above ₹30,000?" If yes, "is the credit score above 650?" and so on, until they reach a decision. That chain of yes/no questions is a decision tree. Each question splits the applicants in front of you into two smaller groups.

The only clever part is choosing the questions. The tree learns them from past data, and it learns them with one simple goal: make tidy groups.

A good question makes tidy groups

Picture your past applicants as coloured dots. Blue means they repaid, red means they defaulted. Before you ask anything, the pile is mixed: blues and reds all jumbled together.

A good question splits that pile so that one side is almost all blue and the other side is almost all red. That is what we mean by tidy. A useless question leaves both sides just as mixed as before.

So the tree is really playing one game over and over: find the question that best separates the colours.

"Impurity" is a number for how mixed a group is

To play that game the tree needs to measure mixed-ness. That measure is called impurity. It works like this:

  • A group that is all one colour is perfectly tidy, so its impurity is 0.
  • A group that is a 50/50 mix is as messy as possible, so its impurity is at its largest.
  • Anything in between sits in between.

There are two common ways to put a number on it, and they tell the same story:

  • Gini impurity — the everyday choice, what scikit-learn shows in each box of the tree picture.
  • Entropy — the same idea borrowed from information theory, using logarithms.

You do not need the formulas to read a tree. Here is the intuition as a table (exact numbers are in the notebook's optional cell):

the group how mixed impurity
all repaid perfectly tidy 0 (lowest)
80 repaid, 20 default fairly tidy small
50 repaid, 50 default as messy as it gets largest

Information gain = how much tidier a question made things

Now the tree can score a question. Measure the impurity before the split. Measure the impurity of the two groups after the split, and combine them by size. The drop from before to after is the information gain: how much mess the question removed.

The whole learning rule is now just:

  1. Try every sensible question.
  2. Keep the one with the biggest information gain.
  3. Repeat inside each new group, until the groups are tidy enough or you tell it to stop.

That is a decision tree, start to finish.

Why industry cares

Two reasons. First, a tree is readable. You can print the flowchart and show a manager, an auditor, or a regulator the exact reason a loan was refused. Few models can do that.

Second, this same "reduce impurity" engine sits inside the models that win on real business tables: Random Forests and XGBoost, used every day for credit scoring, churn prediction, and fraud detection at banks, NBFCs and lending apps.

The smallest example

Ten past applicants: 5 repaid, 5 defaulted. As mixed as it gets, impurity at its peak. You ask "is the credit score above 650?" and the pile splits into:

  • above 650: 4 repaid, 0 defaulted — perfectly tidy.
  • 650 or below: 1 repaid, 5 defaulted — mostly red.

Both sides are far tidier than the messy start, so the impurity dropped a lot. Big information gain. That is a good question, and the tree would keep it.

Where you'll use this
