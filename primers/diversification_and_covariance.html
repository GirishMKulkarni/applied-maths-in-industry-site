Spread, moving together, and diversification

This is the whole idea behind the portfolio session. It sounds like finance, but underneath it is one simple picture about numbers that bounce around. No proofs here, just the picture. Ten minutes and the session will feel easy.

Return: how much a stock earns

A stock's price changes every day. The return for a day is just the percentage change: if a share goes from ₹100 to ₹102, that is a +2% day. Returns are nicer to work with than raw prices, because they put a ₹50 stock and a ₹5000 stock on the same footing. A stock's expected return is roughly the average of its daily returns, scaled up to a year. Higher is better. That part is easy.

Risk is just spread

Now the more interesting number. Two stocks can both average, say, 12% a year, and yet feel completely different to own. One drifts up smoothly. The other lurches: up 6% one week, down 5% the next, up 8%, down 7%. The second one is riskier, even though the average is the same.

Risk is a name for how much the returns bounce around their average. A calm stock's returns huddle close to the average; a wild stock's returns are scattered far from it. The everyday word for that scatter is spread, and the standard way to measure spread is the standard deviation: the typical distance of the daily returns from their average. In finance the standard deviation of returns has its own name, volatility. Big volatility means a bumpy ride.

calm stock   returns:  1.0   0.9   1.1   1.0   0.9    -> small spread, low risk
wild stock   returns:  6.0  -5.0   8.0  -7.0   5.0    -> big spread,   high risk

Both average to about 1, but the second is far riskier. That is the whole idea of risk: same average, different spread.

(You will also hear variance. Variance is just the standard deviation squared. The maths is a little tidier with variance, so formulas use it, but it means the same thing: bigger variance, bigger spread, more risk.)

Moving together: covariance

Here is the idea that makes portfolios work. Owning two stocks is not the same as owning each alone, because of how they move together.

Think of two stocks over the same days.

  • If they tend to rise on the same days and fall on the same days, they move together. A software services firm and another software services firm often do this, because the same news lifts both.
  • If one tends to rise on the days the other falls, they move apart. An airline and an oil producer can behave like this, because cheap oil helps the airline and hurts the producer.
  • If there is no pattern between them, they move independently.

The single number that captures which of these is happening is called the covariance. Positive covariance means "they move together". Negative covariance means "they move opposite". Near zero means "no real link". You never compute it by hand today; the computer does it in one line. You just need the picture: covariance is a togetherness score for a pair of stocks.

When you have many stocks, there is a covariance for every pair. Stack all those numbers into a table, one row and one column per stock, and you get the covariance matrix, written Σ (the Greek capital letter Sigma). It is just a table of togetherness scores. The diagonal, where a stock meets itself, is that stock's own spread. You will see it drawn as a coloured heatmap in the notebook.

Why mixing lowers risk

Now the payoff, and it is genuinely a little bit magic.

Suppose you split your money between two stocks that do not move together. On a day the first one drops, the second often holds up or rises, so it cushions the fall. On a day the second drops, the first cushions it. The bumps partly cancel, and the mix bounces around less than either stock does on its own.

That is diversification: by spreading across things that move differently, you get the same expected return with less risk. "Don't put all your eggs in one basket" is not just folk wisdom, it is a fact about spread and covariance. And it is why the covariance matrix Σ is the star of the session: it is exactly the information you need to find the mix whose bumps cancel best.

One honest caveat. Diversification can only cancel the bumps that are specific to individual companies. When the whole market falls together, as in a crash, almost everything moves the same way at once and there is a floor of risk you cannot diversify away. Mixing helps a lot; it is not a force field.

What you'll do with this

In the session you measure each stock's return and risk, build the covariance table Σ, and then let the computer search through all the possible mixes for the one with the least bounce at each level of return. The best mixes trace a curve called the efficient frontier. Every step rests on the two ideas above: risk is spread, and covariance is how things move together.
