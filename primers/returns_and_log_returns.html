Returns and log-returns

This is the one idea behind the whole finance block of the course: don't study the price, study how much it changed. Read this before Week 7 and the session will feel easy. No proofs here, just the picture.

The problem it solves

Suppose Reliance trades near ₹3,000 and a small stock trades near ₹100. Reliance goes up ₹30 today and the small stock goes up ₹10. Who had the better day?

The rupee amounts say Reliance (₹30 beats ₹10). But ₹30 on ₹3,000 is a 1% move, while ₹10 on ₹100 is a 10% move. The small stock had the far bigger day. Raw prices hide this. We need a number that is fair across a ₹100 stock and a ₹3,000 stock.

That number is the return.

A return is the percent change, day to day

A return is simply how much the price changed, as a fraction of where it started:

return today = (price today − price yesterday) / price yesterday

If a stock goes from ₹100 to ₹102, the return is (102 − 100) / 100 = 0.02, i.e. +2%. If it drops to ₹98, the return is −2%. That is it. A return is the day's move expressed as a percentage, so a 2% move means the same thing for any stock at any price. This is the "simple return", and it is what your broking app shows you in green or red each evening.

Log-returns: the same thing, in a form that adds up nicely

There is a second, closely related way to measure the same daily move, called the log-return. It is the natural logarithm of the price ratio:

log-return today = ln( price today / price yesterday )

For everyday moves (a percent or two) the log-return and the simple return are almost the same number. A +2% simple return is a +1.98% log-return. So why bother?

Because log-returns add up over time, and simple returns do not. Say a stock rises 10% one day and falls 10% the next. Simple returns suggest you are back to even, but you are not: 100 → 110 → 99, a small loss. Simple returns have to be multiplied together, which is fiddly. Log-returns just add: the log-return over a week is the plain sum of the five daily log-returns. That one property makes months of algebra later in the course disappear, which is why every quant uses them.

So: a return is "percent change, day to day". A log-return is "the same thing, in a form that adds up nicely". Whenever this course says "returns", picture the first one, and know the second is a tidier twin used under the hood.

Why we study returns and not prices

Two more reasons this switch matters, both of which you will see with your own eyes in the lab:

  • A price wanders; a return sits still. A price drifts up and down for years with no fixed level. Its return hovers around zero with a roughly steady spread. A quantity that keeps a stable character is one you can actually study and model. (The proper word for "keeps a stable character" is stationary.)
  • Returns are comparable. Across stocks, across years, across a ₹100 penny stock and the whole Nifty 50, a return is a return.

Why industry cares

Before anyone at a bank, a mutual fund, or a trading desk forecasts a price, measures risk, or builds a portfolio, the very first step is always the same: pull the prices and turn them into returns. Risk numbers, portfolio weights, and options prices are all built on returns, never on raw prices. Getting this one conversion right is the foundation the rest of the finance block stands on.

Where you'll use this: Session 14 (financial data and time series), and again in Session 15 (forecasting), Session 16 (portfolios) and every risk topic after.
