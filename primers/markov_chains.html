A system that hops between states — Markov chains

This is the picture behind Session 24. It sounds technical, but the whole idea is something you can watch in your head: a system that is always in one of a few situations and hops between them, one step at a time. No proofs here, just the picture and the few words that go with it.

States and hops

Think of something that is always in exactly one of a few situations, and every so often it changes. The weather today is sunny or rainy. A phone customer is loyal to their network or has ported out to a rival. A machine on a factory floor is working or broken. A shop shelf holds this many units today and a different number tomorrow.

Each of those situations is a state. A move from one to another is a transition, or a hop. A step is one tick of the clock: one day for the weather, one month for the customer, one shift for the machine.

The one rule: only "now" matters

To predict the next step, all you need is the current state. Tomorrow's weather depends on today's, not on the whole month before it. When that is true, the system is called a Markov chain, and the rule has a name: the Markov property, or "memoryless".

It is an assumption, and often a very good one. Its payoff is huge: because only the present matters, the entire system is captured by one small table of chances.

The table of chances

List the states down the side and across the top. In each box write the chance of hopping from the side-state to the top-state in a single step. That grid is the transition matrix. (If a grid of numbers is new, the primer Vectors and matrices has the picture; a matrix is just a table of numbers.)

Here is the weather as a table:

             to Sunny   to Rainy
from Sunny      0.7        0.3
from Rainy      0.4        0.6

Read the top row as "if it is sunny today, there is a 70% chance of sun tomorrow and a 30% chance of rain." One rule the table must always obey: each row adds up to 1, because from wherever you are, something has to happen next.

Where it settles: the long run

Here is the surprising and useful part. Start the weather off as "definitely sunny" and roll it forward with those chances, day after day. The chance of sun drifts, then stops drifting, and settles near 57%. Now start it off as "definitely rainy" instead. It settles at the same 57%. After a while the system forgets where it began.

That settled mix, roughly 57% sunny and 43% rainy, is the stationary distribution, also called the steady state. It is the long-run share of time the system spends in each state: the long-run share of sunny days, the long-run fraction of customers still loyal, the long-run fraction of time a machine is up and running.

You can find that settled mix two ways, and both show up in the lab. You can roll the system forward until it stops moving. Or you can just play it out at random for a long time and count how often each state comes up. They agree.

Why industry cares

This one idea is everywhere. Google's original PageRank treats the whole web as a giant Markov chain, a random surfer clicking links, and the settled mix ranks the pages by long-run popularity. Banks and telecom firms model customers hopping between "active", "at-risk" and "gone" to forecast churn and value their customer base. Engineers model machines hopping between working and broken to plan maintenance. And a restock rule for a warehouse is a Markov chain on stock levels, which is exactly the case study you build in this session.

The appeal is always the same: a messy, dynamic, random question, "what happens to this system in the long run?", turns into something you can just compute or simulate.
