Overfitting and regularization

This is the idea behind Week 4's Session 8. Read it before that class and the lab will feel easy. No proofs here, just the picture.

The problem it solves

You train a model on past data, it looks brilliant on that data, and then it embarrasses you the moment it meets something new. A house-price model trained on a few Pune localities quotes a sensible price for those flats, then tags an ₹80 lakh flat in a locality it barely saw at ₹2 crore. It did not learn the real trend. It memorised the quirks of the flats it happened to see.

That failure has a name: overfitting. The model fit the training data too snugly, including the random noise, so it does great on what it has seen and badly on what it has not.

The picture: memorising versus understanding

Think of two students preparing for an exam.

  • One understands the subject. Give her a question she has never seen and she reasons it out.
  • The other memorised last year's paper word for word. On those exact questions he is perfect. Change the numbers and he is lost.

A model that overfits is the second student. It has memorised, not understood. The whole game in machine learning is to build the first kind of student.

Why models over-trust features

Left to itself, a model will grab any pattern that helps it fit the data it has seen, even a coincidence. If, by pure chance, expensive flats in your sample tended to have odd door numbers, an unconstrained model will happily decide door number matters and crank up its weight. On new flats that "rule" is nonsense, and the prediction blows up.

The more freedom the model has, the more of these coincidences it can chase. A model with large, wild weights is usually a model that has over-trusted a few features.

The fix: turn down the volume

Regularization is a gentle rule added while the model learns: "don't over-trust any one feature." Concretely, we charge the model a small fee for making any weight large. It now has to earn every bit of weight it uses, so it spends weight only where the pattern is real and stops chasing coincidences.

The result is a model that fits the training data slightly less snugly and does noticeably better on new data. That trade is almost always worth it, because new data is the only data that ever mattered.

There is a single knob, usually called alpha, for how firmly you turn the volume down:

  • alpha small: barely any effect, close to the plain model (risk: still overfits).
  • alpha large: everything is quietened hard (risk: the model becomes too timid and underfits, missing the real trend too).
  • the best alpha is somewhere in between, and you find it by cross-validation — trying several settings on held-out slices of the training data and keeping the one that does best, without ever peeking at the final test set.

Two flavours you will meet

Both turn the volume down; they differ in how.

  • Ridge shrinks every weight a little, smoothly. Nothing is dropped; the loud features just get quieter. Good when many features each contribute a bit.
  • Lasso pushes some weights all the way to exactly zero, switching those features off. It hands you a shorter list of features that actually matter, which is why people call it automatic feature selection. Good when you suspect a lot of the columns are useless.
  • ElasticNet is a blend of the two, for when you want a bit of both.

You do not need to know the formulas to use these. The session's optional box has them for anyone curious.

Why industry cares

Every model that goes into production faces data it was not trained on: tomorrow's customers, next month's prices, a new city's flats. A model that overfits looks great in the demo and fails in the field. Regularization is one of the cheapest, most reliable ways to make a model behave on data it has never seen, so it shows up in nearly every real prediction system, from credit scoring at a bank to demand forecasting at Flipkart.
