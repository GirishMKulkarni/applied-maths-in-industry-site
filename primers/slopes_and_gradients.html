Slopes and gradients: which way is downhill

This is the picture behind the phrase "the model learns". Almost every model in the course is trained by rolling downhill, and this page is that idea in plain words. No proofs, just the picture. Read it before Session 7 or Session 13 and those sessions will feel easy.

The problem it solves

A model has some dials on it: a line has a slope and a starting value; a neural network has thousands of weights. Each setting of the dials makes the model a little right or a little wrong on your data. You want the setting that makes it as right as possible. Trying every combination is hopeless once there is more than a couple of dials. So we need a way to improve the dials without searching blindly.

Height is "how wrong"

Picture a landscape. Sideways is a dial you can turn. Height is how wrong the model is at that setting, a single number we call the error or the loss. A high spot means the model is doing badly; a low spot means it is doing well. The best dial setting is the lowest point of the valley.

You cannot see the whole landscape at once. You can only feel the ground right where you are standing. So how do you get to the bottom? You feel which way is downhill, take a small step that way, and repeat.

The slope tells you which way is down

The slope of the ground at your feet is exactly that "which way is downhill, and how steep" information. A steep slope means a big improvement is nearby, so a step helps a lot. A gentle slope means you are close to flat ground, near the bottom. When the slope is zero, the ground is level and you have arrived.

For a single dial, the slope is one number: negative means "down is to the right", positive means "down is to the left". You step the opposite way to the slope, and you go downhill.

A gradient is just the slope with many dials

When the model has many dials at once, "which way is downhill" is no longer a single left-or-right. It is a direction across all the dials together: turn this one down a lot, that one up a touch, leave this one alone. That whole bundle of "which way, and how much, for every dial" is called the gradient. It is the same idea as a slope, only pointing through a space with one axis per dial.

You do not have to picture that high-dimensional space. Hold on to the one-dial version: the gradient tells you which way is downhill, and you step against it.

Gradient descent: step downhill, repeat

The recipe is called gradient descent, and it is three lines forever:

  1. Work out the gradient where you are (which way is downhill).
  2. Take a small step in the downhill direction.
  3. Repeat until the ground is flat.

The size of each step is the learning rate. Too large and you leap over the valley and bounce around, maybe flying off entirely. Too small and you crawl and it takes forever. A sensible middle step size is why training "just works" most of the time, and picking it is most of the art.

Where the chain rule sneaks in (optional)

For a neural network the model is a stack of small steps, one feeding the next. To get the slope of the whole stack you multiply the little slopes of each step together. That multiplying-along-the-chain is the chain rule from calculus, and doing it backwards through the network is what people call backpropagation. You will never do it by hand in practice: libraries like PyTorch compute the gradient for you automatically. Knowing it is "just the slope of a stack of steps" is enough.

Why industry cares

This one idea trains almost everything. Fitting a line, a logistic model, a demand forecaster, a giant language model: under the hood each one is rolling downhill on its own "how wrong" landscape, nudging its dials to do better. Understand rolling downhill and you understand what "the model learns" means everywhere it appears.
