P-values in plain words

We changed something for half our users. The new version looks a little better. Now we must decide: is the difference real, or did it happen by chance? A p-value is the standard way to answer this. This primer gives you one picture and one sentence that matter. There are no proofs.

The problem it solves

A shopping app changes its checkout screen. Half the users keep the old screen (call it A). The other half get a new one-tap UPI screen (call it B). B looks a little better: slightly more people finished paying. But every measurement has some random variation. Toss a fair coin a hundred times, and then do it again. You will not get exactly fifty heads both times. So the real question is not "did B do better in our sample?" (it did, a little). The real question is: could a gap this big appear even if the two screens were really the same?

The one picture: the "change did nothing" world

Imagine a world where the new screen changed nothing. In this world, A and B are really the same. Even here, the two groups will not get exactly equal results. A gap appears by chance. Sometimes A is ahead, sometimes B is ahead. Now think of every gap that chance could give in this world, and how often each one appears. Small gaps appear often. Big gaps appear rarely. If you draw them, you get a heap centred on zero. Most gaps are small, and fewer and fewer appear as you move away from the middle.

Now place your real result on this picture. If your gap falls in the thick middle of the heap, chance gives gaps like this all the time. So it does not prove anything. If it falls far out in the thin edge (the tail), chance would almost never give a gap that big. So the result is surprising, and that is real evidence that the change did something.

The p-value, in one sentence

The p-value answers one question:

If the change really did nothing, how often would chance alone give a result at least this extreme?

A small p-value means your result sits far out in the tail. It would be surprising if the change did nothing, so you have evidence that it did something. A large p-value means your result sits in the middle. Chance gives gaps like this often, so you have no real evidence.

What it is NOT

Many people get this part wrong, so remember it well. The p-value is not the chance that the change did nothing. We calculated it by assuming the change did nothing. So it cannot also be the chance that this assumption is true.

A p-value of 0.03 does not mean "there is a 3% chance the new screen is useless." It means "if the new screen were useless, a result this extreme would appear about 3 times in 100, by chance alone." Say this sentence aloud a few times until it feels natural. Mixing up these two meanings causes more bad decisions than any other mistake in applied statistics.

The 0.05 line

By custom, we call a result "statistically significant" when the p-value is below 0.05, that is, one in twenty. There is nothing special about 0.05. It is only a custom. In high-risk work, people use a stricter line such as 0.01. The one rule that matters: choose your line before you look at the data, never after.

A more useful partner: the likely range (confidence interval)

A p-value gives only a yes or no. It does not tell you how big the effect is. But size is usually what a business cares about. A likely range (confidence interval) gives the size and the uncertainty together. For example: "the new screen increases completed payments by about 1.4 percentage points, and we are fairly sure the true increase is between 0.3 and 2.5." This is the right thing to report to a manager. It is much more useful than "p = 0.02".

One trap to know now

A p-value means what it claims only if you fixed the question before the experiment. Choose one main metric and your sample size in advance. Suppose instead you measure twenty things and report only the one that came out "significant". Then you gave chance twenty separate tries to fool you. About one of them will look significant even when the change did nothing.

Why industry cares

Product teams, banks, fintech companies and delivery apps run experiments like this all the time. A wrong reading costs real money. The p-value is a careful way to separate a real improvement from random variation. Then you launch the changes that help, and drop the ones that only looked good for one week.

Where you'll use this: Session 25 (A/B testing and hypothesis tests). The same question, "how surprising is this if the change did nothing?", is behind every hypothesis test you will meet. It does not matter whether the test compares two averages, two conversion rates, or three designs at once.
