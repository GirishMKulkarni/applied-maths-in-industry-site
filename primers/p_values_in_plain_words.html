P-values in plain words

We changed something for half our users, the new version looks a little better, and now we have to decide: is that real, or is it just luck? A p-value is the honest way to answer. This primer gives you the picture and the one sentence that matters. No proofs.

The problem it solves

A shopping app changes its checkout screen. Half the users keep the old screen (call it A), half get a redesigned one-tap UPI screen (call it B). B looks a bit better: slightly more people finished paying. But every measurement wobbles. Flip a fair coin a hundred times, twice, and you will not get exactly fifty heads both times. So the honest question is not "did B do better in our sample?" (it did, a little). It is: could a gap this size have turned up even if the two screens were truly the same?

The one picture: the "it made no difference" world

Imagine a pretend world where the new screen changed nothing at all, where A and B are secretly identical. Even in that world the two groups will not tie exactly. Luck alone leaves a gap, sometimes A ahead, sometimes B. Now picture every gap that pure luck could produce in that world and how often each one shows up. Small gaps are common, big gaps are rare. Drawn out, they make a pile centred on zero: mostly small wobbles, thinning out as you move away from the middle.

Now drop your actual result onto that picture. If your gap lands in the fat middle of the pile, it is the kind of thing luck produces all the time, so it is not convincing. If it lands far out in the thin tail, luck would almost never produce something that big, so it is surprising, and that is real evidence the change did something.

The p-value, in one sentence

The p-value is the answer to one question:

If the change really did nothing, how often would luck alone produce a result at least this extreme?

A small p-value means your result sits far out in the tail: it would be surprising if nothing had happened, so you have evidence that something did. A large p-value means your result sits in the fat middle: unremarkable, the sort of gap luck hands you routinely, so you have no real evidence.

What it is NOT

This is the part everyone gets wrong, so hold onto it. The p-value is not the probability that the change did nothing. We worked it out by assuming the change did nothing, so it cannot also be the chance that the assumption is true. A p-value of 0.03 does not mean "there is a 3% chance the new screen is useless." It means "if the new screen were useless, a result this extreme would show up about 3 times in 100 by luck alone." Say that sentence out loud until it feels natural. Confusing the two causes more bad decisions than anything else in applied statistics.

The 0.05 line

By custom we call a result "statistically significant" when the p-value falls below 0.05, a one-in-twenty line. There is nothing sacred about 0.05. It is a convention, chosen by habit, and in high-stakes work people use a stricter line like 0.01. The one rule that matters: pick your line before you look at the data, never after.

Its friendlier companion: the confidence interval

A p-value only gives a yes or no. It hides how big the effect is, and size is usually what a business actually cares about. A confidence interval keeps the size and the uncertainty together: "the new screen lifts completed payments by about 1.4 percentage points, and we are fairly sure the true lift is somewhere between 0.3 and 2.5." That is the honest thing to report to a manager, far more useful than "p = 0.02".

One trap worth naming now

A p-value only means what it claims if you decided the question before the experiment. Pick one main metric and your sample size in advance. If instead you measure twenty things and proudly report the one that came out "significant", you have handed luck twenty separate chances to fool you, and roughly one of them will look significant even when nothing is going on.

Why industry cares

Every product team, bank, fintech and delivery app runs experiments like this constantly, and a wrong read costs real money. The p-value is simply the disciplined way to tell a genuine improvement from a lucky wobble, so you ship the changes that help and drop the ones that only looked good for a week.
