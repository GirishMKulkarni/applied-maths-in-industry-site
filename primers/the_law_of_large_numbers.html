The law of large numbers (and why we roll the dice)

This is the one idea that makes simulation work. Read it before Session 23 and the whole session will feel like common sense. No proofs here, just the picture.

A number decided by chance

Some numbers you know for certain. The number of days in a week is 7. Other numbers are decided by chance, and you only learn them when they happen: the roll of a dice, tomorrow's move in the Nifty, how long the next customer waits at an SBI counter. A number like that, one you cannot know in advance but that follows some pattern of "how often each value shows up", is called a random variable.

You cannot predict a single roll. But you can say a lot about many rolls. That gap, between one unpredictable outcome and the very predictable behaviour of a large batch of them, is where today's tool lives.

Roll it once: useless. Roll it a thousand times: reliable.

Throw one dice and ask "what is the average roll?" One throw tells you almost nothing. It might be a 2. Throw it ten times and average: you get something in the rough neighbourhood of 3.5, but it still jumps around. Throw it a thousand times and average: you land very close to 3.5, every time, no matter who does it.

That is the law of large numbers: the average of many independent random samples settles down close to the true average, and the more samples you take, the closer and steadier it gets. The wobble does not vanish after ten throws; it shrinks smoothly as the pile of throws grows.

Picture a running average being redrawn after every throw. Early on the line lurches about. As the throws pile up, the line calms down and hugs the true value. You will draw exactly this picture in the lab, using darts instead of dice.

The trick: write your answer as an average

Here is the move that turns this from a curiosity into a tool you reach for at work. Anything you can write as "the average of a random outcome", you can estimate by sampling that outcome many times and averaging. When the maths is too hard to solve on paper, you stop solving and start rolling.

  • The number pi is hard to pin down with a formula you can do by hand. But it can be written as an average: throw random darts at a square with a circle inside, and four times the fraction landing inside the circle averages out to pi.
  • The area under an awkward curve (an integral) is an average too: scatter random points and average how many fall under the curve.
  • A bank's average customer wait has no clean formula once the queue gets realistic. So you simulate a day of arrivals many times and average the waits.

This recipe, "write the quantity as an average, sample it a lot, and average", is called Monte Carlo, after the casino. It is one of the most used tools in industry precisely because it needs no clever mathematics, only patience and a computer that can roll the dice fast.

The catch: accuracy is expensive

Nothing is free. The law of large numbers promises the average settles down, but it settles slowly. The typical error shrinks like 1 / sqrt(N), where N is how many samples you took. In plain words: to make your answer ten times more accurate, you need about a hundred times as many samples. To get another decimal place, a hundred times again.

This is why a Monte Carlo estimate of pi is a lovely teaching demo but a terrible way to actually compute pi. It is also why the real skill is not "sample more" but "sample smarter": there are tricks (one is called importance sampling) that spend your samples where they matter most and get a good answer for far less effort. You will meet the idea, lightly, in the lab.

Why industry cares

Whenever a system has too much randomness for a formula, this is the tool. Insurers simulate a year of claims. Banks simulate thousands of market days to size their risk. Delhivery simulates a sorting hub to see where parcels pile up. Flipkart simulates a packing floor to decide how many stations to open on sale day. In every case the logic is the same one you are about to learn: you cannot predict one outcome, but you can roll the dice thousands of times and read off what usually happens.

Where you'll use this: Session 23 (Monte Carlo and queue simulation) and Session 24 (the inventory project, which is built on top of today's simulation skills). The same "average of many samples" idea underlies the confidence intervals you report whenever a result comes from randomness.
