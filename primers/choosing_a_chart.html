Choosing a chart

This is the picture behind the visualization half of Session 5. Read it once and charts stop being a menu of shapes to memorise. No proofs, just the idea.

The problem it solves

You have a table with many columns and many rows. You have found something in it, and now you have to show it to someone who will not read your code or your table. A chart is how you do that. The catch is that a page has only two directions, across and up, while your data has many. So a chart can never show everything at once. It shows a slice. The whole skill is choosing the slice that answers your question and makes the pattern obvious.

A chart is a projection

Think of each column as a separate direction your data can vary in. Sales, month, city, ad spend, number of orders. That is five directions already, and a flat page has only two. Drawing a chart means picking which directions to keep, putting one across the bottom and one up the side, and letting the rest fall away.

That act of squashing many directions down to two is called a projection. It is the same idea as the shadow a 3D object throws on a wall: the shadow is a flat, two-dimensional version of the thing. A good shadow shows the shape clearly. A bad angle turns a person into an unreadable blob. Choosing your chart is choosing the angle of the shadow.

Start from the question, not the chart

The most common beginner mistake is to start from "I want a pie chart" and then go looking for data to put in it. Do the opposite. Start from the question, and the question tells you the chart. Here is the whole cheat sheet.

  • Comparing a value across a few categories? Use a bar chart. One bar per category. Example: revenue by city this month.
  • Watching something change over time? Use a line chart. Time goes across the bottom. Example: monthly revenue through the year.
  • Looking at the shape of one column of numbers? Use a histogram. It sorts the values into bins and shows how many land in each. Example: how delivery times are spread, in minutes. This shape is called the distribution.
  • Asking whether two numbers move together? Use a scatter plot. One dot per record, placed by its two numbers. Example: ad spend against revenue, one dot per month. If the dots drift up to the right, more spend tends to go with more revenue.

Four questions, four charts. That covers most of what you will ever need. Bar and line are the honest workhorses; reach for them first.

Keep it honest

A chart is an argument, so it can be a dishonest one. The commonest trick, usually by accident, is a bar chart whose bottom axis does not start at zero. Say four cities sold 52, 54, 53 and 56 lakh. Start the axis at 50 and the tallest bar looks several times bigger than the shortest, when the real gap is tiny. Start the axis at zero and the bars look almost equal, which is the truth. For bar charts, always start the height axis at zero.

Two more, in the same spirit. Do not use a rainbow of colours for something that has a natural order, and do not add 3D, drop shadows or clip-art. Every mark of ink on a chart should carry information. If it does not, remove it. Less decoration, more truth.

Say the one thing

Before you draw anything, decide the single sentence you want the reader to leave with. "Pune drives nearly half our festive sales." Then pick the chart that makes that sentence obvious, strip everything that does not support it, and title the chart with the takeaway rather than the column names. A figure that makes the reader hunt for the point has failed.

Why industry cares

Nobody funds a project, changes a price or ships a feature because of a table. They do it because a chart made a pattern impossible to miss. An analyst who can turn a query into one clear, honest figure is worth a great deal, in a bank, at Flipkart, on an NSE desk or in a government office. The maths of the model matters, but the chart is what gets it believed.
