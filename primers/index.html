Primers — ten-minute catch-up notes

Each primer explains one idea a session leans on, using pictures and plain words and no proofs. If a session mentions something you have not met, open the matching primer first. Ten minutes is usually enough.

You do not need to read these in order or read all of them. Open the one you need, when you need it. Each session's README lists the primers worth reading for that day under "You don't need to already know".

For the plain meaning of any single word, see the glossary.

Getting started

  • Python in 15 minutes — the whole of the code you need to begin: variables, lists, loops, functions, and running a notebook.

Numbers and the maths ideas

  • Vectors and matrices — a vector as a list of numbers, a matrix as a table of numbers (Session 2).
  • What is a model — the five parts of any model: variables, parameters, constraints, objective, assumptions (Session 6).
  • Lines and least squares — what "fitting a line" means and why it is the idea behind linear regression (Session 7).
  • Slopes and gradients — "which way is downhill", the idea behind how models learn (Sessions 7 and 13).
  • What is a distribution and its companion Distributions and the bell curve — histograms, centre and spread, the bell curve, fat tails and the lognormal (Sessions 14, 17, 18). Read whichever the session points you to.
  • Quantiles and tails — the far end of a distribution and how risk numbers are read off it (Session 17).
  • The law of large numbers — "roll the dice many times and average", the idea behind simulation (Session 23).

Working with data

  • Choosing a chart — which chart answers the question, and keeping charts honest (Session 5).
  • Graphs and DAGs — dots joined by arrows, and why a Git history is one (Session 4).
  • Representation and features — why how you write data down changes what a model can learn (Session 12).

Machine learning

  • Overfitting and regularization — memorising versus understanding, and how to keep a model honest (Session 8).
  • Reading a confusion matrix — the four counts, precision versus recall, and the threshold dial (Session 9).
  • Impurity and information gain — how a decision tree decides which question to ask (Session 10).
  • Distance between points — measuring how similar two rows are, and why you standardise first (Session 11).

Statistics

  • p-values in plain words and its companion Hypothesis testing and p-values — what a p-value is and is not, and the multiple-testing trap (Sessions 25 and 19).

Finance

  • Returns and log-returns — why we study day-to-day returns instead of prices (Session 14).
  • Time series and forecasting — trend, season and noise, and AR/MA as weighted averages over time (Session 15).
  • Diversification and covariance — why mixing assets that don't move together lowers risk (Session 16).

Optimization and operations research

  • What is optimization and its companion Objectives and constraints — choices, a goal, and limits; and the jump from smooth to whole-number decisions (Sessions 20 and 21).
  • Convex sets and functions — why a bowl-shaped problem always has one best answer (Session 22).
  • Markov chains — a system that hops between states and where it settles in the long run (Session 24).
