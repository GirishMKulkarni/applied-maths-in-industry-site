Vectors and matrices, as lists and tables of numbers

This is the picture behind a lot of the course. It sounds like heavy mathematics, but the whole idea fits on a napkin: a vector is a list of numbers, and a matrix is a table of numbers. That is it. No proofs here, just the picture.

A vector is a list of numbers

Say a student scored 72, 85, 66 and 90 in four subjects. Write those four numbers in a row and you have a vector:

marks = [72, 85, 66, 90]

A vector is nothing more mysterious than that: a fixed-length list of numbers, kept in order. The order matters, because the first slot always means the first subject. A flat described by its size, its age and its distance from a metro station is also a vector: [850, 6, 1.2]. Any single thing you measure a few numbers about becomes a vector.

People sometimes write a vector standing up (a column) instead of lying down (a row). It is the same list either way.

A matrix is a table of numbers

Now line up many students, one per row, with the same four subjects as columns:

        maths  english  science  history
Asha       72       85       66       90
Ravi       80       78       74       61
Meera      65       90       88       84

Strip the labels and you are left with a grid of numbers with rows and columns. That grid is a matrix. One row is one student (a vector). One column is one subject across all students (also a vector). A matrix is just a stack of vectors, looked at as a single table.

This is exactly how real data arrives. A property website holds a table with one row per flat and columns for size, age, bedrooms and price. A bank holds one row per customer. A matrix is the natural shape of "many cases, each measured the same way".

Shape: how big the table is

The one thing you always want to know about a list or a table is how big it is. We call that its shape.

  • A list of 4 numbers has shape (4,).
  • A table with 3 rows and 4 columns has shape (3, 4): rows first, then columns.

When code misbehaves, nine times out of ten the shapes did not line up the way you thought. Printing the shape is the first thing to check, always.

Doing maths on a whole list at once

Here is why we bother. Suppose every flat's price should rise by 8 percent. You do not walk down the list multiplying each price by 1.08 one at a time. You multiply the whole list by 1.08 in a single stroke, and every number updates together:

new_prices = old_prices * 1.08

Add two lists and the numbers add up slot by slot. Multiply a list by a single number and every entry scales. This "do it to the whole list at once" style is the entire reason the numpy library exists, and it is both shorter to write and far faster to run than a loop. Session 2 is where you learn to do this.

Solving a small system

One more everyday use. Suppose two samosas and one chai cost ₹50, and one samosa with three chais also costs ₹50. What is each item worth? Those are two facts about two unknown prices, and you can write them as a small table (the amounts) and a short list (the totals). Handing both to the computer gives you back the two prices at once. That "here are the knowns, find the unknowns" step is called solving a system, and it is a single line of code in Session 2.

What about eigenvectors and the SVD?

You may hear the words eigenvector and singular value decomposition (SVD). These are deeper facts about what a table of numbers does when you multiply things by it, and they power tools like data compression and recommendation engines. They are genuinely useful, but you do not need them to do the main work of Session 2. When you are ready, they are waiting in the optional "for the mathematically curious" boxes. Skipping them costs you nothing today.

Why industry cares

Almost every dataset in the world is a table of numbers: customers as rows, measurements as columns. Every spreadsheet, every export from a database, every batch of images fed to a model is a matrix underneath. Learning to hold that table as one object and do arithmetic on all of it at once is the foundation the rest of the course stands on.

Where you'll use this: Session 2 (NumPy and arrays), and then everywhere. Session 3 (tables in pandas), the machine-learning sessions from Session 7 onward, and the finance and optimisation modules all sit on top of lists and tables of numbers.
