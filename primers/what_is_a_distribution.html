What is a distribution?

A distribution is just a picture of "how often each kind of value happens". It is one of the most useful pictures in all of data work, and you do not need any statistics to read it. This primer gives you the picture and two words that go with it. No proofs.

Start with a histogram

Suppose you write down the daily move of the Nifty for a thousand days: −1.2%, +0.4%, −0.1%, +2.1%, and so on. Now sort those thousand numbers into bins ("how many days moved between 0% and 0.5%?", "between 0.5% and 1%?", ...) and draw a bar for each bin, taller where more days landed. That bar chart is a histogram, and the shape it makes is the distribution of the daily moves.

Reading it is intuitive. Tall bars in the middle mean most days were small moves. Short bars far out on the sides mean big moves were rare. The histogram answers, at a glance, "what does a typical day look like, and how wild can a day get?"

Two words: the centre and the spread

Almost every distribution is summarised by two numbers.

  • The centre (the average, or mean) is where the pile sits. For daily stock returns it is very close to zero.
  • The spread (the standard deviation) is how wide the pile is. A narrow pile means calm, predictable days. A wide pile means wild, uncertain days. In finance this spread has its own name: volatility. High volatility just means a wide, risky distribution of daily moves.

If you know the centre and the spread, you know most of what the picture is telling you.

The bell curve, and why markets break it

The most famous distribution is the normal distribution, the smooth, symmetric "bell curve". Lots of everyday things (heights, exam marks, measurement errors) follow it closely, so it is the default many people reach for.

Here is the twist that matters for finance. Real market returns are not a neat bell curve. They have fat tails: extreme days, crashes and huge rallies, happen far more often than a bell curve would ever predict. If markets were a clean bell curve, a once-in-a-century crash would be genuinely once-in-a-century. In reality they show up every few years. You will draw this yourself in the lab: the real histogram pokes out well past the bell curve on both sides.

This is not a technicality. Assuming a bell curve when the tails are fat is exactly how firms underestimate risk and get badly hurt. Seeing the fat tails with your own eyes is one of the main points of the session.

Why industry cares

A distribution is how anyone honest talks about uncertainty. What is a typical outcome? How bad is a bad day? How likely is the worst case? Insurers, banks, inventory planners and risk teams all live in this picture. The moment you can look at a histogram and say "centre here, spread this wide, and watch those fat tails", you are reading data the way a professional does.
