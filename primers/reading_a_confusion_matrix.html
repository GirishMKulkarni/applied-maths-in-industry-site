Reading a confusion matrix

This is the whole idea behind judging a yes-or-no model in Week 5's classification session. Read it before that class and the evaluation half will feel easy. No proofs here, just the picture and a bit of counting.

The problem it solves

You built a model that answers a yes-or-no question. Is this UPI transaction fraud? Will this borrower default? Will this customer churn? Now you have to say how good it is. The tempting answer is accuracy: out of all its guesses, what fraction did it get right? That single number hides too much, and when the "yes" cases are rare it can be downright misleading. The confusion matrix is the honest replacement. It does not throw away information; it lays every kind of mistake out where you can see it.

The four kinds of right and wrong

Every guess the model makes lands in one of four buckets. There are two ways to be right and two ways to be wrong:

                        model said NO        model said YES
   really NO   (legit)   correctly cleared     false alarm
   really YES  (fraud)   missed it             caught it

Read across the rows by the truth, and down the columns by the guess:

  • Caught it — it really was fraud, and the model flagged it. A win. (The textbooks call this a true positive.)
  • Correctly cleared — it really was legit, and the model let it through. Also a win. (True negative.)
  • False alarm — it was legit, but the model shouted fraud. Annoying, wastes an investigator's time, irritates a good customer. (False positive.)
  • Missed it — it really was fraud, but the model waved it through. Usually the expensive one. (False negative.)

That table of four counts is the confusion matrix. Every other score in the session is built from these four numbers.

A worked count

Say we test the model on 1,000 transactions. 950 are legit, 50 are fraud (rare, as fraud always is). The model's guesses land like this:

                        model said legit     model said fraud
   really legit             920                     30
   really fraud              20                     30

Reading it off: it caught 30 of the 50 frauds, missed 20 of them, raised 30 false alarms on legit transactions, and correctly cleared 920. Now we can ask the two questions that actually matter.

Precision and recall — two different questions

These two words trip people up because they sound similar. They are asking opposite things.

Precision looks only at the cases the model flagged and asks: of these, how many were real?

precision = caught / (caught + false alarms) = 30 / (30 + 30) = 0.50

Half of the model's fraud alarms were genuine. High precision means "when it shouts, believe it."

Recall looks only at the cases that really were fraud and asks: of these, how many did we catch?

recall = caught / (caught + missed) = 30 / (30 + 20) = 0.60

It caught 60% of the real fraud. High recall means "not much slips past."

A handy way to remember: precision is "of the ones we flagged, how many were real?"; recall is "of the real ones, how many did we catch?"

Why plain accuracy can lie

Look at the same example. A lazy model that simply says "legit" to everything gets 950 of the 1,000 right, which is 95% accuracy. It sounds excellent. But its recall is zero: it caught none of the 50 frauds. That is the accuracy paradox, and it is exactly why we count the four buckets instead of trusting one headline number. When the interesting answer is rare, watch recall and precision, not accuracy.

The threshold is a dial

The model does not really output a hard yes or no. It outputs a probability, like 0.62. We turn that into a decision by picking a cut-off, the threshold: flag it if the probability is at least, say, 0.5. That cut-off is a dial you can turn. Lower it and you catch more fraud (recall goes up) but raise more false alarms (precision goes down). Raise it and the reverse happens. Where you set the dial is a business decision: how bad is a missed fraud compared with a false alarm? The maths does not answer that; the cost does.

Why industry cares

Almost every high-stakes model is a rare-event detector: fraud, loan default, disease, machine failure. On all of them accuracy flatters and hides, and the confusion matrix tells the truth. Being able to read one, and to say whether a model needs more precision or more recall for the job at hand, is one of the most practical skills in the whole course, and a question interviewers love to ask.
