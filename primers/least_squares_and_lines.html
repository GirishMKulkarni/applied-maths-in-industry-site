Lines and least squares

This is the whole idea behind Week 4's linear regression. Read it before that session and the class will feel easy. No proofs here, just the picture.

The problem it solves

You have some data that seems to follow a trend. Bigger flats cost more. More study hours means higher marks. Hotter days sell more cold drinks. You want to draw the single straight line that best captures the trend, so that you can make a prediction for a new case you have not seen.

A straight line, in words

A straight line is a rule that turns one number into another:

predicted price = slope × size + starting value
  • The starting value (mathematicians call it the intercept) is where the line begins, the price when size is zero.
  • The slope is how steep the line is, how much the price goes up for each extra unit of size.

If slope is 2.3 and the starting value is 4, then a flat of size 10 is predicted at 2.3 × 10 + 4 = 27.

"Best" means smallest total miss

Your data points will not sit perfectly on any line. Real data is noisy. So for a given line, each point is a little above or below it. That gap, the difference between the real value and the line's prediction, is called the residual, the "miss".

Some misses are above the line, some below. To stop them cancelling out, we square each miss (a square is always positive) and add them all up. That total is the squared error.

The best line is the one that makes this total squared error as small as possible. That is the entire meaning of the phrase least squares: the line with the least total of squared misses.

Picture it as choosing, out of all possible lines, the one that sits most snugly through the middle of the cloud of points.

You do not have to try lines one by one

Here is the pleasant surprise. You do not have to test thousands of lines to find the snuggest one. There is a direct formula that hands you the best slope and starting value in a single step. In the notebook we let the computer apply that formula, and we also check the answer against scikit-learn, the standard library, which does the same thing.

If you have met matrices, the formula is called the normal equation, and the "Go deeper" box in the session shows it. If you have not met matrices, you do not need it. The idea, "find the line with the smallest total squared miss", is the part that matters, and the computer does the rest.

Why industry cares

This is the simplest useful prediction machine, and it is everywhere. Predicting demand, prices, risk, yield, sales. It is also the foundation the rest of machine learning is built on. Almost everything later is a cleverer version of "fit a rule to data by making the total miss small".

Where you'll use this: Session 7 (linear regression) and Session 8 (regularization). The same "smallest total miss" idea returns in Session 16 (portfolios) and Session 22 (convex optimization).
