Distributions and the bell curve

This is the picture behind the finance and risk sessions, and behind today's options session especially. It sounds like heavy statistics, but the whole idea is just a shape and an area. No proofs here, only the picture.

A distribution is "how often each value shows up"

Measure the daily percentage change of the Nifty for a few years and sort the days into little buckets: days it fell about 2 percent, days it barely moved, days it rose about 1 percent, and so on. Count how many days land in each bucket and draw a bar for each. That shape, "how often each value shows up", is a distribution.

Most days the market barely moves, so the tall bars sit in the middle near zero. Big up days and big down days are rarer, so the bars get shorter as you move out to the sides. Almost anything you measure across many cases looks like this: heights of people, marks in an exam, delivery times for Zomato orders. A big lump in the middle, thinning out on both sides.

The bell curve (the normal distribution)

Smooth that lumpy histogram into a clean curve and you very often get the bell curve, whose proper name is the normal distribution. It is the single most common shape in all of statistics. Two numbers describe it completely:

  • the mean — where the peak sits, the typical value in the middle,
  • the spread — how wide the bell is, how far values usually stray from the middle. In finance the spread has a special name: volatility, written with the Greek letter sigma (σ). A calm bluechip stock has a narrow bell; a jumpy smallcap has a wide one.

Same mean, bigger spread, means a wider, flatter bell and more uncertainty about what will happen.

The area under the curve is a probability

Here is the one move you need for options. The bell curve is drawn so that the total area under it is exactly 1, meaning "something definitely happens". So the area under any slice of it is the probability of landing in that slice.

The area to the left of a point answers "what is the chance the value comes out below here?" That running area has a name, the CDF (cumulative distribution function), written N(·) for the bell curve. A few values worth carrying:

  • N(0) = 0.5 — half the area is left of the middle, so there is a 50 percent chance of being below the mean.
  • N(1.96) ≈ 0.975 — about 97.5 percent of the bell sits to the left of 1.96 spreads above the mean.

In code you never work this area out by hand. The scipy library hands it to you: norm.cdf(x) is N(x), the area to the left of x. That single function is the N(·) that appears all over the Black-Scholes option formula.

There is also norm.pdf(x), the height of the bell at x (lower-case n(·)), as opposed to the area. The height turns up in the option sensitivities called the Greeks.

Lognormal: why stock prices lean to one side

A stock price cannot be neatly bell-shaped, for one blunt reason: a price can never go below zero. It can fall to zero at worst, but it can multiply many times over on the upside. So its distribution is lopsided, squashed against zero on the left and with a long tail stretching to the right.

The fix is simple and it is the shape today's model uses. The daily returns (the percentage changes, strictly the log of them) are taken to be normal, a tidy bell. The price itself is then exp of that normal quantity, which is called a lognormal distribution. "Lognormal" just means "its logarithm is normal". The picture is a bell that has been bent to keep everything positive and to lean right.

You do not need to memorise the formula. Carry the picture: possible future stock prices spread out in a right-leaning lognormal shape, never going negative.

Expectation: the probability-weighted average

Last idea. When the future can turn out many ways, the fair value of a payoff is its expectation, which is just a weighted average: list every possible outcome, multiply each payoff by how likely it is, and add them up. Common outcomes count for a lot, rare ones count for a little.

You can compute an expectation two ways. If you know the distribution's formula you can work the average out exactly with pen and paper, which is what the Black-Scholes formula does. Or you can simulate thousands of possible futures, take the payoff in each, and average them, which is what a Monte Carlo simulation does. Today's options session does both and watches them agree.

Why industry cares

Every risk number a bank reports, every option a trader prices, every "how bad could tomorrow be" question rests on a distribution of outcomes and areas under it. The bell curve and its lognormal cousin are the default working shapes for prices and returns, and the CDF N(·) is the workhorse behind the most quoted formula in finance.
