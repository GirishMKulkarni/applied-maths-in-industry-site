Hypothesis testing and p-values

This is the idea behind "is this result real, or did I just get lucky?" It is the quiet backbone of Session 19 (algorithmic trading), where the whole danger is mistaking luck for skill. Read it once and the class will feel easy. No proofs here, just the picture.

The problem it solves

You ran an experiment and got a nice-looking result. A new trading rule turned ₹1 into ₹1.40 on past data. A new medicine seemed to help. A coin came up heads seven times out of ten. In every case one nagging question remains: could this have happened by chance alone, even if there is nothing special going on?

Hypothesis testing is a careful way to answer that one question.

The setup: a "nothing is going on" story

You start by writing down the boring explanation, the one that says your result is just luck. This boring explanation is called the null hypothesis. It is deliberately the dull, skeptical story:

  • "This trading rule has no real edge; it made money by chance."
  • "This medicine does nothing; the patients who improved would have anyway."
  • "This coin is fair; seven heads was just a lucky run."

You are the prosecutor and the null hypothesis is the defendant. You do not get to declare it guilty because you feel like it. You have to show the evidence would be surprising if the defendant were innocent.

The p-value: how surprising is my result, if nothing is going on?

Now the key number. The p-value answers exactly this:

If the null hypothesis were true (nothing going on), how often would pure luck hand me a result at least this good?

A small p-value means "luck almost never produces something this good, so I doubt the boring explanation." A large p-value means "luck produces this all the time, so I have no case."

A common line in the sand is 0.05, meaning a 1-in-20 fluke. If the p-value is below 0.05, people say the result is statistically significant and reject the null. That line is a convention, not a law of nature.

Picture a bell-shaped pile of all the results luck could give you. Your actual result is a marker somewhere on that pile. If the marker sits deep in the thin tail, far from the middle, the p-value is small: luck rarely goes that far. If the marker sits near the fat middle, the p-value is large: luck lands there often.

The trap that ruins trading: many tries

Here is the part that matters most for Session 19, and it is where honest people still fool themselves.

A 1-in-20 fluke sounds rare. But what if you run 20 experiments? Then, on average, you expect one of them to clear the "significant" bar by pure luck, even if every single one is worthless. Run 100 worthless trading rules and about 5 will look "significant" at the 0.05 level for no reason at all.

So if you quietly try many strategies and proudly report only the best one, your p-value is a lie. The right p-value has to account for how many things you tried. Keeping the best of many tries is called data-snooping or p-hacking, and it is the single easiest way to fake a winning backtest. Session 19's second notebook shows this happening with 200 pure-noise strategies: the luckiest one looks brilliant, and it is nothing.

The honest rules that follow from this:

  • Decide your one strategy first, then test it once.
  • If you must compare many, demand a much stronger result before you believe any of them.
  • Be suspicious of a single gorgeous result, not excited by it.

What a p-value is not

Two honest cautions, because these trip up everyone at first:

  • A small p-value does not tell you the result is important or large. It only says luck is an unlikely explanation. A tiny, useless edge can be "significant" if you have enough data.
  • A p-value is not "the probability the null is true." It is the probability of your evidence assuming the null is true. Those are different sentences, and the difference is the whole subject.

Why industry cares

This is the grammar of evidence. Medicine uses it to approve drugs. Factories use it to decide whether a new process really cut defects. A/B tests at Flipkart or Zomato use it to decide whether a new checkout screen truly lifted orders or just had a good week. And in quant finance it is the difference between a real edge and an expensive illusion. Learning to ask "how many things did I try before I got excited?" will save you money and embarrassment for the rest of your career.

Where you'll use this: Session 19 (algorithmic trading and backtesting), where a trading edge is a hypothesis and a backtest is the test. The same skeptical habit shows up wherever you compare options and must ask whether the winner is real.
