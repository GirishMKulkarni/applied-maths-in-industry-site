Quantiles and tails

This is the picture behind Week 9's risk session. It sounds like heavy statistics, but the whole idea is something you already do when you look at exam marks. No proofs here, just the picture.

The problem it solves

You have a big pile of numbers: the daily gains and losses of a portfolio over the last few years, say. You do not want to stare at all of them. You want to summarise them with one honest number that answers a sharp question: "how bad is a bad day?" The average will not do, because the average hides the disasters. You need a way to point at the far end of the pile, not the middle.

A quantile is just a mark on the pile

Line up all your numbers from smallest to largest. A quantile is a mark that splits that line at a chosen fraction.

You have met this already. If a student is in the 90th percentile of a class, 90% of the class scored below them and 10% scored above. A percentile is a quantile written as a percent. The median is simply the 50% quantile: half the numbers are below it, half above.

So the 95% quantile of a pile of numbers is the value with 95% of the numbers below it and only 5% above. Nothing more mysterious than that. In code it is one line, np.percentile(numbers, 95), and the computer does the sorting and counting for you.

The tail is the far end

Draw the pile as a histogram: tall bars where numbers are common, short bars where they are rare. Daily losses look like a hill: most days sit near zero (small gains and small losses), and the bars get shorter as you move right toward the big losses.

That thin far-right stretch, the rare big losses, is called the tail. It is where the scary outcomes live. The whole job of risk measurement is to describe that tail honestly, because that is the part that can sink a fund.

VaR: a line drawn at the 95% mark

Now the payoff. Draw one vertical line at the 95% quantile of the losses. Only the worst 5% of days fall to its right. That line is the Value at Risk, or VaR.

Read it in plain words: "on 95 days out of 100 we lose less than this; only about one day in twenty is worse." It is a threshold, the edge of the everyday-bad zone. It is not the worst case. Losses can and do go past it. That is the honest small print people forget.

CVaR: how far past the line, on average

VaR tells you where the tail begins. It says nothing about how deep it goes. Two piles of numbers can have the very same 95% mark and yet, beyond it, one is calm and the other is catastrophic.

So we ask a second question: among just those worst 5% of days, what is the average loss? That average is the CVaR (also called Expected Shortfall). It always sits further right than VaR, because it averages numbers that are all at least as big as VaR. If VaR is the edge of the cliff, CVaR is how far you actually fall once you step over.

The mature answer in a risk report, and in an interview, is to quote both: VaR for the threshold, CVaR for the depth.

Why the tail is fatter than a bell curve says

You may be tempted to assume the losses follow a neat normal "bell curve". Be careful. Real market returns have fat tails: genuinely extreme days (a crash) happen far more often than a bell curve predicts. A bell curve says a huge drop is almost impossible; the market delivers one every few years. So a risk number built on the bell-curve assumption will quietly understate the danger. This is exactly why the session computes VaR more than one way and compares.

Why industry cares

Every bank, fund and trading desk has to put a rupee figure on "how bad could a bad day be", and regulators (the RBI, following the global Basel rules) require it and make them hold spare capital against it. VaR and CVaR are the language that number is spoken in. Getting the tail right is not academic: understate it and you blow up in a crisis, overstate it and you tie up capital you could have used.
