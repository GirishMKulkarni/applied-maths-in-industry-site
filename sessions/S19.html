The problem

A friend gives you a tip: "Buy HDFC Bank when its 20-day average climbs above its 50-day average. Sell when it drops below. Easy money." It sounds sensible. Before you put real rupees behind it, you want to know one thing: does this rule actually make money?

The only way to check is to replay history. You take seven years of daily prices, 2019 to 2025, pretend you followed the rule every day, and add up what you would have earned. This is called a backtest. It is the single most useful, and the single most dangerous, tool in quant finance. Useful, because it is the only honest way to check an idea before risking money. Dangerous, because it is astonishingly easy to run a backtest that says "you would be rich" when the truth is you would not.

The one idea

A backtest replays history and asks: if I had followed this rule, day by day, without ever peeking at the future, and after paying the fees I really would have paid, would I have beaten simply buying and holding the stock?

Done honestly on HDFC Bank, the answer is no. ₹1 following the rule from January 2019 became about ₹1.05; ₹1 simply held became about ₹1.97. Most backtests you see online look far better than this, and today is about why. They peek at the future, they try many rules and keep the lucky one, they test only on stocks that survived, and they forget that every trade costs money.

We finish with a more advanced rule, a pairs trade on HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank, fitted on 2024 and traded on 2025, a year it never saw. The session's one sentence: a trading strategy is easy; an honest backtest is hard.

What you'll build today

  1. The 20/50 moving-average rule on HDFC Bank: a moving average by hand, the rule as one number a day, five days of the backtest by hand, then all seven years in code, drawn against buy and hold with its return, Sharpe ratio and worst fall.

For the mathematically curious (optional)

Everything above is enough to do the lab. This box is the extra depth for those who want it, and none of it is needed to finish.

Today's mathematical object has two halves. The first is hypothesis testing. Treat a strategy as a claim and its backtest as a test, with the null hypothesis "this rule has no real edge." A gorgeous backtest is a small p-value, a result that would be surprising if the null were true. The catch is multiple testing: run a hundred tests and, by chance, about five clear the 5% bar with no edge at all. Serious quants therefore deflate their Sharpe ratio to penalise the number of trials they ran; Bailey and López de Prado's papers in the reading list make this precise.

The second half is cointegration. A single stock price is usually a non-stationary random walk: it drifts with no fixed mean, so you cannot say where it will be next year. Two such walks can share a common trend so tightly that a particular combination of them, the spread A − β·B, is stationary: it has a stable mean and keeps returning to it. That β is the hedge ratio, and you find it with the same least squares from Session 7. The Engle–Granger test makes this testable: regress A on B, then run an ADF stationarity test on the residual spread, with the null "the spread is not stationary." For HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank the test passes in 2024 (p ≈ 0.02) and fails in 2021 and 2025, which is the practical lesson: cointegration is a property of a period, not of a pair, so it must be re-tested.

New words in this session

  • backtest — replay a trading rule over past prices to see what it would have earned.
  • moving average — the average of the last n closing prices, recomputed each day.
  • equity curve — the running value of ₹1 put into a strategy over time.
  • drawdown — how far the equity curve sits below its previous high; the worst one is the maximum drawdown.
  • look-ahead bias — accidentally using information you could not have had yet, which flatters a backtest.
  • survivorship bias — testing only on stocks that survived to today, leaving out the ones that failed.
  • data-snooping — trying many strategies or settings and keeping the best, so the "winner" is mostly luck.
  • in-sample / out-of-sample — the data a rule was tuned on, and fresh data it never saw.
  • transaction cost — what each trade really costs: brokerage, STT, stamp duty, exchange fees and slippage.
  • pairs trade — betting on the gap between two related stocks rather than on either one.
  • spread and hedge ratio — the gap A − β·B, and the β that balances one share of A.
  • cointegration — two wandering prices whose spread keeps returning to its average, like a dog on a leash.
  • z-score — how many typical swings a value sits from its average.

Full definitions are in the glossary.

The problem: a rule that sounds like easy money

  • A friend says: "Buy HDFC Bank when its 20-day average climbs above its 50-day average. Sell when it drops below. Easy money."
  • It sounds sensible. Before you risk a single rupee, ask one question: does this rule actually make money?
  • The only way to check is to replay the past: pretend you followed the rule every day from 2019 to 2025 and add up what happened.
  • That replay is a backtest. Writing the rule takes one line. Testing it without fooling yourself is today's real lesson.

The one idea, in one picture

  • Put ₹1 in on 1 January 2019.
  • Grey: just buy HDFC Bank and hold it. ₹1 became ₹1.97.
  • Green: follow the rule. ₹1 became ₹1.05.
  • The question is never "did it go up?" It is "did it beat doing nothing clever, after fees, without peeking?"

Systematic trading: rules in, orders out

Speeds range from microseconds (high-frequency trading, not us) to holding for weeks. We live at the slow, statistical end.

Three families of rules, three different bets

A moving average, by hand

  • The average of the last n closing prices. Each day it slides forward by one.
  • HDFC Bank's first closes of 2025: ₹865.2, ₹870.5, ₹848.9, ₹830.1
  • A 3-day average on 3 Jan: (865.2 + 870.5 + 848.9) / 3 = ₹861.5
  • Next day, drop the oldest and add the newest: (870.5 + 848.9 + 830.1) / 3 = ₹849.8
  • 20 days gives a fast, jumpy line; 50 days gives a slow, calm one.

The rule, in one picture

Two boxes, one comparison, once a day. That is the whole strategy.

The rule on real prices

HDFC Bank, 2023–24. Green triangles: fast crosses above slow (buy). Red: fast crosses below (sell).

The rule in code: one number a day

  • The picture's two boxes are the line position = (fast > slow).astype(int).

CODE prices = pd.read_csv("hdfc_icici_2019_2025.csv", index_col=0, parse_dates=True) price = prices["HDFCBANK"] # HDFC Bank, daily close fast = price.rolling(20).mean() # 20-day average slow = price.rolling(50).mean() # 50-day average position = (fast > slow).astype(int) # 1 = own the stock, 0 = cash print("days owning the stock:", position.sum()) print("days in cash :", (position == 0).sum()) print("buy or sell days :", int(position.diff().abs().sum())) OUTPUT ▸ days owning the stock: 1078 days in cash : 652 buy or sell days : 44

A backtest: replay history, one day at a time

  • Walk forward through the past, one day at a time.
  • Each day, use only what you knew by yesterday's close.
  • Earn the stock's return if you held it; pay a fee if you traded.
  • The output is an equity curve: the running value of ₹1.

Replay five days by hand

The whole backtest in code

  • shift(1) moves every signal one day later: the table's 'held today' column.

CODE stock_return = price.pct_change() strategy_return = position.shift(1) * stock_return # hold YESTERDAY's signal rule = (1 + strategy_return).prod() # multiply up every day's growth hold = (1 + stock_return).prod() print("Rs 1 following the rule became:", round(rule, 2)) print("Rs 1 just holding HDFC became :", round(hold, 2)) OUTPUT ▸ Rs 1 following the rule became: 1.05 Rs 1 just holding HDFC became : 1.97

Judge more than the final number

  • Rule: 0.7% a year, Sharpe 0.13, worst fall -46%.
  • Hold: 10.1% a year, Sharpe 0.52, worst fall -41%.
  • Worst fall (drawdown): how far below the last high you sank.
  • A rule you cannot bear to hold through its worst fall is no use.

Trap 1: look-ahead bias, peeking at the future

Left: decide using only the past. Right: secretly using data from after the decision.

Forget one shift(1) and the rule looks better

  • The cheat uses today's close to decide today's trade, and pockets the move that caused the signal.

CODE honest = position.shift(1) * stock_return # decide yesterday, earn today cheat = position * stock_return # decide AND earn today: peeking print("honest:", round((1 + honest).prod(), 2)) print("cheat :", round((1 + cheat).prod(), 2)) OUTPUT ▸ honest: 1.05 cheat : 1.23

Trap 2: survivorship bias, testing only the winners

  • Yes Bank was in the Nifty 50 until March 2020.
  • By then it had fallen 84% from its 2018 high.
  • Backtest on today's Nifty 50 list and Yes Bank is not in your data.
  • Your rule never has to live through its collapse.

Trap 3: try 200 rules and one will look brilliant

  • None of these 200 rules has any edge. The best one is pure luck, and it is the one people show you.

CODE np.random.seed(1) results = [] for i in range(200): r = np.random.normal(0, 0.01, 500) # a rule with NO edge: pure noise results.append((1 + r).prod()) print("average rule:", round(np.mean(results), 2)) print("best rule :", round(max(results), 2)) print("rules that made 20% or more:", sum(x > 1.2 for x in results)) OUTPUT ▸ average rule: 1.03 best rule : 1.75 rules that made 20% or more: 44

The same trap with real knobs

  • Try 19 pairs of averages on 2019–22 and keep the best: 30/100 days.
  • On those years it turned ₹1 into ₹1.40.
  • On 2023–25, years it never saw: ₹0.93. Just holding: ₹1.27.
  • Tune on one stretch of history. Judge on another, once.

Trap 4: every trade costs money

  • Brokerage, STT, stamp duty, exchange fees and slippage. If an edge survives only at zero cost, it does not exist.

CODE trades = position.diff().abs() # 1 on every buy or sell day net_return = strategy_return - 0.001 * trades.shift(1) # pay 0.1% per trade print("trades :", int(trades.sum())) print("before costs:", round((1 + strategy_return).prod(), 3)) print("after costs :", round((1 + net_return).prod(), 3)) OUTPUT ▸ trades : 44 before costs: 1.052 after costs : 1.007

Why quants lose money

  • Good-looking strategy + dishonest backtest
  • It peeked at the future
  • It was tuned until it shone
  • It ignored costs
  • It was tested only on survivors
  • Result: real money bet on a mirage. Disaster.
  • Mediocre strategy + honest backtest
  • Signals shifted, costs charged
  • Settings fixed before the test
  • Judged once, on unseen years
  • You know exactly how little it does
  • Result: small, known, survivable. Much safer.

Honest backtesting: the one slide

  • Tempting shortcuts
  • Decide on today's close and trade at it
  • Use today's index members for all of history
  • Keep tweaking until the curve is gorgeous
  • Assume free, instant trades
  • Believe an edge from a handful of trades
  • Honest practice
  • shift(1): act the day after the signal
  • Point-in-time data that keeps the dead names
  • Fix settings on one period, test once on the next
  • Charge realistic fees and slippage on every trade
  • Demand many trades, and compare with buy and hold

Pairs trading: two stocks that usually move together

  • HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank: same economy, same interest rates, same regulator.
  • Each price alone is hard to predict.
  • But when they drift apart, bet that they come back together.
  • Market crashes hit both, so the bet barely cares.

Build the gap

  • Gap = HDFC − 0.52 × ICICI. The 0.52 comes from fitting a straight line through 2024's prices.

CODE hdfc, icici = prices["HDFCBANK"], prices["ICICIBANK"] fit = prices.loc["2024"] # learn from 2024 only beta = np.polyfit(fit["ICICIBANK"], fit["HDFCBANK"], 1)[0] spread = hdfc - beta * icici # the gap we trade print("hedge ratio:", round(beta, 2), "ICICI shares per HDFC share") print("2024 gap: average", round(spread.loc["2024"].mean()), " typical swing", round(spread.loc["2024"].std())) OUTPUT ▸ hedge ratio: 0.52 ICICI shares per HDFC share 2024 gap: average 182 typical swing 36

Cointegration: a dog on a leash

  • A dog and its owner each wander anywhere.
  • The leash keeps the gap between them steady.
  • Two prices that wander, with a gap that keeps coming back: that pair is cointegrated.
  • In 2024 the HDFC–ICICI gap kept returning to about ₹182.

Only now, test the leash

  • Small p-value (below 0.05): the gap does come back. 2024 passes; 2021 does not. Leashes come and go.

CODE from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import coint for year in ["2021", "2024"]: part = prices.loc[year] p = coint(part["HDFCBANK"], part["ICICIBANK"])[1] print(year, "p-value:", round(p, 3)) # small p: the leash holds OUTPUT ▸ 2021 p-value: 0.389 2024 p-value: 0.024

The trading rule: a z-score and three lines

  • z = (gap − 2024 average) ÷ 2024 swing.
  • z above +2: gap too wide. SELL the gap: sell HDFC, buy ICICI.
  • z below −2: gap too narrow. BUY the gap.
  • z back near 0: EXIT.
