The problem

Stand in an SBI branch at lunchtime. Customers wander in at random moments, some minutes apart, some in a bunch. One teller serves them one at a time, and each person takes a different amount of time. The branch manager wants a straight answer to a simple question: how long will a customer wait, and will the queue get out of hand if the teller slows down even a little?

There is no neat formula waiting to be plugged in here. The randomness is everywhere: in when people arrive, in how long each one takes. The same problem shows up in a hospital OPD, at a Flipkart packing station on sale day, in a Zomato kitchen at 9pm. When the maths runs out like this, industry does something almost cheeky: it stops trying to solve the problem and instead rolls the dice thousands of times to see what usually happens.

The one idea

When you cannot compute an answer, you can often sample it. Write your quantity as "the average of some random outcome", then generate that outcome many, many times on the computer and take the average. The more times you roll the dice, the closer that average sits to the truth. That is it. That is simulation.

It splits into two flavours, and we do both today.

Monte Carlo ignores the clock. It just wants an average. Throw random darts at a square with a circle drawn inside it, and four times the fraction landing inside the circle comes out as pi. No calculus, just darts and patience. The same trick finds the area under any awkward curve.

Discrete-event simulation cares about timing. Instead of averaging static outcomes, you press play on a little model of the bank: the clock ticks, a customer arrives, joins the queue, reaches the teller, is served, leaves. You watch the whole day unfold and read off how long people waited. It is a flight simulator for a queue.

Both rest on the same quiet fact, the law of large numbers: the average of many random samples settles down near the true value. One run is one story; enough runs tell you what really happens.

What you'll build today

  1. Estimate pi by throwing random darts at a square, and watch the estimate settle down as you add more darts.
  2. See the price of accuracy for yourself: the error only shrinks like 1/sqrt(N), so ten times better costs a hundred times more darts. Then estimate an area under a curve the same way.
  3. Meet the five distributions that power almost every simulation, draw a sample from each with NumPy, and see the shape each one makes.
  4. Build a single-teller bank queue in SimPy: random arrivals, random service, run the clock, and read off the average wait and queue length.
  5. Run that queue many times over and report a confidence interval, because one run is only one anecdote.

For the mathematically curious (optional)

Everything above is enough to do the lab. This box is the extra depth for anyone who wants it, and none of it is needed to finish.

Today's mathematical object is the random variable: a quantity X whose value is governed by a distribution, so we speak of its expectation E[X] (its true average) rather than a fixed number. Monte Carlo estimates E[X] by drawing independent samples X_1, ..., X_N and forming the sample mean. The law of large numbers guarantees this sample mean converges to E[X], and the central limit theorem sharpens that: the estimate's own spread shrinks like sigma / sqrt(N), which is exactly the 1/sqrt(N) cost you will measure. That is also why a confidence interval on a simulation result is mean ± about 2 standard errors.

The variance-reduction trick we mention, importance sampling, is the honest statement that you may sample from a different, more convenient distribution q instead of the true p, as long as you reweight each sample by the ratio p/q. Chosen well, q concentrates samples where they matter and cuts the variance sharply for the same N. On the queue side, Little's Law (L = lambda · W, average number in system equals arrival rate times average time in system) links the two things you measure, and the M/M/1 result that the wait grows like rho / (1 - rho) explains why the queue explodes as utilisation rho nears one. All a bonus, never a gate.

New words in this session

  • Monte Carlo — estimate a quantity by generating many random scenarios and averaging the outcomes.
  • law of large numbers — the average of many random samples settles close to the true value as you take more of them.
  • random variable — a number whose value is decided by chance, like a dice roll or a customer's waiting time.
  • distribution — the pattern of how often each value of a random quantity shows up (uniform, normal, exponential, and so on).
  • discrete-event simulation — a model that jumps along a clock from one event to the next (an arrival, a service finishing).
  • utilisation — the fraction of time a server is busy; waits blow up as it nears 100%.
  • Little's Law — average number in a system equals arrival rate times the average time each item spends in it.
  • confidence interval — an honest range for a result that came from randomness, reported instead of a single number.

Full definitions are in the glossary.

The problem: how long will the queue be?

  • Stand in an SBI branch at lunchtime. Customers wander in at random moments — some a minute apart, some in a bunch.
  • One teller serves them one at a time, and each person takes a different amount of time.
  • The manager wants a straight answer: how long will a customer wait, and will the queue get out of hand if the teller slows down a little?

The one idea: roll the dice many times

  • You cannot compute the answer, so you sample it instead.
  • Make the random outcome happen many, many times on the computer.
  • The more times you roll, the closer the average sits to the truth.
  • Simulation = run many random futures and average them. That is the whole session.

Each roll is random and tells you little. Average thousands of them and the answer appears.

Everything starts with one random number

  • Every simulation is built from one move: draw a random number of a chosen kind.
  • In Python, NumPy does this — 'rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)' gives you a dice-roller you can call.
  • Pick the kind of randomness that matches what you are modelling. That choice is where the real judgement lives.
  • Set a seed and a run becomes repeatable: same seed, same 'random' numbers, every time.

Four kinds of randomness you will actually use

  • Uniform — equal chance in a range: a raffle draw, a random location. rng.uniform(low, high)
  • Normal — many small effects added up: measurement error, daily returns. rng.normal(mean, spread)
  • Exponential — the gap between random events: time between two arrivals. rng.exponential(1/rate)
  • Poisson — count of events in a window: customers per hour. rng.poisson(rate)

Each shape models a different kind of chance. Choosing the right one is the modelling step.

The real risk: picking the wrong shape

  • The computer will happily simulate nonsense if you feed it the wrong kind of randomness.
  • Arrivals are almost never evenly spaced — people cluster; that is exactly what Poisson and exponential capture.
  • Where you can, fit the shape to real data rather than guessing it.
  • Garbage in, confident garbage out — the output looks just as precise either way.

The recipe has just three steps

  • 1. Write the thing you want as an average of some random outcome.
  • 2. Roll the dice many times to get many outcomes.
  • 3. Average them. That average is your estimate.
  • That is the whole method — Monte Carlo. Why it works is exactly the settling average from the opening picture.

The classic: estimate π by throwing darts

  • Throw random darts at a square with a circle inside.
  • The circle's area is π; the square's area is 4.
  • So the share landing inside should be π/4.
  • π ≈ 4 × (darts inside ÷ total darts).

The fraction of darts landing inside the circle, times four, estimates π.

Estimate π by counting darts inside

  • No formula for π anywhere — we measured it by counting which random darts fell inside.

CODE OUTPUT ▸ np.random.seed(0) n = 3000 x = np.random.uniform(-1, 1, n) # dart x-coordinates y = np.random.uniform(-1, 1, n) # dart y-coordinates # a dart is inside the circle when x*x + y*y <= 1 inside = (x**2 + y**2) <= 1 pi_estimate = 4 * np.sum(inside) / n print('darts inside:', int(np.sum(inside)), 'of', n) print('estimate of pi:', round(pi_estimate, 4)) darts inside: 2359 of 3000 estimate of pi: 3.1453

Finding an area is the same trick

An awkward area is just the fraction of darts that land under the curve.

Watch the average settle

Early on the estimate jumps around; as darts pile up it homes in on true π.

Accuracy is expensive: the 1/√N rule

  • The error shrinks like 1 over the square root of N.
  • Want the error half as big?
  • You must throw four times as many darts.
  • This is the price of the whole method.

Ten times more accuracy costs a hundred times more darts.

When timing and order matter

  • Monte Carlo just averages outcomes; it does not care about time.
  • But the bank queue is all about timing: who arrives when, who waits, who is served first.
  • So we press play on a little model and watch the day unfold. This is discrete-event simulation.
  • Three plain words: things flowing (customers, patients, orders), the resource they queue for (a teller, a doctor), and events (an arrival, a service finishing).

The clock jumps from event to event

Nothing changes between events, so the clock skips straight to the next one.

The single-teller queue

Arrivals join the line, wait for the one teller, get served, then leave.

The textbook queue, in plain words

  • The simplest queue has a name — M/M/1 — meaning random arrivals, random service times, one server.
  • Two knobs: how fast people arrive, and how fast the teller serves.
  • 'How busy the teller is' is just arrivals divided by service speed — the fraction of time the teller is working.
  • If people arrive faster than the teller can serve, the queue grows forever. Below that, it settles, and we can ask real questions.

Little's Law: one handy formula

A remarkably general link between how many are waiting, how fast they arrive, and how long each stays.

Push the teller too hard and the wait explodes

  • At low load the wait barely grows.
  • Near 100% busy it shoots toward infinity.
  • Randomness is the reason.
  • Plan for slack, not for full.

Run the teller past about 85% busy and the average wait shoots upward.

Being 95% busy is not the same as being efficient

  • The tempting mistake
  • 'The teller is 95% busy, so we are efficient'
  • Assumes busier is always better
  • Ignores that waits explode near 100%
  • Customers walk out; OPD patients wait dangerously
  • The reality
  • 95% busy can mean huge, volatile queues
  • Some slack is what keeps waits short
  • The right busy-ness depends on the wait you can tolerate
  • Add a teller before you hit the cliff

SimPy in three plain ideas

  • SimPy is a small Python library for press-play simulation — the whole bank model is about twenty lines.
  • Environment: holds the clock and runs the events.
  • Process: one thing's story, written step by step — arrive, ask for the teller, hold for a service time, release it, leave.
  • Resource: the limited thing they queue for — a teller, a doctor, a forklift — with a fixed count.

What a SimPy model says, in English

  • A customer: ask for a teller → wait until one is free → hold for a random service time → release the teller → leave.
  • Arrivals: every random gap, a new customer walks in.
  • Record each customer's wait, and run the clock for, say, an 8-hour day.
  • At the end, read off the average wait and the average queue length.

Run the bank queue and read the average wait

  • Press play once: 491 customers flow through and we read the wait straight off the clock.

CODE OUTPUT ▸ # customer() and generate_arrivals() define the model (notebook 03) waiting_times = [] np.random.seed(0) env = simpy.Environment() # holds the clock teller = simpy.Resource(env, capacity=1) # one teller env.process(generate_arrivals(env, teller)) env.run(until=2400) # run for 2400 minutes waits = np.array(waiting_times) print('customers served:', len(waits)) print('average wait :', round(waits.mean(), 2), 'min') print('longest wait :', round(waits.max(), 2), 'min') customers served: 491 average wait : 27.37 min longest wait : 106.08 min

The simulation recipe — works every time

  • 1. Define the randomness: which shapes, with what rates?
  • 2. Generate random events from those shapes.
  • 3. Run the system: just average, or press play on a clock.
  • 4. Measure the outcome you care about (a wait, a loss, a count).
  • 5. Repeat many times with fresh seeds.
  • 6. Average the results — and report the spread.

A simulation's answer is itself random. One run is an anecdote, not an answer.

The answer is a spread, not a single number

Report the average with a confidence range, never a lone number.

Average 30 runs for an honest wait and range

  • The single run said 27; averaging 30 says 13.4 with an honest window — that lone run was unlucky.

CODE OUTPUT ▸ runs = [] for seed in range(30): # replay the whole day 30 times np.random.seed(seed) waiting_times = [] env = simpy.Environment() teller = simpy.Resource(env, capacity=1) env.process(generate_arrivals(env, teller)) env.run(until=2400) runs.append(np.mean(waiting_times)) mean, se = np.mean(runs), 2 * np.std(runs) / np.sqrt(len(runs)) print('average over 30 runs:', round(mean, 2), 'min') print('95% range:', round(mean - se, 2), 'to', round(mean + se, 2), 'min') average over 30 runs: 13.39 min 95% range: 11.47 to 15.31 min

Add a second teller: watch the wait collapse

  • One extra teller does not halve the wait — it nearly erases it, from 13 minutes to under one.

CODE OUTPUT ▸ two = [] for seed in range(30): np.random.seed(seed) waiting_times = [] env = simpy.Environment() counter = simpy.Resource(env, capacity=2) # two tellers now env.process(generate_arrivals(env, counter)) env.run(until=2400) two.append(np.mean(waiting_times)) print('average wait, ONE teller :', round(np.mean(runs), 2), 'min') print('average wait, TWO tellers:', round(np.mean(two), 2), 'min') average wait, ONE teller : 13.39 min average wait, TWO tellers: 0.73 min

A checklist for an honest answer

  • Never report a single run — always use many replications.
  • Report an average with a confidence range, not a bare number.
  • Throw away the warm-up: a branch that starts empty is not yet typical, so cut the opening stretch before you measure.
  • Sanity-check against theory where you can (Little's Law, the M/M/1 formulas).
  • Say your assumptions out loud — especially the shapes of the randomness you fed in.

Monte Carlo: answer 'what are the odds?'

  • Simulate the uncertain inputs many times, then just count outcomes — no formula needed.

CODE rng = np.random.default_rng(0) sims = rng.normal(mu, sigma, size=100_000) # 100k what-if scenarios prob_loss = (sims < 0).mean() # P(loss) low, high = np.percentile(sims, [5, 95]) # 90% range print(prob_loss, low, high) # accuracy improves like 1/sqrt(N): 4x the runs -> half the error

Simulation traps (and how to dodge them)

  • Avoid
  • Reporting one run as the answer
  • Pretending a random output is exact
  • Measuring from an empty start (no warm-up)
  • Guessing input shapes (e.g. evenly-spaced arrivals)
  • Confusing 'busy' with 'has enough capacity'
  • Do instead
  • Run many times and average
  • Report an average with a confidence range
  • Throw away the warm-up; measure the steady state
  • Fit shapes to real data; try a few and check
  • Remember waits explode near 100% — plan slack

Key takeaways

  • The framework, in four words: Randomness → Model → Repeat → Average. Every simulation you will ever build is these four.
  • When a system is too tangled for a formula, simulate it — roll the dice many times; the law of large numbers does the rest.
  • Accuracy is expensive: the error shrinks only like 1/√N — halve the error, quadruple the runs.
  • Press-play (discrete-event) simulation steps through events on a clock; SimPy = environment, process, resource.
  • Waits explode as the server nears 100% busy — busy is not the same as having enough capacity.
  • A simulation's answer is random: many runs, a confidence range, and a warm-up thrown away.
