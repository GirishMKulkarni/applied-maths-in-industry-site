The problem

You have found a 2BHK in Pune you love, priced at ₹80 lakh, but your home loan will not clear for two months. Prices are rising and you are worried the flat will cost more by then. So you pay the builder a small booking amount, say ₹1 lakh, and in return the builder holds the ₹80 lakh price for you for two months. If the market jumps, you still buy at ₹80 lakh and you are delighted. If you change your mind, you simply walk away and lose only the ₹1 lakh booking amount.

That booking amount bought you a right without a duty: the right to buy at a fixed price, with no obligation to go through with it. On a share or the Nifty index, that exact contract is called an option, and it is one of the biggest markets in the world. It is not only for traders. An airline buys the right to buy fuel at a fixed price, an exporter the right to sell dollars at a fixed rate, a farmer the right to sell a crop at a fixed price, much like an MSP they paid for, and a fund manager the right to sell the Nifty before a Budget or an election.

Today we work out what such a right is fairly worth, on one real example that runs through the slides and all three notebooks: the right to buy Reliance shares at ₹1,560 at any time in the month after 31 December 2025, when Reliance closed at about ₹1,563.

The one idea

An option is the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell at a fixed price.

Because you are never forced through a bad deal, your loss is capped at the small amount you paid up front, while your gain can keep growing. Draw that on a chart and you get the famous bent "hockey-stick" shape. From that one idea comes the key sentence for pricing:

price = discounted average future payoff.

Average what the option might pay over all the prices Reliance could have in a month, then bring that average back to today's money. One formula, Black-Scholes, does the average exactly. Think of it as a calculator: five inputs in (the price today, the strike, the time left, the interest rate and the volatility), a call price and a put price out. We use it; we do not derive it, because the derivation needs a separate course. To be sure the formula is not magic, we also price the same option by simulating 100,000 possible futures, and watch the two answers land on the same number.

For the mathematically curious (optional)

Everything above is enough to do the lab. This box is the extra depth for those who want it, and none of it is needed to finish.

Today's mathematical object is the lognormal distribution and the risk-neutral expectation. The fair price of an option is a discounted expected payoff:

price = e^(-rT) × E[ payoff at expiry ]

The expectation E[·] is an ordinary probability-weighted average, but taken over a special spread of possible final prices. That spread is lognormal: the stock price at expiry is modelled as exp of a normal quantity, S_T = S × exp((r − σ²/2)T + σ√T × Z) with Z a standard bell-curve draw, which keeps the price positive and leans its spread to the right (a stock can multiply but can only fall to zero). The average is computed under risk-neutral probabilities, a bookkeeping device in which every asset is assumed to grow at the risk-free rate r, so no one's view of the market's direction sneaks into the price. It works because an option can be copied by continuously adjusting a holding of shares and cash (Delta hedging), and the copy's cost does not depend on anyone's forecast.

The Black-Scholes formula is that discounted expectation worked out exactly:

C = S·N(d1) − K·e^(−rT)·N(d2)
d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)T] / (σ√T),   d2 = d1 − σ√T

where N is the standard-normal CDF and N(d2) is the risk-neutral chance that the call ends in the money. Deriving it needs stochastic calculus (Itô's lemma), so we state it and use it. One identity needs no model at all, only the rule that there is no free money: put-call parity, C + K·e^(−rT) = P + S. The third notebook replaces the pen and paper with a Monte Carlo simulation and recovers the same price, which is the cleanest way to see that the formula is just a careful average. Its last Stretch cell shows why the lognormal model is only a first answer: Reliance's real daily moves have far fatter tails.

New words in this session

  • option — a contract giving the right, not the duty, to trade at a fixed price.
  • call / put — the right to buy (call) or to sell (put) at the fixed price.
  • strike — the fixed price written into the option, written K.
  • expiry — the deadline by which the right must be used.
  • premium — the price the buyer pays up front to own the option.
  • writer — the seller of an option, who collects the premium and takes on the duty.
  • payoff — what the option is worth at expiry, given the final stock price.
  • in / at / out of the money — whether using the option right now would pay something.
  • intrinsic value / time value — what using it now pays, and the rest of its price.
  • volatility — how jumpy the stock is; the yearly spread of its returns, written σ.
  • implied volatility — the σ that makes Black-Scholes match a market price.
  • the Greeks — how much the option price moves when one input moves (Delta, Gamma, Theta, Vega, Rho).
  • put-call parity — a fixed relationship linking a call, a put, the stock and cash, true with no model at all.

Full definitions are in the glossary.

The problem: locking a price you are not sure you will take

  • You have found a 2BHK in Pune you love, priced at ₹80 lakh. Your home loan will not clear for two months.
  • Prices are rising. You worry the flat will cost more by then, or sell to someone else.
  • So you pay the builder a booking amount of ₹1 lakh, and the builder holds the ₹80 lakh price for you for two months.
  • Prices jump: you still buy at ₹80 lakh and you are delighted. You change your mind: you walk away and lose only the ₹1 lakh.

The one idea, in one picture

  • An option is the RIGHT, not the obligation, to trade at a fixed price.
  • Never forced into a bad deal, so the loss stops at what you paid.
  • On the winning side, the gain keeps growing.
  • That bent line is the famous hockey stick. Today is about putting a fair price on it.

Call, put, and the words around them

Not just for traders: who buys options, and why

What the option pays on expiry day

  • Use the right only when it helps you. Otherwise the payoff is zero.
  • S_T = Reliance's price on expiry day, K = ₹1,560
  • Reliance ends at ₹1,700: call = max(140, 0) = ₹140, put = max(−140, 0) = ₹0
  • Reliance ends at ₹1,450: call = max(−110, 0) = ₹0, put = max(110, 0) = ₹110
  • Profit = payoff − premium. The call cost ₹41, so at ₹1,700 the buyer is ₹99 ahead.

Call and put, side by side

Solid: payoff on expiry day. Dashed: profit after the premium. The two worked numbers are marked.

The payoff rules in code

  • np.maximum keeps the bigger of the two, price by price: that is the kink in the hockey stick.

CODE K = 1560 # strike, in rupees S_T = np.array([1450, 1560, 1700]) # three possible prices in a month call_payoff = np.maximum(S_T - K, 0) # right to BUY at 1,560 put_payoff = np.maximum(K - S_T, 0) # right to SELL at 1,560 print("Reliance on expiry:", S_T) print("call payoff :", call_payoff) print("put payoff :", put_payoff) print("call profit :", call_payoff - 41) OUTPUT ▸ Reliance on expiry: [1450 1560 1700] call payoff : [ 0 0 140] put payoff : [110 0 0] call profit : [-41 -41 99]

Who pays whom

  • Every option has two sides.
  • The buyer pays the premium and gets a right.
  • The writer (the seller) collects the premium and takes on a duty.
  • The buyer's worst case is known on day one. The writer's is not.

Buyer vs writer: loss limited vs risk that can be large

  • Buyer: pays ₹41; the loss can never be bigger than that.
  • Writer: earns ₹41 in most months, which feels like free income.
  • But if Reliance jumps 10%, the writer loses about ₹118 a share.
  • Writing options without owning the shares is how beginners blow up accounts.

In, at or out of the money

Protective put: insurance for a share you own

  • Share + put = stock + insurance.
  • You own Reliance at ₹1,563 and buy the ₹1,560 put for ₹30.
  • However far Reliance falls, you lose at most ₹33 a share.
  • If it rises, you keep the rise, less the ₹30.

Covered call: renting out your shares

  • Share + sold call = renting out what you own.
  • Like renting out a flat you own: steady rent now, but you have agreed to sell at ₹1,560 if asked.
  • The ₹41 premium cushions small falls.
  • Above ₹1,560 the gain stops at ₹38 a share.

Straddle: a bet on a big move, direction unknown

  • Buy a call AND a put at ₹1,560: cost about ₹71.
  • Think of election results: a big move is certain, its direction is not.
  • On 4 June 2024 Reliance fell 7.5% in one day.
  • A quiet month loses both premiums.

Two portfolios: do they pay the same in a month?

Same payoff, so same price: put-call parity

  • If two portfolios pay the same in every future, they must cost the same today.
  • Left: portfolio A today. The call C, plus cash K·e^(−rT) = ₹1552.22 (grows to ₹1,560 in a month).
  • Right: portfolio B today. The put P, plus one share S = ₹1563.17.
  • Check with today's prices (from Black-Scholes, later): 40.81 + 1552.22 = 1593.03 and 29.86 + 1563.17 = 1593.03
  • No model needed: if they differed, buy the cheap side, sell the dear one, keep free money.

What is a fair price for the right?

  • Price = discounted average future payoff.
  • Average: over all the prices Reliance might have in a month, weighted by how likely each is.
  • Discounted: a rupee next month is worth a bit less today, so multiply by e^(−rT).
  • By hand, in a toy world with only two futures, ₹1,450 or ₹1,700, equally likely: average payoff (0 + 140) / 2 = ₹70, today ₹69.65.
  • Black-Scholes does this average exactly, over every possible future.

Think of Black-Scholes as a calculator

  • Five inputs in, two prices out.
  • Four you read off a screen: price, strike, time, interest rate.
  • One you must estimate: σ, how jumpy Reliance is. Its 2025 daily moves give 19.5% a year.
  • Nobody prices options by hand. Traders type in five numbers.

Inside the calculator: the formula, once

  • Value of the shares you expect to get, minus today's value of the cash you pay.
  • N(·) = area under the bell curve (scipy's norm.cdf). N(d₂) ≈ the chance the call ends in the money.
  • d₁ and d₂ are helper numbers the computer works out. No need to compute them by hand.
  • We use this formula; we do not derive it (that needs stochastic calculus).

Press the button: price the Reliance call and put

  • Using the call today pays only ₹3. The other ₹38 of its price is time value: the chance Reliance climbs further this month.

CODE prices = pd.read_csv("reliance_2023_2025.csv", index_col=0, parse_dates=True) daily = prices["Close"].loc["2025"].pct_change().dropna() S = prices["Close"].iloc[-1] # price on 31 Dec 2025 K = 1560 # strike T = 1 / 12 # one month, in years r = 0.06 # 6% a year sigma = daily.std() * np.sqrt(252) # yearly volatility from 2025 call = black_scholes_price(S, K, T, r, sigma, kind="call") put = black_scholes_price(S, K, T, r, sigma, kind="put") print("S =", round(S, 2), " sigma =", round(sigma, 3)) print("call price:", round(call, 2), " put price:", round(put, 2)) OUTPUT ▸ S = 1563.17 sigma = 0.195 call price: 40.81 put price: 29.86

Which option is worth more?

Why: the downside is capped, the upside is not

  • Every bad outcome counts as zero: the buyer walks away.
  • Every good outcome counts in full.
  • A wider spread reaches bigger payoffs and costs nothing on the left.
  • More uncertainty = more option value, for calls and puts.

Turn only the volatility dial

  • Same Reliance, same strike, same month. Double the jumpiness and the call is worth about double.

CODE for vol in [0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40]: price = black_scholes_price(S, K, T, r, vol, kind="call") print("volatility", vol, " -> call price", round(price, 2)) OUTPUT ▸ volatility 0.1 -> call price 23.94 volatility 0.2 -> call price 41.61 volatility 0.3 -> call price 59.45 volatility 0.4 -> call price 77.32

The Greeks: how the price reacts when one input moves

The Greeks of our Reliance call

  • Reliance up ₹10: the call gains about ₹5.6. A quiet day: it loses about 71 paise.
  • Volatility from 19.5% to 20.5%: the call gains about ₹1.78.

CODE g = greeks(S, K, T, r, sigma, kind="call") print("Delta:", round(g["delta"], 2)) print("Gamma:", round(g["gamma"], 4)) print("Theta:", round(g["theta_per_day"], 2), "rupees a day") print("Vega :", round(g["vega_per_1pct"], 2), "rupees per 1% more volatility") print("Rho :", round(g["rho_per_1pct"], 2), "rupees per 1% higher interest rate") OUTPUT ▸ Delta: 0.56 Gamma: 0.0045 Theta: -0.71 rupees a day Vega : 1.78 rupees per 1% more volatility Rho : 0.7 rupees per 1% higher interest rate

Where Black-Scholes breaks

  • It assumes
  • Volatility is one fixed number, the same for every strike
  • Daily moves follow a bell curve
  • Prices glide smoothly from one moment to the next
  • Trading is free and never stops
  • Reality
  • Volatility changes, and differs by strike: the smile
  • Fat tails: Reliance had 9 days beyond 3σ in three years; a bell curve expects 2
  • Prices jump on news: Reliance +5.6%, then -7.5%, on 3 and 4 June 2024
  • Spreads, brokerage, and a market closed at night and on holidays

Assumption 1 fails: the volatility smile

  • Take a market price and ask: which σ makes Black-Scholes match it? That is the implied volatility.
  • If the model were right, every strike would give the same σ: a flat line.
  • Real markets give a curve, highest for low strikes.
  • Traders still use Black-Scholes, with a different σ for each strike.

Assumptions 2 and 3 fail: fat tails and jumps

  • Left: Reliance jumped 5.6% on exit-poll day and fell 7.5% the next day.
  • No smooth path in between, so no chance to adjust a hedge.
  • Right: 4 June 2024 was a 6-sigma day. A bell curve says it should almost never happen.
  • So a bell curve underprices protection against big moves.

Can we trust the formula? Price it a second way

  • Formula route: five inputs into Black-Scholes. Instant.
  • Simulation route: roll 100,000 possible Reliance prices in a month, take each payoff, average, discount.
  • That is the fair-price sentence, done by brute force.
  • Same engine as S17's Monte Carlo. If both routes are right, they must agree.

Simulation route: 100,000 futures for Reliance

  • More futures, closer answer. The error shrinks like 1/√n: 100 times the futures, 10 times closer.

CODE np.random.seed(0) # same random futures for everyone for n in [1_000, 10_000, 100_000, 1_000_000]: Z = np.random.standard_normal(n) # n bell-curve draws S_T = S * np.exp((r - 0.5 * sigma**2) * T + sigma * np.sqrt(T) * Z) payoffs = np.maximum(S_T - K, 0) # call payoff in each future mc_price = np.exp(-r * T) * payoffs.mean() # average, then discount print(n, "futures -> call price", round(mc_price, 2)) print("formula route -> call price", round(call, 2)) OUTPUT ▸ 1000 futures -> call price 37.99 10000 futures -> call price 39.9 100000 futures -> call price 40.91 1000000 futures -> call price 40.86 formula route -> call price 40.81

Before you leave: can you answer these five?

  • What is an option? The right, not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) at a fixed price.
  • Why is the buyer safer than the writer? The buyer's loss stops at the premium; the writer's does not.
  • What is a fair price? The average future payoff, discounted to today. Black-Scholes computes it from S, K, T, r and σ.
  • Why does volatility raise the price? Bad outcomes count as zero, so wider swings only add upside.
  • How do we check the formula? Simulate many futures, average the payoffs, and compare.
