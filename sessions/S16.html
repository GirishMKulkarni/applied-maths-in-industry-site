The problem

You have saved ₹1 lakh and you want to put it to work in the share market. You open your broking app and see a hundred NSE names: TCS, Reliance, HDFC Bank, ITC, Maruti and the rest. The scary way to invest is to pick one, put the whole lakh in, and hope. If that one company has a bad year, so do you.

Everyone sensible spreads the money instead. But across what, and how much in each? Fifty thousand in TCS and fifty in Reliance? A little in ten different names? There are endless ways to split ₹1 lakh across ten stocks, and they are not equally good. Today you build the small piece of maths that picks a genuinely good split, and you run it on a real basket of Indian stocks.

The one idea

Don't put all your eggs in one basket. That old line is exactly right, and today you will see why it is right, in numbers.

Here is the picture. Every stock has good days and bad days. If you own two stocks that tend to go up and down at the same time, a bad day hits both at once and you feel the full bump. But if you own two stocks that do not move together, then on a day one drops the other often holds up, and the bumps partly cancel. Your whole pile of money feels calmer than either stock on its own. That calming effect is called diversification, and it is the closest thing to a free lunch in finance: less risk for the same return, just by mixing well.

So a portfolio is a mix: what fraction of your money goes into each stock. Two things follow from the mix. One is the return you can expect, which is just the weighted average of the stocks' returns. The other is the risk, the amount your money bounces around, and risk is the interesting one because a clever mix makes it small. The job today is to find the mix that gives you the most return for the least bounce.

What you'll build today

  1. Measure the return and the risk of a few NSE stocks, and build the table that records how every pair of them moves together.
  2. See diversification with your own eyes: mix the stocks and watch the risk drop below the risk of the typical single stock.
  3. Ask the computer for the calmest possible mix at each level of return, and join those best mixes into one curve, the efficient frontier.
  4. Pick the two portfolios everyone talks about: the calmest one, and the one with the best reward per unit of risk.
  5. Run the whole thing on a basket of ten NSE stocks and report the winning mix as a bar chart, with its return, its risk, and its Sharpe ratio.

For the mathematically curious (optional)

Everything above is enough to do the lab. This box is extra depth for those who want it, and none of it is needed to finish.

Write the mix as a weight vector w whose entries sum to 1. The portfolio's expected return is the dot product wᵀμ, where μ lists each stock's expected return. Its risk, measured as variance, is the quadratic form wᵀΣw, where Σ is the covariance matrix that records how every pair of stocks moves together. Because covariance can be negative, wᵀΣw can be smaller than any single stock's variance, and that is diversification stated in algebra.

Finding the least-risk mix for a demanded return is therefore: minimise wᵀΣw subject to wᵀμ ≥ r*, Σ wᵢ = 1, and (if we forbid short selling) w ≥ 0. A squared objective with straight-line constraints is exactly a Quadratic Program (QP). The covariance matrix Σ is positive semi-definite, so wᵀΣw is a convex bowl with a single lowest point, which means the solver always finds the one best answer. Sweeping the target return r* traces the efficient frontier. This is Harry Markowitz's 1952 idea, the founding result of modern portfolio theory and a 1990 Nobel Prize. The library cvxpy lets you write the QP almost exactly as it appears above and solves it for you.

New words in this session

  • return — how much a stock earns over a period, as a percentage of its price.
  • risk (volatility) — how much the returns bounce around; the spread of the returns.
  • covariance — a number saying whether two stocks tend to move up and down together.
  • covariance matrix (Σ) — the table holding the covariance of every pair of stocks.
  • diversification — spreading money across stocks that don't move together, to lower risk.
  • efficient frontier — the curve of best portfolios: the most return for each level of risk.
  • Sharpe ratio — return above the safe rate, divided by risk; reward per unit of risk.
  • CVXPY — a Python library where you state an optimisation almost as you write it on paper, and it solves it.

1 · What a portfolio is

A portfolio is a recipe for your money

  • A portfolio is just a recipe: how much of your money goes into each stock.
  • We write the recipe as shares (weights) — one number per stock.
  • The shares add up to 1: every rupee is placed somewhere.
  • A share of 0.20 for a stock means 20% of the money goes there.
  • A negative share would mean short selling — betting a stock falls; we will usually forbid it.

A portfolio is a weighted recipe of stocks

Choose the shares; the return and the bounce both follow from them.

The two numbers we care about

  • Return: how much we expect the money to grow.
  • Bounce (risk): how much that growth swings around — the uncertainty.
  • Bounce is measured as the spread of the returns; finance calls it volatility.
  • Every investor wants high return and low bounce. The whole game is the trade-off between them.

Each stock is a dot in the return-vs-bounce picture

A toy 10-stock NSE basket. Higher up = more return; further right = more bounce.

2 · Return is easy; bounce is the interesting part

Portfolio return is the easy part

  • The expected return of the portfolio is just the weighted average of the stocks' returns.
  • Put 60% in something expected to make 10% and 40% in something making 15% → 0.6 × 10 + 0.4 × 15 = 12%.
  • That is all there is to return. Comfortingly simple.
  • Stretch (optional): stacked up, that weighted sum is a dot product, wᵀμ — the linear algebra from the vectors primer.

Bounce is NOT a weighted average. Two bumpy stocks can combine into something far calmer than either one — if they don't move together.

Why bounce is different: moving together

  • The bounce of a mix depends on how the parts move together, not just on each part alone.
  • The number that captures 'do these two move together?' is called covariance.
  • It can be positive (move together), zero (unrelated) or negative (move oppositely).
  • Negative covariance literally subtracts bounce. That is the magic.
  • Stretch (optional): for two stocks the bounce is w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2w₁w₂·cov — that cross term is the covariance.

Mixing lowers the bounce — the closest thing to a free lunch

Two stocks, different 'move together' amounts. Moving oppositely (green) bends the curve furthest left.

Split ₹1 lakh five ways: the bounce drops

  • Same return as the average stock, but the bounce falls from 32.6% to 25.4% — over 7 points removed just by spreading.

CODE # put 20% in each of the five stocks w = np.ones(5) / 5 port_return = w @ mu.values port_risk = np.sqrt(w @ Sigma.values @ w) avg_single_risk = yearly_volatility.mean() print("Equal-mix return :", round(port_return*100, 2), "%") print("Equal-mix bounce :", round(port_risk*100, 2), "%") print("Avg single-stock :", round(avg_single_risk*100, 2), "%") OUTPUT ▸ Equal-mix return : 14.42 % Equal-mix bounce : 25.36 % Avg single-stock : 32.64 %

What diversification can and cannot do

  • It removes the bounce that is specific to one company — a fraud, a product recall, one bad quarter.
  • It cannot remove the bounce shared by the whole market — a rate hike, a pandemic, a budget shock.
  • So spreading lowers bounce down to a floor; it does not reach zero.
  • That is why a crash hits everything at once — more on that next session.

3 · The table of how stocks move together

One table holds every pair

  • With many stocks, we record how every pair moves together in one square table.
  • Finance calls it the covariance matrix, written Σ (capital sigma).
  • Down the diagonal sits each stock's own bounce; off the diagonal, each pair's 'move together' number.
  • It is symmetric — how A moves with B is the same as how B moves with A.
  • It has a special good property that keeps the bounce sensible; we unpack it in a moment.

The 'move together' table as a heatmap

Darker = the two stocks move together more. The diagonal is each stock's own bounce.

Print the 'move-together' table, Σ

  • One .cov() call builds the whole square table: the diagonal is each stock's own bounce, every other cell a pair's move-together number.

CODE # annualised covariance of the daily returns Sigma = daily_returns.cov() * 252 Sigma.round(4) OUTPUT ▸ TCS.NS INFY.NS RELIANCE.NS ITC.NS HDFCBANK.NS TCS.NS 0.1039 0.0438 0.0629 0.0824 0.0424 INFY.NS 0.0438 0.0765 0.0325 0.0545 0.0296 RELIANCE.NS 0.0629 0.0325 0.1419 0.0855 0.0431 ITC.NS 0.0824 0.0545 0.0855 0.1534 0.0544 HDFCBANK.NS 0.0424 0.0296 0.0431 0.0544 0.0701

The 'good property', in plain words

  • A proper bounce table always gives a positive bounce for any real mix — never zero, never negative.
  • Bounce that came out negative would be nonsense, so this rule keeps the maths honest.
  • Maths name: the table is positive-definite (its eigenvalues are all positive — the linear-algebra test).
  • It also means the 'best mix' problem has exactly one answer, with no false traps to get stuck in.
  • A badly estimated table can fail this — and then the maths breaks (we return to it at the end).

The total bounce curves like a bowl

Because the table is a proper one, the bounce curves upward in every direction — one lowest point.

Give the computer the returns and the 'move together' table, and it can score any mix — one number for return, one for bounce.

4 · Asking the computer for the best mix

The question, in plain words

  • Among all the ways to split the money, find the one with the least bounce for a return you are willing to demand.
  • Fix a target: 'I want at least this much return.'
  • Then make the bounce as small as possible, while using all the money.
  • Optionally add rules: no short selling, or no more than 20% in any one name.
  • Harry Markowitz worked this out in 1952 and won the 1990 Nobel Prize for it.

Why this is a friendly problem to solve

  • Bowl-shaped = one answer
  • Standard = well supported
  • It is a bowl
  • One lowest point, no false traps
  • A solver finds it fast and reliably
  • Scales to hundreds of stocks
  • It is a standard problem
  • Decades of solid, tested solver theory
  • The library CVXPY hands it to a solver for you
  • Same friendly family as the line-fitting in S7

Ask CVXPY for the lowest-bounce mix

  • You describe the goal (least bounce) and the rules; CVXPY returns the exact best split — here nothing in the fourth stock.

CODE w = cp.Variable(5) # the weights we solve for constraints = [ cp.sum(w) == 1, # invest every rupee w >= 0, # no short selling mu @ w >= mu.mean(), # hit at least the target return ] prob = cp.Problem(cp.Minimize(cp.quad_form(w, Sigma)), constraints) prob.solve() print("status :", prob.status) print("weights:", (w.value*100).round(2)) OUTPUT ▸ status : optimal weights: [ 7.5 46.7 10.59 0. 35.21]

The shape of the answer: the efficient frontier

  • Solve it once, for one target return, and you get one best mix — one point.
  • Sweep the target return from low to high and you trace out a whole curve of best mixes.
  • That curve is called the efficient frontier: the most return for each level of bounce.
  • Anything below the curve is achievable but wasteful; above it is impossible.

5 · The best curve & scoring a portfolio

Thousands of random mixes, and the best-possible curve

Each dot is a random split of the money; colour is its reward per unit of bounce. None beats the curve.

Reading the best curve

  • The curve is the upper-left edge of everything achievable — up and left is good.
  • On the curve you cannot get more return without accepting more bounce.
  • The leftmost point is the calmest possible mix — the least-bounce portfolio.
  • Where you choose to sit on the curve depends on your appetite for bounce.

Add a safe asset, and a straight line beats the curve

Blend the best risky mix with a safe asset (an FD or T-bill) and you can do better than the curve.

The best reward-per-bounce mix

  • The point where the straight line touches the curve is the best reward-per-bounce mix.
  • It gives the most extra return for each unit of bounce you take on.
  • Blend it with the safe asset for less bounce, or lean into it for more.
  • Its reward-per-bounce number has a name: the Sharpe ratio, coming up next.

The Sharpe ratio: reward per unit of bounce

Return above the safe rate, divided by bounce. The steepest line wins.

Sortino: count only the bad surprises

Nobody complains about upside. Sortino divides by the bad-day bounce only.

Drawdown: the worst drop from a peak

From the highest point to the lowest that follows — the fall that tests your nerve.

You describe it in words; the library does the solving

Each thing you want maps to one short CVXPY line. Schematic only — full code is in the notebook.

Cautions before you trust a best mix

  • The return guesses come from noisy past data — small errors, big effects.
  • The best curve is sensitive: nudge the guesses a little and the winning mix jumps around.
  • The rules matter: forbidding short selling or capping any one name changes the answer a lot.
  • Left unchecked, the optimiser happily dumps almost everything into a couple of names.

Small errors in the guesses wobble the whole curve

Six curves from slightly different return guesses. The 'best' answer is not stable.

The bounce table has to be a proper one

A proper table gives a sensible bounce shape; a broken one gives nonsense (negative bounce).

When it breaks: portfolio pitfalls

  • Avoid
  • Trusting noisy return guesses blindly
  • Ignoring brokerage, taxes and trading costs
  • Letting the optimiser pile into a few names
  • Feeding in a broken 'move together' table
  • Believing the pretty curve is what you'll get
  • Do instead
  • Smooth the guesses; lean on the calmest mix
  • Add a cost, and rebalance less often
  • Cap each name and forbid short selling
  • Check the table is proper, and repair it
  • Always test on data you did not optimise on

It looks great on old data; real life is humbler

The mix you tuned on history is flattering. Next year's reality sits lower and to the right.

Mean-variance portfolio in a few lines of CVXPY

  • Write the objective and constraints as maths; CVXPY finds the optimal weights. gamma sets risk appetite.

CODE import cvxpy as cp w = cp.Variable(n) # portfolio weights ret = mu @ w # expected return risk = cp.quad_form(w, Sigma) # variance prob = cp.Problem(cp.Maximize(ret - gamma * risk), [cp.sum(w) == 1, w >= 0]) # fully invested, long-only prob.solve(); print(w.value.round(3))

Key takeaways

  • A portfolio is a mix: shares of your money that add up to 1.
  • Return is the easy weighted average; bounce is not, because stocks move together.
  • Diversification lowers bounce for free — mix stocks that don't move together.
  • The efficient frontier is the curve of best mixes; a solver like CVXPY finds it.
  • Score a portfolio with Sharpe, Sortino and drawdown — no single number is enough.
  • Guesses are noisy and the table must stay proper — always test on unseen data.
