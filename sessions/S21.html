The problem

A quick-commerce company like Blinkit or Zepto wants to promise ten-minute delivery across a metro. To do that it runs small neighbourhood stores called dark stores. Each one it opens costs rent and staff every month, but a store close to you means a faster, cheaper delivery. Open too few and deliveries come from far away; open too many and the rent eats the profit. So someone has to decide, store by store, open this one or not, and then work out which neighbourhoods each open store serves.

That single word "or" is the whole session. Open this store, or not. Load this parcel on the van, or not. Send the van down this road, or not. Decisions that can only be yes or no, never halfway.

The one idea

Last session you met linear programming, where the answer could be any number: ship 2.4 tonnes, use 1.7 machines. That world is easy and a free solver cracks it in milliseconds.

Today we add one rule that sounds tiny and changes everything: some decisions must be whole. You cannot open half a dark store or send a third of a van. Very often the decision is just 0 or 1, a plain yes/no switch. Open it (1) or don't (0). Take the parcel (1) or leave it (0).

Here is the picture. In the easy world you could slide a dial smoothly to any setting and the best setting was easy to find. Now the dial can only click into whole notches, and the best notch can be surprisingly hard to find, because you often cannot just take the smooth answer and round it off. That is the catch, and coping with it is what today is about.

For the mathematically curious (optional)

Everything above is enough to do the lab. This box is extra depth, and none of it is needed to finish.

Today's mathematical object is combinatorial optimization: choosing the best option from a finite but enormous set of discrete possibilities. Formally we write an integer (or mixed-integer) program: a linear objective and linear constraints, exactly as in linear programming, plus the extra demand that some variables take integer values, very often x ∈ {0, 1}.

That extra demand breaks the geometry that made LP easy. If you drop it you get the LP relaxation, whose optimum sits at a corner of the feasible polygon, found fast. But that corner is usually fractional, so it is not a legal answer, and rounding it can be worse than optimal or even infeasible. Solvers instead run branch-and-bound: split on a fractional variable (one branch with x ≤ 2, one with x ≥ 3), use each sub-problem's relaxation as a bound, and prune any branch that cannot beat the best whole-number answer found so far. Cutting planes sharpen the relaxation further. These problems are NP-hard in general: no known algorithm solves every instance quickly. For routing the possibilities grow like a factorial ((n−1)!/2 distinct tours through n stops), so past a few dozen stops exact solving is hopeless and industry uses heuristics under a time budget. That link between clean models and brutal difficulty is the heart of operations research, but it is a bonus here, not a gate.

New words in this session

  • binary variable — a decision that can only be 0 or 1, a yes/no switch.
  • integer programming — optimization where some variables must be whole numbers.
  • knapsack problem — pick items under a capacity limit to maximise total value.
  • assignment problem — match each worker to exactly one task at least total cost.
  • facility location — choose which sites to open and who each serves, cheapest overall.
  • LP relaxation — the easy version with the whole-number rule dropped; its answer can be fractional.
  • heuristic — a fast, simple rule that finds a good answer, not always the perfect one.
  • combinatorial explosion — the number of possible choices blowing up as the problem grows.

1 · Four classic yes/no models

Four models you'll meet again and again

  • Packing (knapsack) — pick items under a capacity to maximise value.
  • Matching (assignment) — give each worker one task, at least cost.
  • Dark stores (facility location) — choose which sites to open.
  • Coverage (set covering) — reach every area with the fewest depots.
  • Each is just: some yes/no decisions, a goal, and a few rules.

Packing a bag: value under a weight limit

  • One yes/no per parcel: take it, or leave it.
  • Goal: make the total value as large as possible.
  • Rule: the total weight must fit the van.
  • That's the whole model — a goal and one rule.

Choose a subset of parcels to maximise total value without exceeding the van.

Matching: one agent, one route

  • One yes/no per pair: does this agent take this route?
  • Goal: smallest total cost (or time, or distance).
  • Rule: each agent takes exactly one route.
  • Rule: each route gets exactly one agent.

Every agent gets exactly one route; every route exactly one agent.

Which dark stores to open

  • One yes/no per site: open this store, or not (it has a rent).
  • One yes/no per link: does this store serve this area?
  • Goal: rent of opened stores + delivery distance, as low as possible.
  • Key rule: you may serve an area only from a store you opened.

Open a subset of candidate sites; serve every neighbourhood from an open one.

Coverage: fewest depots to reach everyone

  • One yes/no per depot: use this depot, or not.
  • Goal: the fewest depots (or the lowest total cost).
  • Rule: every area is within reach of at least one chosen depot.
  • 'At least one' — an area may be covered more than once.

Each depot covers nearby areas; pick the fewest depots covering all areas.

One line of code behind all four models

  • Same mathematics, different business. cat="Binary" is the only thing that separates today from last week's PuLP code.

CODE import pulp # The same one-line move, four different businesses: take = pulp.LpVariable("take_parcel", cat="Binary") # load it, or not match = pulp.LpVariable("asha_routeA", cat="Binary") # this agent here, or not opn = pulp.LpVariable("open_andheri", cat="Binary") # open the site, or not use = pulp.LpVariable("use_depot_3", cat="Binary") # pick the depot, or not # Then every model is the same three moves: # m += <goal: a linear sum of the switches> # m += <each business rule, as a linear (in)equality> # m.solve(pulp.PULP_CBC_CMD(msg=0))

All four models are the same question

Can mathematics build a better AI?

  • Even choosing the inputs for an AI model is a yes/no optimisation problem.
  • 100 candidate inputs: age, income, education, location, credit history, ...
  • Using all of them can overfit, slow training and cost more to collect.
  • Accuracy is not a simple sum of the switches, so this one is hard to solve exactly.

Which forest patches should we protect?

  • 50 forest patches. The budget protects only about 10. Which ones?
  • Would you protect the biggest patch? The cheapest? The one with rare species?
  • This is the knapsack: value = biodiversity, weight = cost, van = budget.
  • Real planners also make sure every species is covered at least once.

Who should we hire?

  • Ten applicants, three roles, ₹25 lakh. Is the most skilled person always hired?
  • One switch per applicant; skill score from the interview, salary in ₹ lakh a year.
  • 'Exactly one per role' is a sum of switches = 1, like the matching model.
  • Next slide: the solver's answer, and who misses out.

The most skilled candidate does not get the job

  • Asha (95) misses out: her ₹14 lakh squeezes the other roles. The best in every role costs ₹33 lakh.

CODE people = [ # (name, role, skill score, salary in Rs lakh) ("Asha","DS",95,14), ("Rohan","DS",80,8), ("Meera","DS",70,6), ("Kiran","Eco",88,9), ("Sana","Eco",75,6), ("Dev","Eco",65,5), ("Priya","GIS",90,10), ("Arjun","GIS",78,7), ("Vikram","GIS",72,6), ("Neha","GIS",60,4)] m = pulp.LpProblem("hiring", pulp.LpMaximize) x = {p[0]: pulp.LpVariable(p[0], cat="Binary") for p in people} m += pulp.lpSum(s * x[n] for n, r, s, c in people) # total skill m += pulp.lpSum(c * x[n] for n, r, s, c in people) <= 25 # budget for role in ["DS", "Eco", "GIS"]: # one per role m += pulp.lpSum(x[n] for n, r, s, c in people if r == role) == 1 m.solve(pulp.PULP_CBC_CMD(msg=0)) print(pulp.LpStatus[m.status], [n for n in x if x[n].value() > 0.5], pulp.value(m.objective)) OUTPUT ▸ Optimal ['Meera', 'Kiran', 'Priya'] 248.0

Shark Tank mathematics

  • You have ₹1 crore. Five startups each ask for funding. Which do you back?
  • Would you invest in all five? You can't: their asks add up to more than ₹1 crore.
  • Each deal is all or nothing, so it is a knapsack again: returns under a budget.
  • Real investors add rules: at most two in one sector, at least one early-stage.

Making a university timetable

  • Should this class be in Room A at 10 AM? One switch per class, room and time.
  • No teacher in two places, no room with two classes, no student with a clash.
  • 50 classes × 20 rooms × 40 slots = 40,000 switches, for one department.
  • Most universities and schools now build timetables with software like this.

Turning reality into mathematics

  • Build this warehouse? → 0/1
  • Hire this candidate? → 0/1
  • Protect this forest patch? → 0/1
  • Invest in this startup? → 0/1
  • Include this AI feature? → 0/1
  • Open this retail store? → 0/1
  • Different questions, one move: a switch per yes/no choice.

2 · Why finding the best answer is hard

The tempting shortcut that fails

  • Tempting idea: ignore 'must be whole', solve the easy smooth problem, then round.
  • Solving without the whole-number rule is called the 'LP relaxation' — the easy version with the rule dropped.
  • It is fast, but its best answer usually sits on a fraction.
  • And rounding that fraction can be worse than the true answer, or even not allowed at all.

The easy answer can land on a fraction

The best corner of the smooth region is at (2.3, 3.6) — not a whole-number dot.

Watch the relaxation return 0.667

  • The answer is 0.667 — but how do we pack two-thirds of a parcel? The honest 0/1 answer is 0: the item simply does not fit.

CODE import pulp # A 3 kg parcel, but only 2 kg of room. Allow a fraction, just to see. m = pulp.LpProblem("relaxed", pulp.LpMaximize) x = pulp.LpVariable("x", lowBound=0, upBound=1) m += 10 * x # goal: as much value as we can m += 3 * x <= 2 # the weight must fit m.solve(pulp.PULP_CBC_CMD(msg=0)) print("Best fraction:", round(x.value(), 3)) OUTPUT ▸ Best fraction: 0.667

Why rounding is dangerous

Round down and you lose value; round up and you can leave the allowed region.

The number of options explodes

  • n yes/no switches: 2ⁿ combinations.
  • n stops on a route: about n!/2 tours.
  • At n = 20 that is already astronomical.
  • So we cannot just try them all — but we needn't.

With n stops or n switches, the number of possible choices grows explosively.

Easy problem, hard problem

  • Easy: shortest route, Pune to Mumbai
  • Google Maps answers in well under a second
  • Clever methods never check every route
  • Double the road network: roughly double the work
  • Solved exactly, every time, at any size.
  • Hard: visit 100 cities exactly once
  • 99!/2 ≈ 4.7 × 10¹⁵⁵ possible tours
  • More tours than atoms in the universe (about 10⁸⁰)
  • Add one city: multiply the work by about 100
  • No known method is guaranteed fast. This is 'NP-hard'.

Count the routes as stops grow

  • Each extra stop multiplies the count of possible round-trips — by 20 stops there are 60 quadrillion of them.

CODE import math print("stops | possible routes (about (n-1)!/2)") for n in [5, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20]: routes_n = math.factorial(n - 1) // 2 print("{:>4} | {:,}".format(n, routes_n)) OUTPUT ▸ stops | possible routes (about (n-1)!/2) 5 | 12 8 | 2,520 10 | 181,440 12 | 19,958,400 15 | 43,589,145,600 20 | 60,822,550,204,416,000

3 · How free solvers cope

Split, bound, prune — in plain words

  • Solve the easy smooth version. If it came out all-whole, you're done.
  • If a decision came out fractional (say x = 2.3), split into two cases: one with x ≤ 2, one with x ≥ 3.
  • Each case's easy version gives a 'best it could ever do' — a bound.
  • If a branch's best-case can't beat the best whole answer you already have, throw it away unexplored (prune it).

The search tree: split, bound, prune

Split on a fraction; bound each branch; prune the ones that cannot win.

The free toolchain you already have

Write the model in PuLP or OR-Tools; the free CBC solver does the split-bound-prune.

4 · Routing: which route to take

A real model usually mixes both kinds of decision

Smooth 'how much' decisions and whole-number 'yes/no' decisions, together in one model.

Routing: the problems that built the field

  • One van, many stops: visit every stop once, return home, shortest total distance — the Travelling Salesman Problem.
  • A fleet of vans from one hub: split the stops across vans — the Vehicle Routing Problem.
  • Add real constraints: van capacity, delivery time slots, driver shifts.
  • These are everywhere in Indian logistics — and the number of options is enormous.

Route a van with the nearest-stop rule

  • Start at the hub.
  • Drive to the nearest stop you haven't visited yet.
  • Repeat until no stops are left.
  • Return to the hub to close the loop.

Spot where the route crosses itself — a crossing means a shorter route exists. This whole loop comes from the four-step rule — quick and decent, not guaranteed shortest.

Two ways to attack a routing problem

  • Prove the best answer
  • Uses the split-bound-prune machinery
  • Gives the guaranteed shortest routes
  • Fine for small problems
  • Time explodes past a few dozen stops
  • Find a good answer fast
  • Simple rules: nearest-stop, swap-to-improve
  • Google OR-Tools' routing engine
  • No guarantee it is the very best
  • Scales to thousands of stops

What makes last-mile genuinely hard in India

  • Density and chaos: narrow lanes, unclear addresses, heavy traffic.
  • Time slots: customers want delivery in a window; some pay cash on delivery.
  • Capacity: each van holds a limited number of parcels.
  • Scale: thousands of stops per city, re-planned every single day.
  • So everyone uses fast good-enough rules with a time budget, not perfect optimisation.

5 · Build one yourself: lab & wrap-up

How to formulate any yes/no model — five steps

  • 1 · Spot the yes/no decisions. Open it? Take it? Assign it?
  • 2 · Give each one a 0/1 variable. In PuLP: cat="Binary".
  • 3 · Write the goal. One linear sum to maximise or minimise.
  • 4 · Write the rules. Each business rule becomes a linear (in)equality between the switches.
  • 5 · Solve and read it back. PuLP + the free CBC solver; translate the 0s and 1s into a business decision.

Common first-timer traps

  • Avoid
  • Solving the easy version and just rounding
  • Ignoring problem size and time limits
  • Expecting a proven-best answer on a huge route
  • Trusting one number without a map
  • Do instead
  • Let the solver enforce whole numbers
  • Set a time limit; accept the best found
  • Use a fast good-enough rule at scale
  • Always plot the routes and sanity-check

Key takeaways

  • Same recipe as last week — variables + a goal + rules — plus ONE new demand: the answer must be whole.
  • The star case is the 0/1 switch: x is 0 or 1, never halfway. Open the store, load the parcel, or not.
  • Packing, matching, dark stores, coverage: the same yes/no mathematics wearing four business suits.
  • Don't solve the easy version and round — fractions lie and the options explode; let split-bound-prune search.
  • Routing a fleet is huge; at scale use a fast good-enough rule with a time budget, not perfect solving.
