The problem

A fund desk at a Mumbai asset manager has ₹100 crore to spread across a basket of Nifty stocks. They want the split that keeps the risk as low as possible for the return they are targeting. A computer searches through the possible splits and hands back an answer: "put this much in each stock". But the desk has to defend that number to a client and to a regulator. So there is one uncomfortable question behind every such answer. Is this genuinely the best split, or is it just where the search happened to stop?

That same question sits behind almost every "find the best setting" task in industry: the cheapest delivery routing, the line that best fits past sales, the boundary a bank draws between safe and risky loans. Today you meet the single property that decides whether you can answer that question with confidence.

The one idea

Picture the problem as a landscape and the answer as the lowest point. If the landscape is a smooth bowl, there is exactly one bottom. Drop a marble anywhere and it rolls to that same lowest point every time. So when the computer stops, you know for certain it found the best answer. Nothing better is hiding elsewhere.

If instead the landscape is wobbly, full of little dips, the marble settles in whichever dip it happens to be near. That might be far from the true bottom, and worse, you cannot tell from inside the dip. A bowl-shaped problem is called convex, and it is the kind a computer can always solve to the genuine best.

Here is the warm surprise waiting in this session. A whole family of models you have already built are secretly bowls: fitting a line, Ridge and Lasso, the support vector machine, the Nifty portfolio. Once a problem is a bowl, you only have to describe your goal and the rules you must obey, and a solver returns the guaranteed best answer. You describe the problem; the solver solves it. (A tool called CVXPY lets the confident write these problems out in maths notation, but you never have to in order to get the answer.)

For the mathematically curious (optional)

Everything above is enough to do the lab. This box is the extra depth for those who want it, and none of it is needed to finish.

Today's mathematical objects are the convex set and the convex function. A set is convex if, for any two points x and y inside it, the whole segment t·x + (1−t)·y for t in [0, 1] stays inside. A function is convex if every chord lies on or above its graph, equivalently f(t·x + (1−t)·y) ≤ t·f(x) + (1−t)·f(y). Their payoff is one sentence: in a convex problem, any local optimum is the global optimum, which is exactly why such problems are considered "solved".

The problems in the lab are all convex for a reason you can name. Least squares minimises the squared norm ‖Xw − y‖², a bowl. Ridge adds λ‖w‖₂² and Lasso adds λ‖w‖₁, both convex, so the sum stays a bowl. The SVM minimises ½‖w‖² subject to one linear constraint per point, a convex quadratic program. The Markowitz portfolio minimises wᵀΣw, convex because the covariance matrix Σ is positive semidefinite.

The KKT conditions are the calculus of the optimum, and we treat them as intuition only, never as a theorem to prove. At the best point the objective's downhill direction is exactly balanced by the active constraints, ∇f = −Σ λᵢ ∇gᵢ; each price λᵢ ≥ 0; and a constraint either binds or its price is zero (complementary slackness). For a convex problem, a point meeting these conditions is certified globally optimal. CVXPY automates all of this through Disciplined Convex Programming (DCP): you assemble the objective from known convex building blocks, CVXPY checks it really is convex, then a solver returns the global answer along with those prices. All of this is a bonus, not a gate.

New words in this session

  • convex set — a shape with no dents: the line between any two inside points stays inside.
  • convex function — a bowl-shaped cost: every chord lies on or above the curve.
  • convex optimization — finding the lowest point of a bowl-shaped problem, which a computer can always do reliably.
  • global optimum — the genuinely best answer, not just the best nearby.
  • local optimum — a dip that beats its neighbours but is not the overall best; a trap that only wobbly problems have.
  • CVXPY — a Python library where you write a convex problem as algebra and it returns the best answer.
  • quadratic program (QP) — a bowl-shaped objective with straight-line fences; the SVM is one.
  • support vector — a data point sitting right on the edge of an SVM's margin; a handful of these fix the whole boundary.

Full definitions are in the glossary.

APPLICATIONS OF MATHEMATICS IN INDUSTRY Module 4 — Optimization & Operations Research · Session S22 · 2 hours Module 4 · after straight lines (S20) and yes/no (S21), the curves

The problem: split an ad budget

  • A Pune skincare brand has ₹20 lakh for next month's ads.
  • Three places to spend it: TV, Instagram and Google search.
  • Last month's test: the first ₹1 lakh brought in extra sales of
  • TV ₹30 lakh, Instagram ₹24 lakh, Google ₹18 lakh.
  • How should the ₹20 lakh be split?

What S20 would say: all ₹20 lakh on TV

  • Treat each channel as a straight line: every lakh on TV brings ₹30 lakh, however much you spend.
  • Then an LP puts everything on the best line. That is the corner answer from S20.
  • Predicted sales: 20 × 30 = ₹600 lakh.
  • Would any marketing head believe that?

Reality: the curve flattens

Diminishing returns: each extra lakh buys less than the one before. All on TV really earns about ₹134 lakh, not ₹600.

All three channels flatten

Sales from x lakh in a channel = a√x, with a = 30, 24, 18.

Write it down: the same recipe as S20

  • Variables, a goal, a rule. Only the goal is now curved.
  • x = ₹ lakh spent in each channel; the budget rule is the same straight line as before.
  • The square roots are the only new thing, and they are why LP can't do this.

1 · The equal-marginal rule

  • Keep moving money until the next rupee earns the same everywhere

Think like the money

  • Suppose TV's next lakh earns ₹3 lakh, but Instagram's next lakh earns ₹6 lakh.
  • Move a lakh from TV to Instagram: lose 3, gain 6. Better.
  • Keep moving while one channel pays more for the next lakh.
  • You stop when the next lakh pays the same in every channel.
  • That point is the best split. Economists call 'what the next unit earns' the marginal return.

The picture: equal slopes

Slope of the curve = what the next lakh earns. At the best split, it is about ₹4.7 lakh in all three channels.

Worked by hand

  • The slope of a√x is a / (2√x). Set all three equal and solve.
  • Equal slopes means √x grows in step with a, so spend grows with a².
  • 30² : 24² : 18² = 900 : 576 : 324. Share ₹20 lakh in that ratio.
  • TV ₹10 lakh, Instagram ₹6.4 lakh, Google ₹3.6 lakh.

The same answer from CVXPY

  • You met CVXPY in S16. Here it handles a curve PuLP can't. status 'optimal' is a guarantee, not a guess.

CODE import cvxpy as cp import numpy as np a = np.array([30, 24, 18]) # TV, Instagram, Google: sales from the first lakh budget = 20 # ₹ lakh spend = cp.Variable(3, nonneg=True) sales = cp.sum(cp.multiply(a, cp.sqrt(spend))) problem = cp.Problem(cp.Maximize(sales), [cp.sum(spend) == budget]) problem.solve() print(problem.status) print("spend (₹ lakh):", spend.value.round(1)) print("sales (₹ lakh):", round(problem.value)) OUTPUT ▸ optimal spend (₹ lakh): [10. 6.4 3.6] sales (₹ lakh): 190

Was it worth it?

Same budget. Spreading it earns about ₹56 lakh more than going all in on the 'best' channel.

Using the rule in real work

  • Ask 'what does the next rupee earn here?', not 'which option is best on average?'
  • If one place earns more at the margin, move money there. Stop when they match.
  • Caps are fine: if TV can take at most ₹8 lakh, the rest is shared the same way.
  • The same rule splits a sales team's time, overtime across factory lines, and a crop budget across fields.
  • Sales teams: Allocate employees across regions until the expected marginal revenue from another employee is comparable.
  • Factories: Allocate overtime across production lines while accounting for increasing overtime costs.
  • Agriculture: Allocate irrigation water or fertilizer across fields where marginal yield responses differ.
  • Advertising: Shift spending toward the channel that produces more additional sales.
  • Electricity: Distribute generation across plants so that marginal costs are balanced.

PRACTICAL TIPS

2 · Same rule, different industry

  • Running a power grid at the lowest cost

The problem: tonight's peak

  • A state grid needs 800 MW for the evening peak.
  • Three plants can supply it: two coal, one gas.
  • Each plant gets more expensive per unit the harder it runs (older boilers, less efficient at full load).
  • How much should each plant produce?

Write it down

  • Cost per plant = b·x + c·x². The next kWh costs b + 2c·x.
  • b = cost per kWh when the plant runs gently; c = how fast that climbs.
  • Coal A: b 2.0, c 0.004. Coal B: b 2.5, c 0.002. Gas: b 3.0, c 0.005.
  • Same rule as marketing: run every plant until its next kWh costs the same λ.

The picture: one flat line

Raise one price line until the plants together give 800 MW. That happens at ₹4.16 per kWh.

CVXPY splits the load

  • ₹2,625 thousand an hour instead of ₹4,160 for 'all on the cheapest plant'. That is about 37% less.

CODE plants = ["Coal A", "Coal B", "Gas"] b = np.array([2.0, 2.5, 3.0]) # ₹ per kWh at low output c = np.array([0.004, 0.002, 0.005]) # how fast that cost climbs demand = 800 # MW mw = cp.Variable(3, nonneg=True) cost = cp.sum(cp.multiply(b, mw) + cp.multiply(c, cp.square(mw))) problem = cp.Problem(cp.Minimize(cost), [cp.sum(mw) == demand]) problem.solve() for name, m in zip(plants, mw.value): print(f"{name:<7} {m:5.0f} MW") print(f"Cost: ₹{problem.value:,.0f} thousand per hour") OUTPUT ▸ Coal A 270 MW Coal B 414 MW Gas 116 MW Cost: ₹2,625 thousand per hour

3 · When can we trust 'optimal'?

  • Bowls and dents

Bowl vs dented

A solver walks downhill from where it starts. In a bowl every walk ends at the same bottom. With dents, it can stop in the wrong one.

Try it: three starting points

  • Bowl: the same answer every time. Dented: two different answers, and only one is the true best (−1.30).

CODE from scipy.optimize import minimize bowl = lambda x: (x[0] - 1)**2 dent = lambda x: x[0]**4 - 3*x[0]**2 + x[0] for start in [-2, 0.5, 2]: b = minimize(bowl, [start]).x[0] d = minimize(dent, [start]).x[0] print(f"start {start:>4}: bowl -> {b:5.2f} dented -> {d:5.2f}") OUTPUT ▸ start -2: bowl -> 1.00 dented -> -1.30 start 0.5: bowl -> 1.00 dented -> 1.13 start 2: bowl -> 1.00 dented -> 1.13

What makes a problem a bowl?

  • Bowls (safe)
  • Dents (careful)
  • Straight-line rules: budgets, demand, capacity
  • Costs that climb faster (x², overtime, fuel)
  • Gains that flatten (√x, log x, ad reach)
  • Sums of these
  • Yes/no decisions (S21)
  • Volume discounts and price slabs
  • Fixed setup costs: a machine is on or off
  • Neural network training (S13)

CVXPY refuses a dented problem

  • CVXPY only accepts problems it can prove are bowls. That is why its 'optimal' can be trusted.

CODE x = cp.Variable() dented = x**4 - 3*x**2 + x cp.Problem(cp.Minimize(dented), [x >= -3, x <= 3]).solve() OUTPUT ▸ DCPError: Problem does not follow DCP rules. The objective is not DCP.

You've been inside bowls all course

Which tool, when?

Lab: move the money

  • In the lab: change the marketing and power problems and watch the equal-marginal rule hold.
  • 1. Run the marketing code. Check by hand that a / (2√x) is about 4.7 for every channel.
  • 2. Add a rule: TV can take at most ₹8 lakh. Predict the new split first, then solve.
  • 3. Add a fourth channel (YouTube, a = 21). Who loses money to it?
  • 4. Dispatch: cap Coal B at 350 MW. Which plant picks up the slack, and what happens to λ?
  • 5. Stretch: give CVXPY a volume-discount cost and read the error it gives.
  • Deliverable: a notebook with the two models, your three changes, and one sentence per change explaining the new split using the equal-marginal rule.

Key takeaways

  • When returns flatten (or costs climb), the best answer spreads out; it is rarely all on one option.
  • The equal-marginal rule: at the best split, the next rupee earns the same everywhere.
  • Same rule for ad budgets, power plants, sales teams and factory lines.
  • Convex means a bowl: no dents, so the solver's 'optimal' is the true best.
  • CVXPY solves bowls and refuses dents; dented problems need restarts or simulation.
