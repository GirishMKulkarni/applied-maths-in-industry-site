The problem

A Flipkart seller keeps one product on the shelf, say phone chargers. Every day a random number of customers buy one. Keep too little stock and the shelf goes empty, so sales are lost and customers go to a rival. Keep too much and cash and warehouse space sit idle. So two questions have to be answered: how low should stock fall before reordering, and how much should each order bring in? Today's deliverable is a small study that answers exactly that for one shop.

That restocking puzzle turns out to be one example of a much bigger pattern, and the pattern is where we start.

The one idea

Lots of real systems are always in one of a few situations and hop between them, one step at a time, where the next hop depends only on where you are right now. The weather is sunny or rainy. A phone customer is loyal or has ported out to a rival. A machine is working or broken. A warehouse holds this much stock today and a different amount tomorrow. Give each hop a chance and you have what is called a Markov chain: a few states, and a fixed set of odds for moving between them.

Here is the useful part. Watch such a system for long enough and it settles. Start the weather off as "definitely sunny" and roll it forward day after day, and the chance of sun drifts and then stops drifting, landing near 57%. Start it off "definitely rainy" and it settles at the same 57%. After a while the system forgets where it began. That settled mix, the long-run share of sunny days, is nearly always the thing we actually want to know: the long-run churn rate, the long-run fraction of time a machine is up, the long-run spread of stock on a shelf.

And you can get that settled mix two ways: roll the system forward until it stops moving, or just play it out at random for a long time and count. The inventory policy is one such system, and we tune it by playing it out.

What you'll build today

  1. Build a small weather chain, write its table of jump chances, and step a starting guess forward day by day until it stops moving.
  2. Read off the long-run mix, then confirm it a second way by simulating the chain, and watch the two answers agree.
  3. Simulate an (s, S) restock policy for a shop, draw the stock "sawtooth", and read off its daily cost and its service level.
  4. Run a full mini operations-research study: estimate demand from past sales, try several policies, pick the cheapest one that keeps 95% of customers served, and write the recommendation with its assumptions. That is the Module 4 deliverable.

For the mathematically curious (optional)

Everything above is enough to do the lab. This box is the extra depth for anyone who wants it, and none of it is needed to finish.

Today's mathematical object is the transition matrix P: a square matrix whose rows are probabilities and each sum to 1 (a stochastic matrix). Pushing a probability row-vector one step into the future is the matrix product p · P, and n steps is the matrix power p · Pⁿ. The settled mix, the stationary distribution π, is the distribution that one more step leaves unchanged: π · P = π. Rearranged, that says π is a left eigenvector of P with eigenvalue 1. Every stochastic matrix has 1 as an eigenvalue, and for a chain that is irreducible (you can get from any state to any other) and aperiodic (not locked into a rigid cycle), that π is unique and is reached from any starting mix. The first notebook finds π both ways and checks they agree. And the (s, S) inventory system is itself a Markov chain, on stock levels, so its long-run stock spread is a stationary distribution too. All of this is a bonus, never a gate.

New words in this session

  • state — one of the few situations a system can be in (sunny/rainy, working/broken).
  • Markov chain — a system that hops between states, where the next state depends only on the current one.
  • transition matrix — the table of jump chances; each row sums to 1.
  • stationary distribution — the steady long-run mix the system settles into.
  • (s, S) policy — a restock rule: when stock falls to s, order back up to S.
  • service level — the fraction of customer demand you actually meet from stock.
  • holding cost / stockout — the cost of stock sitting idle, and of running out.
  • lead time — the delay between placing an order and it arriving.

The problem: how much stock should a shop hold?

  • A Flipkart seller keeps one product on the shelf — say phone chargers.
  • Each day a random number of customers buy one. Some days five, some days none.
  • Keep too little and the shelf goes empty: lost sales, and customers go to a rival (a 'stockout').
  • Keep too much and cash and warehouse space sit idle (the 'holding cost').
  • Two questions: how low should stock fall before we reorder, and how much should each order bring in?

  • Many systems sit in one of a few states and hop between them.
  • Weather is sunny or rainy; a shelf holds this much stock or that.
  • Watch it long enough and it settles down.
  • That settled mix is almost always what we want to know.

Start a hopping system anywhere and, after a while, it settles into the same steady mix and forgets where it began.

A system that lives in one of a few situations

  • Picture something that is always in exactly one of a few STATES.
  • A 'state' is just a label for the situation right now.
  • Weather is Sunny or Rainy. A customer is Active or Churned. A machine is Working or Broken.
  • Each step (a day, a week, a click) it may hop to another state, by fixed odds.

The one rule that makes it a Markov chain

  • Knowing the full history adds nothing beyond today's state.
  • This 'memoryless' rule is why one small table of odds can describe the whole system.
  • It is a simplification — sometimes great, sometimes poor.

Where it goes next depends only on where it is now — not on how it got there.

Everyday systems that hop between states

Putting all the jump chances in one table

  • List the states. Make a square table, one row and one column per state.
  • The entry in row i, column j is the chance of hopping from i to j in one step.
  • Rows are where you are NOW; columns are where you go NEXT.
  • Every row adds up to 1 — from any state, something must happen next.
  • Its formal name is the transition matrix; think of it as the jump-chance table.

A two-state weather chain: the picture

  • Sunny stays Sunny 70% of days.
  • Sunny → Rainy with chance 0.3.
  • Rainy stays Rainy 60%.
  • Rainy → Sunny with chance 0.4.

Circles are states; arrows are hops, labelled with their chances.

The same weather chain as a table

Row i, column j = the chance of going to j when you are in i. Each row adds to 1.

Reading the table: rows are FROM, columns are TO

  • Two rules — every entry ≥ 0, every row sums to 1 — and P.sum(axis=1) is the one-second check. Columns need NOT add to 1.

CODE import numpy as np # rows = 'from' (today), columns = 'to' (tomorrow) P = np.array([[0.7, 0.3], # from Sunny [0.4, 0.6]]) # from Rainy print("Transition matrix P:") print(P) print("Sum of each row:", P.sum(axis=1)) OUTPUT ▸ Transition matrix P: [[0.7 0.3] [0.4 0.6]] Sum of each row: [1. 1.]

From 'which state' to 'how likely each state'

  • Often we do not know the exact state, only a chance for each.
  • Write that as a little row of numbers: e.g. 60% Sunny, 40% Rainy.
  • One day later, the new mix is that row 'run through' the jump-chance table.
  • Concretely: chance of Rainy tomorrow = (chance Sunny today × its chance of turning Rainy) + (chance Rainy today × its chance of staying Rainy).

Step the mix forward one day

  • One matrix multiply turns today's mix into tomorrow's — the whole engine of the chain.

CODE OUTPUT ▸ today = np.array([1.0, 0.0]) # certainly Sunny today # one step = run the mix through the table tomorrow = today @ P print("Today's chances :", today) print("Tomorrow's chances:", tomorrow.round(3)) Today's chances : [1. 0.] Tomorrow's chances: [0.7 0.3]

Multiply again and again — it settles, and forgets its start

  • n days ahead: run the mix through the table n times.
  • Start Sunny: the amber line falls.
  • Start Rainy: the blue line rises.
  • Both meet — the starting point is forgotten.

today, today × P, today × P², today × P³, ... — two very different starting points race to the same long-run mix.

The mix that stops changing

  • Suppose a mix is left unchanged by one more step.
  • Then it is unchanged by every step after that — it is a fixed point.
  • It is the long-run fraction of time the system spends in each state.
  • For the weather chain it is about 57% Sunny, 43% Rainy.

Finding the settled mix: roll it forward, simulate, or solve

  • Method 1: roll any start forward until it stops moving. Method 2: simulate and count time in each state. Method 3 (shown): one eigenvector solve, exact.

CODE # the settled mix is the left eigenvector of P for eigenvalue 1 values, vectors = np.linalg.eig(P.T) i = np.argmin(np.abs(values - 1.0)) mix = np.real(vectors[:, i]) # make it a set of chances: positive and adding to 1 mix = np.abs(mix); mix = mix / mix.sum() print("Settled mix:", mix.round(4)) print("Exact 4/7 and 3/7:", round(4/7, 4), round(3/7, 4)) OUTPUT ▸ Settled mix: [0.5714 0.4286] Exact 4/7 and 3/7: 0.5714 0.4286

Two conditions for a clean answer

  • All-connected: from any state you can eventually reach any other state.
  • No rigid cycle: the system is not locked into a fixed on-off-on-off rhythm.
  • With both, there is one settled mix, reached from any starting point.
  • Those two conditions are exactly why our weather chain behaved so nicely.
  • Stretch: mathematicians call these 'irreducible' and 'aperiodic'.

One connected system vs a one-way trap

Left: every state reachable → one settled mix. Right: a trap breaks it.

PageRank: the whole web as one giant chain

  • States = web pages.
  • Links = hops.
  • Settled mix = importance.
  • Billions of states, one answer.

A random surfer clicking links; the settled mix ranks pages by how often they are visited.

Customer state models in industry

  • Model each customer as Active → At-risk → Churned, hopping month to month.
  • The jump chances come from your own past data — just count the moves.
  • Useful answers: the steady mix of customers, expected lifetime, churn rate.
  • BFSI, telecom and e-commerce firms in India run exactly these models.

Reliability: an up-and-down machine

The same skeleton, relabelled: Working / At-risk / Failed.

Why this is worth an applied mathematician's time

  • One small table captures a whole random system's long-run behaviour.
  • The hard question 'what happens eventually?' becomes a short calculation.
  • It scales from 2 states to billions with the very same idea.
  • This is exactly the transferable, cross-industry skill the course keeps building.

The Markov-chain recipe — works every time

  • 1. Define the states: a short list of situations the system can be in.
  • 2. Estimate the jump chances from data: count the moves, row by row.
  • 3. Build the table P and check every row sums to 1.
  • 4. Compute the settled mix: roll forward, simulate, or solve.
  • 5. Interpret it in business terms: cost, churn, uptime, stock.

Back to the shop: how much stock to hold

  • Hold too little and you run out — lost sales and unhappy customers.
  • Hold too much and you tie up cash and warehouse space.
  • Demand each day is random, so you cannot simply plan it away.
  • We need a rule that balances the two costs under that uncertainty.

The (s, S) rule, in one line

  • Watch the stock each day.
  • When it falls to the reorder point s, order back up to the level S.
  • Otherwise, do nothing.
  • Two numbers, s and S, define the whole policy — our job is to choose them well.

The (s, S) sawtooth

Stock drifts down with demand, then jumps back up to S each time it hits s.

Random demand drives the whole thing

How many customers buy each day is a random draw, estimated from past sales.

One day of the simulation — and why it is a settling system too

  • The stock level is the state; each day's demand is the hop.
  • So the (s, S) shop is one of today's hopping systems.
  • Its settled mix is the long-run spread of stock on the shelf.
  • We read that off by simulating — same long-run idea.

See the demand, update stock, reorder if low, note the cost — then repeat.

Play the (s, S) rule out for 200 days

  • One seeded run of the policy returns its two headline numbers: cost and service.

CODE OUTPUT ▸ reorder_point, order_up_to_level = 20, 60 # the (s, S) policy number_of_days = 200 np.random.seed(0) # same demand every run # simulate_inventory plays the rule day by day (full code in the notebook) results = simulate_inventory(reorder_point, order_up_to_level) avg = results['total_cost'] / number_of_days print('Average cost per day :', round(avg, 2)) print('Service level :', round(results['service_level'] * 100, 1), '% of demand met') Average cost per day : 23.95 Service level : 97.9 % of demand met

What a good simulation report contains

  • Let it settle: ignore the first few days, then measure the long-run behaviour.
  • Service level: the fraction of demand met from stock (e.g. aim for 95%).
  • Average cost per day: holding + ordering + lost-sale penalties.
  • Report a range, not one number — it is a random simulation.

Hold too little or too much and it costs more

  • More stock → more holding cost.
  • More stock → fewer lost sales.
  • Total cost dips in the middle.
  • Simulate to find the dip.

Total cost dips in the middle — the cheapest restock level.

Policy tuning: sweep the candidates, pick the cheapest good one

  • Push S up: higher service, higher holding cost. Pull it down: cheaper, more stockouts. Goal: the cheapest policy that clears the 95% target — (20, 60).

CODE # demand estimated from a year of sales history (Poisson, seed 0) candidates = [(10,40),(15,50),(20,60),(25,70),(30,80),(35,90)] for s, S in candidates: cost, service = simulate_inventory(s, S) # 300-day play-out print('s =', s, ' S =', S, ' -> cost/day =', round(cost, 2), ' service =', round(service * 100, 1), '%') OUTPUT ▸ s = 10 S = 40 -> cost/day = 21.65 service = 81.7 % s = 15 S = 50 -> cost/day = 21.07 service = 89.9 % s = 20 S = 60 -> cost/day = 21.77 service = 97.7 % s = 25 S = 70 -> cost/day = 28.23 service = 99.6 % s = 30 S = 80 -> cost/day = 34.96 service = 99.8 % s = 35 S = 90 -> cost/day = 42.28 service = 100.0 %

When these models break

  • Avoid
  • Assuming memoryless when history really matters
  • Letting the jump chances quietly drift over time
  • Reading rows as columns (or the reverse)
  • Quoting the warm-up as the long-run answer
  • Tuning s and S by gut feel
  • Do instead
  • Check whether now really summarises the past
  • Re-estimate the table per season; flag the drift
  • Label 'from' rows and 'to' columns explicitly
  • Discard the warm-up; measure the long run
  • Sweep s and S by simulation and compare costs

Warm-up vs the long run — don't mix them up

  • The warm-up is the early, settling-in behaviour just after you start.
  • The long run is the steady regime the system relaxes into.
  • The settled mix and the service-level numbers describe the long run only.
  • Always let the system warm up before you start measuring.

Where a Markov system settles

  • Multiply the state by the transition matrix repeatedly and it converges to the stationary distribution.

CODE P = np.array([[0.9, 0.1], [0.5, 0.5]]) # each row sums to 1 state = np.array([1.0, 0.0]) for _ in range(50): state = state @ P # step forward until it stops changing print(state.round(4)) # the long-run (stationary) distribution

  • 1. Estimate the model from data: demand history, or counts of state-to-state moves.
  • 2. Build it: an (s, S) simulation, or a jump-chance table.
  • 3. For a chain, find the settled mix two ways — roll it forward and simulate — and check they agree.
  • 4. For inventory, sweep s and S; plot the cost trade-off and the service level.
  • 5. Recommend a policy or report the settled mix, with its cost and service level, and state your assumptions.

Key takeaways

  • The framework, in three words: States → Transitions → Long-run behaviour. Every chain you will ever build is these three.
  • A Markov chain is a few states plus a table of jump chances whose rows add to 1 — and you estimate those chances by counting moves in your own data.
  • Roll a starting mix forward and a well-behaved chain settles to one steady mix; find it by rolling forward or by simulating — and they should agree.
  • Some states are traps: then ask 'where do we end up?' instead of 'what's the mix?'.
  • The (s, S) shop is itself a settling system; tune s and S by simulation, not guesswork.
