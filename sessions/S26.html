The problem

A young analyst at a telecom company spends three weeks building a churn model. "Churn" means customers leaving. The model works well. It can find the customers most likely to cancel next month.

The analyst presents it to the senior managers. They start with an ROC curve, then show five slides of checks on the model. By slide ten the CEO is looking at their phone. The project is stopped. The company decides that "analytics does not work". That is unfair, but that is what happened in the meeting.

The model was good. The last step failed. The manager had about sixty seconds and was not interested in the mathematics. Nobody told them the one thing they needed: what should we do, and how much is it worth in rupees? This session is about that last step. For your career, these may be the most useful two hours of the course.

The one idea

Say the answer first, as a rupee amount, in one sentence a busy manager can act on. After that, give the reasons.

Think of a newspaper. The headline gives the whole story. You read the article only if you want the details. A good one-page summary is built the same way.

You did the work from the bottom up: data, method, checks, result. You present it from the top down: recommendation first, then the two or three reasons that matter, then the details for anyone who asks. This is called answer first, or BLUF: bottom line up front.

The key step is translation, which means changing model language into business language. "Churn is 4.1% and the model has good precision" means nothing to a manager who is not technical. Compare it with this: "If we call the 500 customers most likely to leave, we keep about Rs 12 lakh of revenue a year. Please approve the pilot." It is the same result, but now it is a decision and a rupee amount. This translation is the main skill today.

What you'll build today

  1. Start from a churn model's results, typed in as plain numbers. Turn the churn rate into a rupee amount, because that is the number a manager will listen to.
  2. Draw one clean summary chart. Write a one-page summary with the recommendation first, and display it as neat Markdown.
  3. Put a misleading chart next to an honest chart of the same data. The misleading one uses a cut-off axis or shows only a chosen part of the data.
  4. Replace a single-number forecast with a fan chart. A fan chart shows the likely range, instead of pretending we know the future exactly.

In the lab you put it all together. Take one project you built earlier in the course. Make a one-page summary and a three-slide presentation for a manager who is not technical. There is a fill-in template for the one-page summary in templates/one_pager.md.

You don't need to already know

Please read this if you are worried that this session is not for you. This session has almost no maths. Here, students from every background start at the same level. A commerce student who writes a clear, confident recommendation will present better than a maths PhD who hides the main point on slide ten. Clear communication is a skill anyone can learn. Many students who are strong in technical work lose marks here, in interviews and in their first jobs.

We teach everything today from the start, step by step:

  • how to turn a model number (a churn rate, a probability) into rupees,
  • how to arrange a recommendation so the answer comes first,
  • what makes a chart honest or misleading, and how to fix it,
  • how to show uncertainty as a range, without using the word "p-value".

By the end you can

  • Change a model number (a churn rate, a probability) into a rupee amount and a clear recommendation.
  • Write a one-page summary that gives the answer first (BLUF) and ends with a clear next step.

For the mathematically curious (optional)

You do not need this box for the lab. It is one way to think about today.

Today's "mathematical object" is an unusual one: the one-page summary, seen as a compression problem. Compression means making something much smaller. A trained model has thousands of parameters and a long story of checks. A busy manager can act on about one sentence. The one-page summary shrinks all of that into one decision, and it loses information on purpose.

As in any compression, the skill is choosing what to keep and what to drop. Keep the recommendation, the rupee amount, the one chart that supports it, and one honest risk. Move everything else to an appendix, or answer it when someone asks.

The fan chart in the second notebook also has some maths behind it. The band is about plus or minus two standard deviations. For a bell-shaped spread, this covers about 95% of likely outcomes. The band gets wider with time because uncertainty adds up month after month. You do not need this to present well. It only explains how the picture is made.

New words in this session

  • churn: a customer leaving or cancelling. The churn rate is the share of customers who leave in a period.
  • BLUF (bottom line up front): answer first. Give the recommendation, then the reasons.
  • one-page summary (executive summary): the whole decision on one page: the answer, a little evidence, one risk, and a next step.
  • pyramid principle: Barbara Minto's rule. The answer is at the top, the grouped reasons are below it, and the details are at the bottom.
  • truncated axis: a cut-off axis. The value axis does not start at zero, so a small change looks like a big one.
  • cherry-picking: showing only the part of the data that looks good, and hiding the rest.
  • fan chart: a forecast drawn as a band that gets wider into the future (a likely range), not as a single line.
  • calibrated confidence: saying how sure you are in a way that matches reality. If you say "70% sure", you should be right about 70% of the time.

Full definitions are in the glossary.

1 · Why explaining well matters

Why the person who can explain gets ahead

  • Many analysts can build a model. Very few can explain it to a CEO.
  • A person who can do both, build AND explain, becomes very valuable to the company.
  • Promotions, budgets and project approvals are decided by people who are not technical.
  • So this skill gives you an advantage that is hard for others to copy.

Your job is not to look clever. Your job is to help someone who will never see your code make the right decision.

2 · Know your listener

Different people need different answers

For more senior people, give less detail and more about the decision and the rupees.

The same result, three ways

One churn model, one result, explained for three different listeners.

3 · Answer first: the Pyramid Principle

Answer first, then group the reasons

  • Start with the one main answer.
  • Support it with 3 or 4 grouped reasons.
  • Put the data and charts under each reason.
  • Details are ready if asked, not shown first.

Barbara Minto's pyramid: the recommendation is at the top.

Give the conclusion first, not at the end

Top: the slow build-up, where people stop listening. Bottom: answer first.

4 · The one-page summary

Situation → Complication → Question → Answer

  • Situation: the background that everyone already agrees on.
  • Complication: what changed, or what problem has come up.
  • Question: the decision this problem forces us to make.
  • Answer: your recommendation, given first, in simple words, with the rupee amount.
  • Then: the evidence, on one page, ending with a next step.

A sample one-page summary

The decision and the rupee amount are in the top third of the page.

Print the one-page summary, top line first

  • The order is the message: the answer and the rupee amount come before any evidence.

CODE # one page, in the order a busy reader wants it one_pager = [ "ANSWER : approve a 3-month pilot on the 500 customers most likely to leave.", "IMPACT : protects about Rs 12 lakh of revenue per year.", "WHY : churn is 4.1% a month; past pilots kept ~30%.", "RISK : the 30% is an estimate; we will measure it.", "NEXT : retention team starts next month; review in 3 months.", ] for line in one_pager: print(line) OUTPUT ▸ ANSWER : approve a 3-month pilot on the 500 customers most likely to leave. IMPACT : protects about Rs 12 lakh of revenue per year. WHY : churn is 4.1% a month; past pilots kept ~30%. RISK : the 30% is an estimate; we will measure it. NEXT : retention team starts next month; review in 3 months.

What should be on the page

  • A title that gives the decision (a sentence, not a topic).
  • 'Cut churn 18%: approve the pilot', not 'Churn analysis'.
  • Only one chart, the one that supports the decision.
  • Three evidence points, one honest risk, one next step.
  • Everything else goes in the appendix, or in your spoken answers.

5 · Dashboards that give a clear message

Busy dashboard vs clear dashboard

Left: many numbers that look good. Right: one message, one decision.

Dashboard design

  • Avoid
  • A dozen unrelated boxes
  • Numbers that look good but nobody acts on
  • Too many colours, 3-D effects, crowded charts
  • No message, no recommended action
  • Do this
  • One main number, then the comparison
  • Numbers connected to a decision
  • Few colours; highlight the main point
  • A clear order: status → reason → what to do

6 · Explaining uncertainty

Talk about uncertainty without technical words

  • Do not say 'p-value' or 'confidence interval' to people who are not technical.
  • Use a range: 'We expect an increase of 8 to 12%, most likely about 10%.'
  • Use cases: best case, expected case, worst case.
  • Say how sure you are, and why, in one simple sentence.

Show a range, not one exact-looking number

One number hides the risk. A range (fan chart) shows it honestly.

Change one number into an honest range

  • The same model gives Rs 8 to 16 lakh: a range you can state honestly.

CODE flagged = 500 # customers the model marks as likely to leave value_yr = 8000 # Rs per customer kept, per year # try a careful and a hopeful success rate, in lakh protected low = flagged * 0.20 * value_yr / 1e5 high = flagged * 0.40 * value_yr / 1e5 print("Careful (20% kept): Rs", round(low, 1), "lakh") print("Hopeful (40% kept): Rs", round(high, 1), "lakh") OUTPUT ▸ Careful (20% kept): Rs 8.0 lakh Hopeful (40% kept): Rs 16.0 lakh

Use a familiar example

'70% chance of rain' is a kind of uncertainty everyone already trusts.

7 · From model output to decision

Model output → decision → ₹ impact

A probability is not an answer until it becomes an action with a rupee amount.

From a marked customer to rupees protected

  • Each line is one step: 500 marked → 150 kept → Rs 12 lakh a year.

CODE # from the model's results flagged = 500 # customers marked as likely to leave save_rate = 0.30 # past pilots kept ~30% of them value_yr = 8000 # Rs from each customer kept, per year retained = flagged * save_rate protected = retained * value_yr print("Customers expected to stay :", retained) print("Revenue protected per year : Rs", protected) print("Revenue protected per year : Rs", round(protected / 1e5, 1), "lakh") OUTPUT ▸ Customers expected to stay : 150.0 Revenue protected per year : Rs 1200000.0 Revenue protected per year : Rs 12.0 lakh

The "so what?" steps

Keep asking 'so what?' until you reach a decision and a rupee amount. If you cannot reach an action, you are not ready to present.

8 · Honesty and limits

Honest charts: where the axis starts

If the axis starts at 100, a rise of 3.5% looks like a huge jump.

The four numbers behind the 'huge jump' chart

  • The cut-off axis made a 3.5% rise look dramatic.

CODE quarters = ["Q1", "Q2", "Q3", "Q4"] sales = [101.0, 102.0, 103.0, 104.5] # sales in Rs lakh # the real change that the dramatic chart was hiding percent_change = (sales[3] - sales[0]) / sales[0] * 100 print("Sales (Rs lakh):", sales) print("Real change Q1 to Q4:", round(percent_change, 1), "%") OUTPUT ▸ Sales (Rs lakh): [101.0, 102.0, 103.0, 104.5] Real change Q1 to Q4: 3.5 %

Choosing only the good months

Six good months vs the full three years.

Talk about limits and still keep trust

  • Say the main limit clearly. If you hide it, you lose trust when it comes out later.
  • With every limit, say what you will do about it: 'The sample is small, so we will run a pilot first.'
  • Give a number for the risk where you can. Do not leave it vague.
  • Being confident and being honest go together. Accepting the limits builds trust.

Key takeaways

  • Explaining well is a career advantage: the analyst who turns results into decisions gets ahead.
  • Say the answer first, in rupees (BLUF / pyramid). Then the reasons. Details last.
  • Match the message to the listener. Start with what THEY care about.
  • One message per dashboard or one-page summary, ending in a decision and a ₹ amount.
  • Show uncertainty as a range, not one exact-looking number. Use familiar examples.
  • Be honest and correctly confident: name the limit, and say what you will do about it.
