The problem

A shop has thousands of customers and wants to stop treating them all the same. Some buy every week and spend a lot; they should get early access to a sale. Some have not been seen in months; they should get a win-back coupon. Some drift in the middle. The trouble is nobody has ever tagged a customer as "loyal" or "lapsing". There is no label column. All the shop has is a table of numbers: how recently each person bought, how often, and how much they spent.

The task today is to let those numbers sort the customers into a handful of natural groups on their own, then look at each group and give it a plain-English name. The same move groups localities by rent and amenities for a property site, or flags a payment that does not look like a customer's normal behaviour.

The one idea

Put every customer as a dot on a chart, positioned by their numbers. Customers who behave alike land near each other; very different customers land far apart. So the groups are already there in the picture as clumps of dots. All the computer does is find the clumps and draw a ring around each one. That is clustering: letting the data group itself, with no answer key.

There is one catch. A customer usually has more than two numbers, and you cannot draw a dot in five or fifty dimensions. So we use a second tool, PCA, to squash the many columns down to just two, keeping as much of the real spread as possible, so the clumps can be drawn on a flat chart and actually seen. Cluster to find the groups, PCA to look at them.

What you'll build today

  1. Make some blob-shaped data, group it with k-means, and plot the groups with their centres marked.
  2. Learn two honest ways to decide how many groups to ask for: the elbow and the silhouette.
  3. Make crescent-shaped data and watch DBSCAN find shapes that k-means gets wrong, while labelling stray points as noise.

For the mathematically curious (optional)

Everything above is enough to do the lab. This box is the extra depth for anyone who wants it, and none of it is needed to finish.

Today's two mathematical objects are distance and the eigen-decomposition, both doing a new job rather than being new maths.

Clustering is built entirely on a distance metric: a rule that turns two points into a single "how far apart" number. The default is the straight-line (Euclidean, or L2 norm) distance; Manhattan (L1) and cosine (angle) are alternatives for grid-like or direction-only data. K-means specifically minimises the within-cluster sum of squares, the total squared distance from each point to its own centre. That is the same least-squares spirit as Session 7, squared distances made small, but with no labels involved, and it settles to a good (not always globally best) solution, which is why we run it from several random starts.

PCA is literally the eigen-decomposition of the covariance matrix of the scaled data. Build the covariance matrix (how the columns vary together), take its eigenvectors and eigenvalues, and the eigenvectors are the principal axes: the directions along which the data spreads out most. The first principal component points along the biggest spread, the next is perpendicular to it, and so on. Each eigenvalue is the amount of variance along its axis, which is exactly what the scree plot shows. Notebook 2 computes this by hand with numpy and checks it matches scikit-learn to the decimal. This is the cleanest link between a linear-algebra course and machine learning, but it is a bonus, not a gate.

New words in this session

  • clustering — sorting unlabelled points into groups of similar ones.
  • k-means — a clustering method: pick a number of groups, then move each group's centre to the average of its points, repeating until nothing moves.
  • centroid — the centre point of a cluster, the average of its members.
  • DBSCAN — a clustering method that groups by crowding, finds any shape, and labels lonely points as noise.
  • elbow / silhouette — two ways to decide how many groups to use.
  • PCA — squashing many columns into a few that keep most of the spread, so you can plot or de-noise the data.
  • standardise — rescale every column to the same footing before measuring distance, so no single column dominates.

1 · The Problem

  • No labels — just a table of numbers

The Problem

  • A shop treats every customer the same: same offer, same mail, same treatment.
  • Some buy weekly and spend a lot; some have not been seen in months.
  • To treat them differently you need groups — but nobody ever tagged a customer.
  • No label column. Just numbers: how recently, how often, how much.
  • Today the numbers sort themselves into groups; we name each one.

The One Idea, in a Picture

  • Each dot is one customer.
  • Customers who behave alike land near each other.
  • Groups show up as clumps of dots.
  • The computer just rings each clump. That is clustering.

Alike customers land near each other — the groups are already there.

Supervised vs Unsupervised

The Building Block Is Distance

  • 'Similar' means 'nearby' — everything today runs on distance between dots.
  • How far apart are two customers, across all their columns?
  • Scale first: income runs to lakhs, visits to tens — one column dominates.
  • Standardise every column so each counts equally. Non-negotiable.

Rescale First, or the Biggest Column Wins

  • Raw numbers: income swamps age → the grouping is just income.
  • Rescaled: both count equally → the real groups appear.

Left alone, the biggest-numbered column decides every distance.

2 · K-Means

  • send each dot to the nearest centre, move the centre, repeat

The Core Idea

  • Decide how many groups you want: k.
  • Think of k 'team leaders' (centres) placed on the chart.
  • Each dot joins its nearest leader.
  • Each leader moves to the middle of its team.
  • Repeat until nobody changes team — the groups settle.
  • Analogy: two friends split a crowd by 'whoever is closest', then re-centre, again and again.

K-Means: How It Works, Step by Step

  • Start from rough centres (✖).
  • Send each dot to its nearest centre.
  • Move each centre to its crowd.
  • Repeat until the centres stop moving.

Start from rough centres (✖), then assign dots and move the centres until they settle.

What 'Best Grouping' Means

  • The number k-means shrinks is 'inertia': total squared distance from every dot to its own centre.
  • Same least-squares spirit as Session 7 — centres instead of a line, no labels.
  • k-means can settle in a good-but-not-best place, so run it from several starts.
  • J = Σₖ Σₓ∈Cₖ ‖ x − μₖ ‖²

Total within-cluster spread — scikit-learn calls this 'inertia_'

Choosing k: The Elbow

  • More groups always cut the spread.
  • The gains shrink past the real number.
  • The bend is the sweet spot.
  • Here: about 3 groups.

Plot the leftover spread against k; look for the bend.

Choosing k: The Silhouette

  • +1: snug, well inside its group.
  • Around 0: sitting on a border.
  • Below 0: probably mis-grouped.
  • Average over all dots; prefer the peak.

How snug each dot sits in its group versus the next one. Higher is better.

Silhouette Score, Step by Step

  • For one dot i, compare two distances.
  • a(i): average distance from i to every other dot in its own group — cohesion.
  • Smaller a(i) = snugger in its group.
  • b(i): the smallest average distance to any OTHER group — separation.
  • Larger b(i) = further from the nearest rival group.
  • Combine the two into one number between −1 and +1.
  • s(i) = [ b(i) − a(i) ] ⁄ max( a(i), b(i) )

Cohesion vs separation: near +1 snug, near −1 misplaced, 0 on the border — average over all dots

Reading the Score

  • a(i) = b(i) → s(i) = 0: the dot sits on the boundary; either group fits equally.
  • a(i) tiny, b(i) huge → s(i) near +1: unmistakably inside its own group.
  • a(i) huge, b(i) tiny → s(i) near −1: probably in the wrong group.
  • Average s(i) over every dot → one number to compare k values (higher = better separation).

K-Means — Hyperparameters to Set

  • k (n_clusters): the number of groups — your main choice (elbow + silhouette).
  • n_init: run 10 random starts, keep the best (default 10).
  • max_iter: how many send/move rounds (default 300 is usually fine).
  • random_state: a seed so your answer reproduces.
  • ALWAYS scale the features first.

K-Means — Strengths & Weaknesses

  • Strengths:
  • Fast, scales to big data (millions of rows), simple to explain.
  • Great when groups are round and similar in size.
  • Weaknesses:
  • You must choose k up front.
  • Assumes round, ball-like clusters — breaks on crescents.
  • Sensitive to scaling and to outliers.

3 · Hierarchical Clustering

  • a family tree you cut to pick groups

The Core Idea

  • Start with every dot as its own group.
  • Merge the two groups that are closest together.
  • Keep merging, nearest first, until one group remains.
  • The record of every merge is a family tree (a dendrogram).
  • No k up front — decide how many groups later, by cutting the tree.

Step 1 · A Tiny Worked Example: Measure Distance

Four points: A(1,1), B(2,1), C(8,8), D(9,8) — every cell is the straight-line distance between that pair.

Step 2–3 · Merge the Closest, Then Recompute

  • Smallest entry in the matrix is 1 → A and B are the closest pair.
  • Merge them into one group (A,B); C and D are also 1 apart → (C,D).
  • Now you need a distance between the two groups — but which one?
  • That choice is the linkage method: single, complete, average, or Ward.

Linkage Methods: How Far Apart Are Two Groups?

How It Works, Step by Step

  • Each dot starts as its own group.
  • Merge the two groups that are closest.
  • Repeat, nearest first.
  • In the end: one family tree.

Start alone, merge the nearest pair, keep merging until one tree.

Reading the Family Tree

  • Bottom: each leaf is one dot.
  • Height = distance when they merged.
  • Cut across a tall gap to pick groups.
  • Here: cut the dashed line → 3 groups.

Height = how far apart groups were when they merged.

Choosing How Many Groups

  • Cut the tree at a tall vertical gap — the natural number appears.
  • Want 5 groups? Cut one notch lower; 2 groups? Cut higher.
  • You can decide AFTER clustering — no need to fix k first.
  • Pair it with the silhouette if you want a second opinion.

Hierarchical vs K-Means, Side by Side

The One-Line Answer

  • Repeatedly merge the nearest clusters (agglomerative) — or split from one (divisive) — building a dendrogram.
  • Choose the number of clusters by cutting the dendrogram at a chosen height.
  • The linkage choice (single / complete / average / Ward) decides how cluster distance is measured.
  • Ward behaves like k-means: merge what least increases within-group spread.

Hierarchical — Hyperparameters to Set

  • n_clusters: how many groups — OR a distance_threshold to cut the tree.
  • linkage: how distance between groups is measured (ward / average / complete).
  • metric: how distance between points is measured (Euclidean default).
  • Cut the dendrogram where a tall gap appears.

Hierarchical — Strengths & Weaknesses

  • Strengths:
  • No k up front — decide later by cutting the tree.
  • A full family tree, great for small data.
  • Deterministic — no random starts.
  • Weaknesses:
  • O(n²) — slow beyond a few thousand rows.
  • Once merged, groups never split again.
  • Result depends on the linkage choice.

4 · DBSCAN

  • Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise.
  • let the crowding decide

The Core Idea

  • Group by crowding (density), not by shape or by k.
  • Where dots sit close together, that is a group.
  • Dots that sit far from any crowd are flagged as noise.
  • No need to choose the number of groups.
  • Finds any shape — crescents, rings, whatever the data makes.

How DBSCAN Decides: Core, Border, Noise

  • Core (★): ≥ min_samples neighbours inside the eps circle.
  • Border (▲): joins the group, but has fewer neighbours.
  • Noise (✕): too far from any crowd — left out.
  • Groups grow out of connected cores.

Within a radius eps, if a dot has enough neighbours it is a core point; the group grows from cores.

Why Bother? Shapes K-Means Cannot Do

  • K-means cuts straight through the crescents.
  • DBSCAN follows the crowding — any shape.
  • Strays are flagged as noise (−1).
  • Struggles only with mixed densities.

Left: k-means slices the crescents wrongly. Right: DBSCAN recovers the shapes.

DBSCAN — Hyperparameters to Set

  • eps: how close 'nearby' means (the crowding radius). The main knob.
  • min_samples: the smallest crowd that counts as a group (start ≈ 2 × columns).
  • metric: distance type (Euclidean default).
  • ALWAYS scale the features first.

Choosing eps (How to Set the Main Knob)

  • Too small → everything becomes noise; too large → one giant group.
  • Rule of thumb: start with eps near the typical distance to the k-th neighbour.
  • k-distance plot: sort each dot's distance to its k-th nearest neighbour and look for the bend.
  • Sweep a few eps values and pick the one where the groups look sensible.

DBSCAN — Strengths & Weaknesses

  • Strengths:
  • Finds any shape, not just round blobs.
  • No need to choose k.
  • Flags outliers as noise (−1).
  • Weaknesses:
  • Very sensitive to eps.
  • One global eps for all groups — mixed densities are hard.

5 · PCA

  • squash many columns to two, so you can SEE the groups

The Problem PCA Solves

  • Customers have many columns — you cannot draw a dot in fifty dimensions.
  • But columns usually move together (height and weight → one direction).
  • PCA squashes many columns into a few that keep most of the spread.
  • Then you can plot the groups and see them.
  • Cluster to group; PCA to look.

The Core Idea: Directions of Widest Spread

  • Find the direction the cloud spreads out most.
  • Then the next, at right angles.
  • Describe each dot by these two directions.
  • Two directions usually keep most of the spread.

One arrow along the widest spread, a second across it.

How It Works, Step by Step

  • Scale every column first (StandardScaler).
  • Find the direction of widest spread → PC1.
  • Then the next at right angles → PC2.
  • Describe each dot by its position along PC1 and PC2.

Left: the raw columns. Right: the same dots in the new PC coordinates.

The Maths, in One Line

  • Keep the direction the data spreads out most; then the next at right angles.
  • The new columns are called principal components.
  • For the curious: exactly the eigen-decomposition of the covariance matrix.
  • maximise Var(Xw) with ‖w‖ = 1

PCA = the eigen-decomposition of the covariance matrix (optional maths)

How Many Columns to Keep: The Scree Plot

  • The first new columns keep the most.
  • Watch the running-total line.
  • Here 2 columns ≈ 70%.
  • Stop where the bars go flat.

Bars: spread each new column keeps. Line: the running total.

PCA — Hyperparameters to Set

  • n_components: how many squashed columns to keep (read the scree).
  • whiten: optional — rescales the components to unit variance.
  • ALWAYS scale the features first (StandardScaler).
  • t-SNE / UMAP are for pictures only — never read distances off them.

PCA — Strengths & Weaknesses

  • Strengths:
  • Fewer columns: faster models, easier plotting, de-noises the data.
  • Removes redundant, correlated columns.
  • Easy visual check of whether groups exist.
  • Weaknesses:
  • New columns have no plain meaning — hard to explain.
  • Only catches straight-line structure.
  • Variance kept ≠ importance.

Key Takeaways

  • Unsupervised = finding structure when there are no labels.
  • Everything runs on distance — scale every column first.
  • K-means: fast, round groups, needs k (elbow + silhouette).
  • Hierarchical: no k up front, a family tree, slow on big data.
