Module 1 — Mathematical Modeling

S06 · Mathematics as a Language for Making Decisions

New here? Start with these

Short, picture-first notes for anything below that is unfamiliar. Ten minutes each is plenty. You do not need a maths or a coding background to follow this session.

  • What is a model
  • Glossary

The problem

It is six in the morning and the airport manager has to make one decision before the first flights: how many security counters to open today. Open too few and passengers miss flights and the queue spills out the door. Open too many and staff stand around, paid to do nothing. Nobody can know the exact right number. And yet a decision has to be made, today, and it will be better or worse depending on how the manager thinks about it.

That same shape of problem is everywhere. Amazon has to decide how many warehouses to build and where. Uber has to set a surge price this minute. A hospital has to decide how many ICU beds to keep free. A farmer has to guess this season's yield. A forest department has to estimate how much carbon a plantation will lock away. Different worlds, one move underneath all of them: turning a vague, messy decision into something you can actually reason about with numbers.

That move is called modelling, and it is the whole of today. This is a short, theory-only session — no code, no notebook. The point is not to build anything yet. The point is to change how you see the rest of the course.

The one idea

A model is a deliberate, useful simplification of reality. You throw away almost everything about the messy world and keep only the few things that matter for the one decision in front of you.

Think of a map. A map of Pune leaves out every tree and every pothole, and that is exactly why it helps you find your way. A model is a map of a problem. It is never the whole truth and it does not try to be. So we never ask "is this model true?" We ask "is it useful for the decision I have to make?" That single shift is the mindset of the whole field, and if you have only ever met mathematics as a search for the one correct answer, it takes a moment to get used to.

Every company is already doing this

The thing worth realising early is that modelling is not an academic exercise for mathematicians. It is what almost every organisation quietly does to make money and to make decisions. Google decides which advert to show you. Netflix decides which show to put in front of you. Tesla estimates how far you can go on the battery you have left. An insurance company sets your premium. An IPL team decides who to buy at the auction. Every one of those is a mathematical model doing its job, usually without anyone calling it that.

Four questions every model answers

The old way to teach this hands you a vocabulary list on day one — variables, parameters, constraints, objective, assumptions. Almost nobody remembers it. So we will use four plain questions instead, and they work on any problem in any field:

  1. What do I know? The data you already have.
  2. What do I want? A number, a yes-or-no, or a choice.
  3. What affects it? The few things that actually move the answer.
  4. How do I know I'm right? How you would check it against reality.

Answer those four for a problem and you have modelled it. Take the airport. You know the flights that morning, the passengers per flight and how fast one counter works. You want the number of counters to open. What affects it is simply whether passengers arrive faster than the open counters can clear them. And you know you got it right by asking what it costs you to be wrong — too few counters costs missed flights, too many costs idle staff. "Good" is not a perfect number; it is the best balance between those two mistakes.

One problem, many models

Here is the idea most worth carrying out of the session. The same problem, with the same data, becomes a completely different model depending on which question you ask of it. Stay at the airport and change only the question:

  • How long will I wait in security? That is a prediction.
  • Will my flight be delayed? That is a classification, a yes or no.
  • Which counter should each passenger go to? That is an optimisation.
  • How many counters will we need tomorrow? That is a forecast.
  • What happens if a storm hits Delhi? That is a simulation.

Beginners grab a technique they already know and bend the problem to fit it. Professionals start from the question and let it decide the model. Those five verbs — predict, classify, optimise, simulate — are also a map of the rest of this course. Every model you build this year is one of them.

The mathematics behind it, and the loop

Students often think "modelling" means frightening equations. It does not. It is a ladder that starts at arithmetic — counting passengers per flight is already mathematics — and climbs through algebra, probability, statistics, optimisation and machine learning. Each rung is the same idea with a little more power, and this course walks you up a few of them. Even the things everyone is nervous about, like ChatGPT or a self-driving car, sit on that same ladder: they are very large models answering exactly the four questions above from enormous amounts of data.

And you almost never get a model right the first time. Modelling is a loop, not a straight line: understand the real problem, simplify it hard, build something small, solve it, check it against reality, and improve. Your first model is meant to be rough. The gap between it and reality is what tells you how to make the next one better.

All models are wrong, some are useful

Every famous model you rely on is wrong somewhere. GPS occasionally tells people to drive into a lake. The weather app promises sunshine and it pours. Some COVID forecasts were badly off. ChatGPT states a wrong fact with complete confidence. This is not a scandal. It is the nature of a map. The statistician George Box put the whole philosophy in eight words:

All models are wrong, but some are useful.

A good modeller is not someone who is never wrong. It is someone who knows where their model is wrong and uses it anyway, with their eyes open.

Why there is no notebook today

Every other session in this course is hands-on and runnable. This one is deliberately not. Before you learn how to fit a line or train a classifier, it is worth spending twenty minutes on why any of it exists. From next session onward we start building — and because you have this frame, regression, classification, optimisation and simulation will each land as one more way to speak the same language, instead of as four unrelated tricks.

By the end you can

  • Explain what a model is in one sentence: a useful simplification, a map of a problem, judged by whether it helps you decide rather than by whether it is "true".
  • Ask the four questions of any real problem: what do I know, what do I want, what affects it, how would I check it.
  • See why the same situation becomes a prediction, a classification, an optimisation or a simulation depending only on the question asked.
  • Recognise that almost every important decision a company makes is a model, and that this whole course is learning to build them.

For the mathematically curious (optional)

None of this is needed to follow the session; it is only extra depth for those who want the formal version. The airport example is a queue in disguise. If passengers arrive at average rate λ and one counter serves at average rate μ, then c counters keep the queue stable exactly when λ < c·μ; push arrivals past what the open counters can serve and the expected queue grows without bound, no matter how clever the arithmetic. That single inequality, not a precise headcount, is often the useful answer a manager needs.

More generally, a model has a set of decision variables, an objective function you are trying to make best, and a feasible set carved out by the constraints. The four questions above are that structure in plain words: "what do I want" names the objective, "what do I know" and "what affects it" name the data and the variables, and "how do I know I'm right" is validation. Later sessions turn each verb — prediction, classification, optimisation, simulation — into its own machinery, but the judgement you practise today is what decides which of those tools to reach for.

New words in this session

  • model — a deliberate, useful simplification of a real problem, kept only as detailed as the decision needs.
  • prediction — a model whose answer is a number (how long, how much, how many).
  • classification — a model whose answer is a category or a yes/no.
  • optimisation — a model that chooses the best option out of many.
  • simulation — running a model step by step to see what would happen.
  • assumption — a simplification you chose on purpose, written down so it can be argued with and tested.

Full definitions are in the glossary.
