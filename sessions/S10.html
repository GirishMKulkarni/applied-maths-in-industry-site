S10 · Flowcharts That Learn — Trees, Forests and Boosting

The problem

A lending app on your phone offers a small personal loan and promises a decision in ten seconds. Behind that promise is a model. It has seen lakhs of past borrowers, who repaid and who did not, and for each new applicant it predicts: will this person repay, or default? Approve the ones likely to repay, decline the rest.

Two things make this hard. The pattern is not a straight line: a young applicant with a modest income but a spotless record can be safer than an older, richer one who is drowning in loans. And the decision affects real lives, so if the model quietly treats one group of people worse than another, that is not a bug to shrug off, it is the whole story. Today you build a model for exactly this, and then you audit it for fairness.

The one idea

Start with one picture: a flowchart of yes/no questions. "Is the credit score above 650? If yes, is income above ₹30,000?" and so on, until you reach a verdict. That flowchart is a decision tree, and the computer learns the questions from past data. One tree is easy to read but a bit wobbly: change the data slightly and it draws a different flowchart.

So we use many trees instead, in two clever ways.

  • Ask many trees and take a vote. Grow hundreds of slightly different trees and let them vote on each applicant. The crowd is steadier than any one tree. This is a Random Forest.
  • Let each new tree fix the last one's mistakes. Build trees one after another, where every new tree focuses on the applicants the earlier trees got wrong. This is boosting, and XGBoost is the famous tool that does it.

That is the entire session in three sentences: one flowchart, a voting crowd of flowcharts, and a relay of flowcharts each correcting the one before.

What you'll build today

  1. Grow a single decision tree on a small loan dataset, draw it as a flowchart, and watch it start memorising the data when you let it grow too deep.
  2. See overfitting happen live: as the tree is allowed more questions, its score on data it has seen keeps rising while its score on new people peaks and falls.
  3. On a harder dataset, beat the single tree with a Random Forest and with XGBoost, and read off which inputs mattered most.
  4. Build a credit-default classifier and run a bias audit: check whether it approves one group less often than another, and whether it makes more mistakes for one group, then write down honestly what you found.

For the mathematically curious (optional)

Everything above is enough to do the lab. This box is the extra depth for those who want it, and none of it is needed to finish.

When a tree splits a group, it wants each side to be pure, all one class. It measures mixed-ness with an impurity number. Two standard choices: Gini impurity, 1 − (p² + q²), and entropy, borrowed from information theory, −p·log₂p − q·log₂q, where p and q are the fractions of the two classes. Both are 0 for a pure group and largest at a 50/50 mix. The drop in impurity after a split, weighted by group size, is the information gain, and the tree greedily picks the split that maximises it.

Boosting has a tidy formal shape too. The final prediction is a sum of many small trees, F(x) = Σ f_k(x) — an additive model — where each new tree f_k is fitted to the leftover errors (the residuals, or more precisely the gradient of the loss) of the trees so far. That is where the name gradient boosting comes from. The notebooks show the picture; ISLR ch. 8 has the full derivation.

New words in this session

  • decision tree — a model shaped like a flowchart of yes/no questions.
  • impurity — a number for how mixed a group of labels is; 0 means all one class.
  • information gain — how much a question lowered the impurity; the tree picks the biggest.
  • overfitting — learning the noise in the training data, so the model does worse on new data.
  • bagging / Random Forest — grow many trees on random samples and let them vote.
  • boosting / XGBoost — grow trees in sequence, each fixing the last one's mistakes.
  • feature importance — how much each input contributed to the model's decisions.
  • bias audit — checking whether a model treats groups of people differently.

Full definitions are in the glossary.

What is a decision tree?

2 · How a Tree Picks Its Questions

  • it chooses the question that tidies the groups

A good question tidies each side

  • 'Impurity' scores how mixed a group is.
  • The drop in impurity after a question is the 'gain'.
  • The tree picks the question with the biggest gain.
  • Pure group → 0; a 50/50 mix → most mixed-up.

One messy group becomes two tidier ones — that tidy-up is the gain.

How mixed? Two recipes

  • Gini impurity: 1 − Σ pᵢ² (scikit-learn's default).
  • Entropy: − Σ pᵢ log₂ pᵢ (borrowed from information theory).
  • Both score 0 for a pure group, highest at 50/50.
  • The maths is optional today — the picture is the point.

Gini and entropy track each other closely; either picks almost the same questions.

3 · When a Tree Overfits

  • left alone, it memorises the noise

A deep tree memorises the noise

  • Left alone, a tree fits every single point.
  • It aces studied data, stumbles on new people.
  • Hold it back: cap max_depth, or prune.
  • Always judge on the held-out test set.

Left: a jagged boundary around every point. Right: shallow and sensible.

4 · Many Trees Beat One

  • a voting crowd, then a correcting relay

Why one tree isn't enough

  • One tree is easy to read but wobbly.
  • Change a little data → a different tree.
  • So grow many trees and combine them.
  • Random Forest = many trees, each on a random sample AND a random subset of the inputs, then a majority vote.

Bagging: resample the data, grow many trees, combine their votes.

Random Forest: many trees, then a vote

  • A Random Forest is a voting crowd of trees.
  • Each tree is slightly different, so their mistakes differ.
  • The vote is steadier than any one tree.
  • Very hard to overfit — a safe default baseline.

Each tree sees a random sample of people AND a random subset of their details.

5 · Boosting and XGBoost

  • a relay of trees, each fixing the last

Boosting fixes the leftovers

  • Boosted trees are grown one after another.
  • Every new tree focuses on the cases the earlier ones got wrong.
  • The final prediction is a sum of many small corrections.
  • XGBoost is the famous, fast tool that does this.

Each new tree corrects the errors the earlier ones left behind.

Random Forest vs Boosting — when to reach for which

  • Random Forest: trees in parallel, then a vote — steadies a wobbly model.
  • Boosting/XGBoost: trees in a relay, each corrects — sharpens accuracy.
  • Forest is a safe default; XGBoost chases the last points of accuracy.
  • Watch overfitting with boosting — stop early.

The boosted model is a running sum: a first guess plus many small corrections.

6 · Which Inputs Mattered?

  • and why that is not proof of cause

Which inputs drove the decision?

  • Trees and forests report how much each input mattered.
  • High importance = the split relied on it often.
  • Correlation is not causation — a proxy can stand in for a sensitive attribute.
  • So importance guides, but never excuses, a decision.

Rank inputs by how much they helped — but that is not proof of cause.

7 · Responsible AI: the Bias Audit

  • built into the project, not bolted on afterwards

Why fairness matters in lending

  • A credit model shapes real lives.
  • Rejecting one group far more often is discrimination.
  • RBI and Indian law expect fair, explainable decisions.
  • So audit outcomes across groups and document it.

Compare approval rates across groups; large gaps are a red flag.

Key takeaways

  • A decision tree is a learned flowchart; its questions tidy the groups.
  • Left alone a tree memorises — hold it back, and prefer a team of trees.
  • Random Forest = a voting crowd (steadies); boosting = a correcting relay (sharpens).
  • Feature importance says which inputs mattered — not proof of cause.
  • Responsible AI is the job: audit outcomes across groups and document it.
