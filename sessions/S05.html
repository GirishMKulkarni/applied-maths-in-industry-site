The problem

You have a table. It might be a year of sales, a month of delivery times, or a spreadsheet a colleague emailed you with no explanation. Somewhere in those rows is something worth knowing — which product is slipping, which month broke the trend, which two numbers move together. You cannot find it by scrolling, and even once you have found it, a column of numbers will not convince anybody.

So you draw it. But a chart drawn badly is worse than no chart: it buries the pattern, or it makes a small difference look like a crisis. The skill is not learning chart syntax. It is choosing the picture that answers the question you actually asked, and drawing it honestly.

The one idea

Every seaborn chart is the same function call.

You hand it a table, say which column runs across, which runs up, and which one decides the colour. Change the verb and you get a different chart.

sns.scatterplot(data=tips, x="total_bill", y="tip", hue="sex")
sns.barplot(data=tips,     x="day",        y="tip", hue="sex")
sns.boxplot(data=tips,     x="day",        y="tip", hue="sex")

Three different charts, one sentence. That is why seaborn is worth an evening: you learn data, x, y and hue once, and ten chart types come free. The rest of today is knowing which verb answers which question.

The ten functions

Do two numbers move together? scatterplot
What happened over time? lineplot
How do categories compare? barplot

The first five carry most of the work. Learn those before the rest.

For the mathematically curious (optional)

Everything above is enough to do the lab. This box is extra depth, and none of it is needed to finish.

A table with n numeric columns is a set of points in n-dimensional space: one point per row, one axis per column. The iris table is 150 points in four dimensions. You cannot see four dimensions, and a page has only two, so every chart you draw is a projection — a map from that high-dimensional space down to the two directions the eye can read.

Choosing a chart is choosing that projection. A scatter of petal length against petal width keeps two coordinates and discards the other two. A hue smuggles a third back in as colour. A pairplot is the honest admission that you do not yet know which projection matters, so it draws every two-dimensional one at once and lets you pick.

That is a real choice with real consequences. Project badly and three clearly separate groups collapse into one blob; project well and the structure is obvious at a glance — which is exactly what happens when you compare the sepal panels of the iris pair plot with the petal ones. Today you choose the projection by hand, by picking columns. In Session 11 (principal component analysis) we compute the projection that preserves as much of the spread as possible, so the machine picks it for you. The pairplot you draw today is the picture that PCA later automates.

New words in this session

  • distribution — the shape of a single column of numbers: where they cluster and how spread out they are.
  • histogram — a chart that chops one column into bins and counts each.
  • median — the middle value; half the data sits above it, half below.
  • outlier — a value far from the rest, worth asking about rather than deleting.
  • correlation — a number from −1 to +1 for how strongly two columns move together in a straight line.
  • skew — a distribution with a long tail on one side.
  • facet — repeating the same chart once per category, side by side.
  • axes object — matplotlib's name for a single panel of a figure; the thing you pass as ax= to draw into a chosen panel.

Full definitions are in the glossary.

Optional coda — SQL and DuckDB

If there is time at the end of the session, we look briefly at where the table came from. In industry, data usually lives in a database, and SQL is the small language for asking it a precise question. DuckDB runs SQL directly on a pandas DataFrame with no database to install:

import duckdb
duckdb.sql("SELECT day, AVG(tip) FROM tips GROUP BY day").df()

That returns a DataFrame, which you then chart exactly as above. Nothing in today's notebooks or the deliverable depends on it — it is a pointer to what a data job looks like, not something you are examined on.
