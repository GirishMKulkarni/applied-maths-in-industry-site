The problem

A hostel mess serves 400 lunches a day. The manager wants each plate as cheap as possible, but every plate must give a student at least 30 g of protein and 900 calories. Rice is cheap but low in protein; dal has protein but costs more. How many katoris of each? Then the warden asks for iron as well, and two more foods, curd and palak, come on the menu.

Prediction tells you what may happen. Optimisation tells you what to do. Choosing the best plan under limits is one of the most valuable things a numbers person does in industry, and today you learn to hand that choice to the computer.

The one idea

Every linear program has the same pieces. Decision variables: what you choose (katoris of each food). An objective: the one number you push as low or as high as it goes (the cost of the plate). Constraints: the rules you must obey (protein at least 30 g, calories at least 900). And non-negativity: no negative katoris.

There is a picture underneath it. With two foods, each rule is a straight line, and the plates that pass every rule form a shaded shape. The cheapest plate is never in the middle of that shape; it sits at a corner, here 3 katoris of rice and 2 of dal for ₹31. A solver finds that corner for you, even when there are thousands of foods and you can no longer draw the picture.

We use the same thali all session: two foods for the picture, four foods with an iron rule for the full model (3 rice, 1.75 dal, 0.75 palak, ₹35), and the same plate again when we break the model on purpose. The second example is a warehouse-to-city delivery plan: different industry, same mathematics.

What you'll build today

  1. Check some plates by hand, draw the two-food thali, shade the allowed plates, price the corners, and watch PuLP land on the same corner.
  2. Write the four-food thali with an iron rule in PuLP, solve it with the free CBC solver, and check the plate by hand. Then add a curd rule and a budget the plate cannot meet, and read each verdict.

For the mathematically curious (optional)

Everything above is enough to do the lab. This box is the extra depth for those who want it, and none of it is needed to finish.

Today's mathematical object is the convex polyhedron. Each constraint aᵀx ≤ b is a half-space; the feasible set is the intersection of finitely many half-spaces, which is a convex polyhedron (in two dimensions, the convex polygon you shaded by hand). "Convex" means the segment between any two feasible points stays feasible, so the region has no dents. Because a linear objective cᵀx has no interior stationary point, its optimum over a bounded polyhedron is attained at a vertex (a corner where enough constraints are tight). That is the corner-point theorem, and it is the reason the simplex method can walk from corner to corner and terminate.

The second half is duality. Every linear program (the primal) has a mirror-image dual: if the primal chooses a plan to minimise cost subject to meeting requirements, the dual prices each requirement, and strong duality says the two problems meet at exactly the same optimal value. Those dual prices are the shadow prices you meet in the Stretch cells: the shadow price of a constraint is how much the best objective would move if you loosened that constraint by one unit. A tight constraint has a nonzero shadow price and is the bottleneck; a slack one has shadow price zero. This duality is a genuine highlight of the subject, but here it is a bonus, not a gate.

New words in this session

  • optimization — choosing the best plan a set of limits allows.
  • linear program (LP) — a plan where the objective and every rule are plain weighted sums, no squares or curves.
  • decision variable — one of the quantities you get to choose.
  • objective — the single number you push as low or as high as it will go.
  • constraint — a rule the plan must obey.
  • feasible region — the set of all plans that obey every constraint at once.
  • solver — the tool (here CBC, inside PuLP) that searches for the best plan.
  • infeasible — no plan obeys every constraint; the rules contradict each other.
  • unbounded — the objective can improve forever; usually a missing constraint.
  • shadow price — how much the best answer moves if you loosen one constraint by one unit.

Full definitions are in the glossary.

APPLICATIONS OF MATHEMATICS IN INDUSTRY Module 4 — Optimization & Operations Research · Session S20 · 2 hours Module 4 begins: the optimisation block

The problem: the cheapest lunch that still feeds a student

  • A hostel mess serves 400 lunches a day. The manager wants each plate as cheap as possible.
  • But every plate must give at least 30 g of protein and 900 calories.
  • Rice is cheap but low in protein. Dal has protein but costs more. How many katoris of each?
  • Prediction tells you what may happen. Optimisation tells you what to DO.

Every linear program has three pieces

The thali, written as a linear program

  • Choose r and d to make the cost smallest while every rule holds.
  • Protein rule: each katori of rice gives 4 g, each katori of dal 9 g.
  • Calorie rule: rice 200 per katori, dal 150.
  • The same four lines work for 2 foods or 2,000: only the lists get longer.

What 'linear' means

  • Linear: multiply by a number, then add
  • 5r + 8d (cost)
  • 4r + 9d ≥ 30 (protein)
  • r ≥ 0
  • Straight lines on a graph. Solvers handle millions of these.
  • Not linear: anything that bends
  • r × d (two choices multiplied)
  • r² (a choice squared)
  • cost ÷ protein (a ratio of choices)
  • a bulk discount after 10 kg (a kink)
  • Curves are S22; whole numbers are S21.

Check some plates by hand

Each rule is a straight boundary

  • Plot every plate as a point: rice across, dal up.
  • 4r + 9d = 30 is a straight line: plates with exactly 30 g.
  • Above it: enough protein. Below it: too little.
  • (2, 2) fails. (3, 2) and (5, 3) pass, as in the table.

Together the rules make the allowed region

  • Keep only the plates that pass BOTH rules: the shaded region.
  • Its corners are where two boundaries cross.
  • (3, 2) is where protein and calories are both exactly met.
  • Region = every allowed plan. The name: feasible region.

The cheapest plan is always at a corner

  • Every dashed line is one price: all plates costing exactly ₹49, ₹43…
  • Slide the line towards cheaper until it last touches the region.
  • It last touches at a corner: 3 rice + 2 dal, ₹31.
  • A straight goal never has a best point in the middle, so we only check corners.

Score the three corners

  • Three corners, three prices. The cheapest corner is the cheapest plate of all: ₹31, the same as our best row in the table.

CODE corners = [(7.5, 0), (3, 2), (0, 6)] # (rice, dal) for r, d in corners: print(r, 'rice +', d, 'dal costs Rs', 5*r + 8*d) OUTPUT ▸ 7.5 rice + 0 dal costs Rs 37.5 3 rice + 2 dal costs Rs 31 0 rice + 6 dal costs Rs 48

Hand the same lunch to a solver: PuLP

  • The solver finds the same corner we found by hand: 3 rice, 2 dal, ₹31.

CODE import pulp model = pulp.LpProblem("lunch_thali", pulp.LpMinimize) r = pulp.LpVariable("rice", lowBound=0) d = pulp.LpVariable("dal", lowBound=0) model += 5*r + 8*d # cost model += 4*r + 9*d >= 30 # protein model += 200*r + 150*d >= 900 # calories model.solve(pulp.PULP_CBC_CMD(msg=0)) print("status:", pulp.LpStatus[model.status]) print("rice:", r.varValue, " dal:", d.varValue) print("cost: Rs", pulp.value(model.objective)) OUTPUT ▸ status: Optimal rice: 3.0 dal: 2.0 cost: Rs 31.0

The PuLP model, line by line

Your turn first: the warden adds iron

  • Our ₹31 plate gives only 3 × 0.5 + 2 × 2 = 5.5 mg of iron. The warden wants at least 8 mg.
  • Two more foods are on offer: curd and palak sabzi.
  • Before any code, answer on paper:
  • 1. Decision variables: what do we choose now?
  • 2. Objective: which number, and up or down?
  • 3. Constraints: how many rules, and which way do they point?
  • 4. Non-negativity: which choices must be ≥ 0?

The four foods, per katori

The checklist, answered

The full thali in PuLP

  • lpSum adds up price × katoris over every food: the objective row of the checklist.

CODE # foods, price, protein, calories, iron: the table as dictionaries thali = pulp.LpProblem("full_thali", pulp.LpMinimize) k = {f: pulp.LpVariable(f, lowBound=0) for f in foods} # katoris of each thali += pulp.lpSum(price[f] * k[f] for f in foods) thali += pulp.lpSum(protein[f] * k[f] for f in foods) >= 30 thali += pulp.lpSum(calories[f] * k[f] for f in foods) >= 900 thali += pulp.lpSum(iron[f] * k[f] for f in foods) >= 8 print(len(k), "variables,", len(thali.constraints), "constraints") OUTPUT ▸ 4 variables, 3 constraints

Solve it and read the plate

  • Iron costs ₹4 a plate: the solver keeps 3 rice, swaps a quarter katori of dal for palak, and skips curd.

CODE thali.solve(pulp.PULP_CBC_CMD(msg=0)) print("status:", pulp.LpStatus[thali.status]) for f in foods: print(f" {f:6s} {k[f].varValue:5.2f} katoris") print("cost: Rs", pulp.value(thali.objective)) OUTPUT ▸ status: Optimal rice 3.00 katoris dal 1.75 katoris curd 0.00 katoris palak 0.75 katoris cost: Rs 35.0

Worked example 2: move stock from warehouses to cities

  • A sale is on. Three warehouses hold stock; three cities need it.
  • Choices: truckloads on each of the 9 routes.
  • Objective: total freight bill, smallest.
  • Rules: ship no more than a warehouse holds; every city gets what it needs.

The freight costs (₹ thousand per truckload)

The delivery plan in PuLP

  • Two loops, two families of rules: one '≤ supply' per warehouse, one '≥ demand' per city.

CODE plan = pulp.LpProblem("move_stock", pulp.LpMinimize) ship = {(w, c): pulp.LpVariable(f"{w}_to_{c}", lowBound=0) for w in warehouses for c in cities} # 9 routes plan += pulp.lpSum(cost[w][c] * ship[w, c] for (w, c) in ship) for w in warehouses: # supply: ship out no more than you hold plan += pulp.lpSum(ship[w, c] for c in cities) <= supply[w] for c in cities: # demand: every city gets what it needs plan += pulp.lpSum(ship[w, c] for w in warehouses) >= demand[c] print(len(ship), "variables,", len(plan.constraints), "constraints") OUTPUT ▸ 9 variables, 6 constraints

Solve it and read the plan

  • Five of the nine routes carry stock, for a bill of ₹4.05 lakh. No warehouse is over-used and no city is short.

CODE plan.solve(pulp.PULP_CBC_CMD(msg=0)) print("status:", pulp.LpStatus[plan.status]) for (w, c), trucks in ship.items(): if trucks.varValue > 0: print(f" {w:8s} -> {c:6s} {trucks.varValue:3.0f} truckloads") print("bill: Rs", pulp.value(plan.objective), "thousand") OUTPUT ▸ status: Optimal Bhiwandi -> Mumbai 20 truckloads Bhiwandi -> Pune 10 truckloads Hosur -> Pune 15 truckloads Hosur -> Nagpur 10 truckloads Kolkata -> Nagpur 20 truckloads bill: Rs 405.0 thousand

Who solves problems like these

Two problems, one recipe

  • What changes
  • Thali: choices are katoris of food
  • Stock: choices are truckloads on routes
  • Thali rules: nutrients, all 'at least'
  • Stock rules: supply 'at most', demand 'at least'
  • What stays the same
  • Name the choices, each ≥ 0
  • Write ONE number and say min or max
  • One line per rule
  • Solve, read the verdict, then the plan
  • Different industries. Same mathematics.

Three things a solver can tell you

Same lunch thali each time. Only the rules or the direction changed.

Break the model on purpose

  • Both verdicts are true statements about the model, not crashes. Read them before any number.

CODE # 1. add a budget the plate can't meet model += 5*r + 8*d <= 25, "budget" model.solve(pulp.PULP_CBC_CMD(msg=0)) print("Rs 25 budget: ", pulp.LpStatus[model.status]) # 2. maximise protein with no budget at all greedy = pulp.LpProblem("more_protein", pulp.LpMaximize) greedy += 4*r + 9*d greedy += 200*r + 150*d >= 900 greedy.solve(pulp.PULP_CBC_CMD(msg=0)) print("max protein, no cap: ", pulp.LpStatus[greedy.status]) OUTPUT ▸ Rs 25 budget: Infeasible max protein, no cap: Unbounded

Reading the verdict

  • Infeasible
  • Unbounded
  • The rules contradict each other
  • e.g. a ₹25 budget, but the cheapest nutritious plate is ₹31
  • e.g. cities need 80 truckloads, warehouses hold 75
  • often a typo: ≥ where you meant ≤, or a wrong unit
  • fix: relax a rule, or correct the data
  • The number can improve forever
  • e.g. maximise protein with no budget
  • almost always a MISSING rule
  • or a forgotten lowBound=0
  • fix: add the real-world limit you left out

The art of mathematical modelling

Manufacturing: chairs or tables?

  • “A factory makes chairs and tables. How many of each?”
  • x = chairs, y = tables. Objective: profit. Constraints: wood and labour.
  • Linear, exactly like the thali, with ≤ instead of ≥.
  • Product-mix problems like this were among the first LPs solved commercially.

Hospital staffing: nurses per shift

  • “How many nurses should work each shift?”
  • x₁, x₂, x₃ = nurses on morning, evening, night; w = pay per nurse per shift.
  • Constraints: the minimum cover each shift needs.
  • Linear, but nurses come whole: that makes it an S21 problem.

Uber and Swiggy: which driver takes which order?

  • “Which driver should pick up which customer?”
  • xᵢⱼ = 1 if driver i is assigned customer j. One driver per order, one order per driver.
  • This is the warehouse problem with every supply and demand equal to 1.
  • Like the transport plan, the LP comes out in whole numbers on its own.

Ecology: where should we plant trees?

  • “I have ₹10 lakh for restoration. Where should I plant?”
  • x₁, x₂, x₃ = saplings in each area; c = carbon per sapling, p = cost per sapling.
  • Or maximise a biodiversity score instead: the objective is a choice you make.
  • Linear as long as each sapling adds a fixed amount; crowding would bend it.

Finance: how to split ₹10 lakh

  • “How much should I put in Reliance, TCS, Infosys and HDFC Bank?”
  • xᵢ = fraction of the money in stock i; rᵢ = its expected return.
  • Return and budget are linear. Risk is not: σ_p multiplies the x's together.
  • Same three pieces as S16's portfolio. The curve is why it waits for S22.

AI: how does ChatGPT learn?

  • Training an AI model is one giant optimisation problem.
  • Decision variables: every weight in the network.
  • Objective: the average prediction error on the training text.
  • Far from linear, and too big for a solver: gradient descent from S07 does it.

Same three pieces, everywhere

Optimisation is not a topic. It is a way of thinking.

  • Whenever you can name the variables, the objective and the constraints, mathematics can help make the decision.

Common traps

  • Trap
  • Wrong units (grams vs kg, ₹ vs ₹ thousand)
  • A missing constraint
  • ≥ where you meant ≤ (or = where you meant ≤)
  • Forgetting non-negativity
  • Sneaking in a curve: r × d, r², a ratio
  • Trusting the solver without checking
  • Habit
  • Write the unit next to every number
  • List every real limit before you code
  • Read each rule aloud: 'at least' or 'at most'?
  • lowBound=0 on every variable that can't go negative
  • Keep it multiply-and-add; curves wait for S22
  • Print the status; check one rule by hand

What to remember

  • LP = decision variables + one objective + constraints (+ non-negativity).
  • The feasible region is every plan that obeys all the rules.
  • The best plan sits at a corner, so a solver only has to search corners.
  • PuLP + the free CBC solver turn the three pieces into an answer in a few lines.
  • Read the verdict first: Optimal, Infeasible or Unbounded.
  • Modelling = three questions: what can I control, what am I after, what limits me?
  • Diets, deliveries, nurses, trees, portfolios, AI: different industries, same thinking.
