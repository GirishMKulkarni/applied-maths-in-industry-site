P3 · The Lot That Fails Final Test

The situation

A semiconductor fab passes wafers through several hundred process steps. At the end of the line, every lot goes through a final electrical test. A lot that fails is scrapped. It is not repaired. It is thrown away. One scrapped lot is worth more than a new engineer's yearly salary. And by the time the test shows the failure, six weeks of plant time have already been spent on that lot.

The line has sensors everywhere. They record temperatures, pressures, flows, gas concentrations and timings, five hundred and ninety signals in all, for every lot. The process engineers strongly suspect that the failures can be seen in this data long before final test. But nobody has been able to prove it, because nobody can study five hundred and ninety charts.

The plant manager wants three things.

First, a warning before final test that a lot is likely to fail. Then the lot can be taken out and inspected instead of being finished.

Second, a short list of the sensors that really matter. It must be small enough to fit on the screen above the line. Five hundred and ninety sensors cannot fit on one dashboard.

Third, an answer to a question the engineers have been arguing about. One particular measurement needs the tool to be stopped, and it takes twenty minutes. Can it be calculated from the sensors that cost nothing to read?

The three questions

As the plant asked them.

1. "Before this lot goes to final test, can you tell me whether it is going to fail?"

2. "Which of these five hundred and ninety sensors actually deserve a place on the screen above the line?"

3. "That measurement costs us twenty minutes and a tool stop. Can you calculate it from the sensors that are free?"

Question 1 is a binary classification problem, and it is the hardest one in the six briefs, because only 104 of the 1,567 lots failed. Start with logistic regression from Session 9. Your first model will probably predict "pass" for every single lot and still seem to do well. This is not a bug in your code. It is the main lesson of this project. Find out what class_weight="balanced" does before you decide something is broken.

Question 2 uses Session 11 in two ways. PCA will reduce the 590 sensors to a few components and tell you how much of the variation you kept. Separately, the tree models from Session 10 will rank the sensors by how much they really help. A clustering of the sensors themselves will show you which ones are almost copies of each other. Before any of this, remove the 116 columns that have the same value in every row. They cannot carry any information.

Question 3 is a regression problem. Pick one sensor column, remove it from the inputs, and predict it from the rest. You have 590 columns and only 1,567 rows. This is exactly the situation Session 8 was about. So start with Ridge, not plain least squares, and you will quickly see why.

The data

The SECOM dataset from the UCI repository. One thousand five hundred and sixty-seven production lots, each with five hundred and ninety anonymised sensor readings and a timestamp. One hundred and four of them failed. The label is recorded as 1 for a failure and -1 for a lot that passed.

https://archive.ics.uci.edu/static/public/179/secom.zip

Licensed CC BY 4.0. The sensors are anonymised, so you will not know what any of them physically measures. This is normal in this industry, and it is part of the exercise.

Constraints on this brief

Do not use accuracy unless you can defend it. There are one hundred and four failures in one thousand five hundred and sixty-seven lots. A model that predicts "pass" for every lot is completely useless, but it scores 93.4%. If the word accuracy appears anywhere in your conclusions, you must justify it in the same paragraph.

Write the two costs first, in rupees. What does it cost to miss a failing lot? What does it cost to take a good lot off the line for inspection? Only these two numbers decide your threshold. Show the curve of expected cost against threshold, and mark where you set it.

Split by time. The lots are timestamped, and the process changes slowly over time. Train on the earlier lots and test on the later ones. A random split lets your model learn a process condition from lots recorded both before and after it.

The data is very messy, and cleaning it is the main work. There are about forty-two thousand missing values. One hundred and sixteen columns have the same value in every row and carry no information at all. About three hundred and forty of the five hundred and ninety columns have almost no variance. Many columns are almost duplicates of each other. You must handle all of this inside a pipeline. That is more difficult than handling it in a notebook cell, and that is why we require it.

Search more here than in any other brief. This is by far the smallest dataset of the six. A cross-validated fit takes one second. So you really do have the computing power to search the hyperparameter space properly. Your budget from stage 6 should be the largest of any team in the class. Your report should say this and show what the extra search gave you. If it gave you nothing, that finding is worth more than a better score.

What is hard about this one

You have five hundred and ninety columns and only one thousand five hundred rows. Usually you want more rows than columns. Here there are more things measured than there are lots to learn from. In this situation a model can find a pattern that is only chance, and fully believe it. Regularisation from Session 8 and dimension reduction from Session 11 were made for exactly this problem.

The other hard thing is this. Your tuned score will be much better than your nested cross-validation score, and the gap will be large. We expect this. Explaining that gap honestly is the most valuable paragraph you will write in this course.
