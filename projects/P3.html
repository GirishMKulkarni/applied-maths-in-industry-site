P3 · The Lot That Fails Final Test

The situation

A semiconductor fab runs wafers through several hundred process steps. At the end of the line every lot goes through final electrical test, and a lot that fails is scrapped. Not reworked. Scrapped. A scrapped lot is worth more than a new engineer's annual salary, and by the time the test says so, six weeks of plant time have already been spent on it.

The line is covered in sensors. Temperatures, pressures, flows, gas concentrations, timings, five hundred and ninety signals in all, recorded for every lot. The process engineers have a nagging feeling that the failures are visible in that data long before final test, but nobody has ever been able to show it, because nobody can look at five hundred and ninety charts.

The plant manager wants three things.

A warning, before final test, that a lot is heading for a fail, so it can be pulled and inspected rather than finished.

A short list of the sensors that actually matter, small enough to fit on the screen above the line. Five hundred and ninety is not a dashboard.

And, quietly, an answer to a question the engineers have been arguing about: whether one particular measurement, which requires stopping the tool and takes twenty minutes, could be inferred from the sensors that are free.

The three questions

As the plant asked them.

1. "Before this lot goes to final test, can you tell me whether it is going to fail?"

2. "Which of these five hundred and ninety sensors actually deserve a place on the screen above the line?"

3. "That measurement that costs us twenty minutes and a tool stop — can you work it out from the sensors that are free?"

The first move only.

Question 1 is a binary classification problem, and it is the hardest one in the six briefs, because only 104 of the 1,567 lots failed. Start with logistic regression from Session 9, but expect your first attempt to predict "pass" for absolutely everything and look like it is doing well. That is not a bug in your code, it is the whole lesson. Look up what class_weight="balanced" does before you decide something is broken.

Question 2 is Session 11, twice over. PCA will squash the 590 sensors into a few components and tell you how much of the variation you kept. Separately, the tree models from Session 10 will rank the sensors by how much they actually help, and a clustering of the sensors themselves will show you which ones are near-copies of each other. Before any of that, throw out the 116 columns that hold the same value in every row: they cannot possibly carry information.

Question 3 is a regression problem. Pick one sensor column, take it out of the inputs, and predict it from the rest. With 590 columns and only 1,567 rows this is exactly the situation Session 8 was about, so start with Ridge rather than plain least squares and you will see why immediately.

The data

The SECOM dataset from the UCI repository. One thousand five hundred and sixty-seven production lots, each with five hundred and ninety anonymised sensor readings and a timestamp. One hundred and four of them failed. The label is recorded as 1 for a failure and -1 for a lot that passed.

https://archive.ics.uci.edu/static/public/179/secom.zip

Licensed CC BY 4.0. The sensors are anonymised, so you will not know what any of them physically measures. That is normal in this industry and it is part of the exercise.

Constraints on this brief

Accuracy is banned unless you defend it. One hundred and four failures in one thousand five hundred and sixty-seven lots. A model that predicts "pass" for every lot, and is therefore completely useless, scores 93.4%. If the word accuracy appears anywhere in your conclusions you must justify it in the same paragraph.

Write the two costs first, in rupees. What does it cost to miss a failing lot? What does it cost to pull a good lot off the line for inspection? Those two numbers, and nothing else, decide your threshold. Show the curve of expected cost against threshold and mark where you set it.

Split by time. The lots are timestamped and the process drifts. Train on the earlier lots, test on the later ones. A random split lets your model learn a process condition from lots recorded on either side of it.

The data is a mess, and clearing it is the work. There are around forty-two thousand missing values. One hundred and sixteen columns hold the same value in every single row and carry no information at all. Around three hundred and forty of the five hundred and ninety have almost no variance. Many are near-duplicates of each other. All of that has to be dealt with inside a pipeline, which is harder than dealing with it in a notebook cell, and is the reason we require it.

Search harder here than anywhere else. This is the smallest dataset of the six by a wide margin. A cross-validated fit takes a second. You genuinely have the compute to explore the hyperparameter space properly, so your budget from stage 6 should be the largest of any team in the class, and your report should say so and show what the extra searching bought you. If it bought nothing, that is a finding worth more than a better score.

What is hard about this one

Five hundred and ninety columns and one thousand five hundred rows is the wrong way round. There are more things to measure than there are lots to learn from, and in that situation a model can find a pattern that is pure coincidence and be completely convinced by it. Everything you know about regularisation from Session 8, and everything about dimension reduction from Session 11, exists for exactly this.

The other hard thing is that the gap between your tuned score and your nested cross-validation score will be embarrassing. It is meant to be. Explaining that gap honestly is the most valuable paragraph you will write in this course.
