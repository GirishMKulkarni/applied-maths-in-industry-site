  • Client: A retired school teacher

  • The catch: The best mix on past data changes a lot when you move the dates by a few months.

F1 · The Retirement Money

The situation

Mrs. Joshi taught mathematics in a government school for thirty-two years. She retired this year. After the house loan was closed she has ₹10 lakh left over. Her pension pays for the monthly expenses, so she does not need this money for three or four years. She wants it to grow faster than a fixed deposit.

She has decided to buy shares directly, not mutual funds, because her neighbour does the same and showed her the broker app. She has made a list of ten large companies she has heard of. She has two conditions. She does not want more than a quarter of the money in any one company. And she wants to know, in rupees, how much she can lose in a bad month, before she puts in a single rupee.

Her son works at an IT company in Bengaluru. His advice is simple: "Put everything in TCS and Infosys. IT always does well." Her neighbour says just buy a Nifty index fund and forget about it.

She has asked you to help her decide.

The three questions

In her own words.

1. "How much of my ₹10 lakh should go into each of these ten shares?"

2. "In a bad month, how much money can I lose? Tell me in rupees, not in percent."

3. "My son says put everything in TCS and Infosys. My neighbour says buy the Nifty. Which is better, your mix or theirs?"

Question 2 is Session 17. Your class code already does historical VaR, parametric VaR, Monte Carlo VaR and CVaR for a five-stock portfolio. Do the same for your mix, but for 21 trading days (one month), not one day. One easy way: add up the daily log returns in each 21-day window and use those as your monthly returns. Then do the stress test: what would March 2020 have done to her money?

Question 3 is Session 14. The growth of ₹100 chart, the Sharpe ratio and the worst drawdown. Put the four choices on one chart: your mix, the equal mix, 50% TCS plus 50% Infosys, and the Nifty 50. Do this on the checking period only.

The data

Daily closing prices from Yahoo Finance. These are the ten shares from Session 16, plus the Nifty 50 index:

import yfinance as yf

shares = ["TCS.NS", "INFY.NS", "HDFCBANK.NS", "RELIANCE.NS", "ITC.NS",
          "MARUTI.NS", "SUNPHARMA.NS", "LT.NS", "NESTLEIND.NS", "TATASTEEL.NS"]

prices = yf.download(shares + ["^NSEI"], start="2020-01-01", end="2026-01-01",
                     auto_adjust=True, progress=False)["Close"]

Use auto_adjust=True. Some of these companies gave bonus shares or split their shares in these years. Without adjusted prices you will see a fall of 50% on one day that never happened.

If Yahoo Finance does not work on the day you need it, save the table once with prices.to_csv(...) and put the CSV in your repo.

Rules for this brief

Two periods, and keep them apart.

  • The learning period is 1 January 2021 to 31 December 2023. Use only this to find the weights.
  • The checking period is 1 January 2024 to 31 December 2025. Use it only for Question 3, at the end.

March 2020 is kept out of both periods on purpose. Use it only for the stress test in Question 2.

No short selling, and no share above 25%. Mrs. Joshi cannot sell shares she does not own, and she gave you the 25% rule herself.

Use 6% a year as the safe return for the Sharpe ratio. That is roughly what a bank fixed deposit pays.

Remember that shares come in whole numbers. You cannot buy 3.7 shares of Nestle India. At the end of Question 1, turn your weights into the number of shares she should buy at the price on 31 December 2023, and say how much money is left over in cash.

What is hard about this one

The weights from the optimiser look exact, like 23.4% in one share and 0% in another. They are not exact. They come from past average returns, and past average returns are a weak guide to the future.

Check this for yourself. Move the learning period by six months, to July 2021 to June 2024, and find the weights again. Put the two sets of weights side by side. If a share goes from 25% to 0%, Mrs. Joshi should know that. Tell her which part of your answer you trust and which part you do not.

The second hard part is Question 3. Over a short period one sector can do very well, and then the son's advice looks clever. Over a different period it does badly. Say what happened in the checking period, and also say what you think can happen to someone who puts all their money in one sector.
