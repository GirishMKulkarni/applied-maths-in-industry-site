P2 · Where the Marketing Budget Goes

The situation

A wholesaler sells gifts for special occasions online. Most of its customers are small shops that buy in bulk and resell. Every transaction from the last two years is in one table. Until now nobody has used this data.

The company has just hired its first marketing person. They have a budget for one campaign every quarter, and there are about six thousand customers. They cannot contact all six thousand. They want to know which customers to spend the money on. They have asked you three questions, in their own words.

"Who is about to stop buying from us?" It is cheap to win a customer back if you contact them early. It is almost impossible once they have found another supplier.

"Who is worth spending money on?" Not every customer who is leaving is worth the cost of contacting them. The marketing person wants an amount for each customer, not a ranking.

"What kinds of customer do we even have?" Nobody in the company can answer this. People think there are three kinds: big regular buyers, seasonal buyers who only come before Christmas, and a long list of shops that bought only once. Nobody has checked this.

The three questions

In the marketing manager's own words.

1. "How much is each of these customers going to spend with us over the next three months?"

2. "Which of them are about to stop buying from us altogether?"

3. "What kinds of customer do we actually have, and what should I say to each kind?"

Stuck at the very start? Open this.

This only tells you where to start, so that nobody wastes a week treating it as the wrong kind of problem.

Question 1 is a regression problem, and the difficult part is that nothing in the file is the answer. You have to make the answer. Pick a date. Treat everything before it as what you know. Then add up what each customer actually spent in the ninety days after it. That total is your target. Start with linear regression from Session 7. Spend is very uneven: a few customers bring most of the money. So also try predicting the logarithm of spend, and see what changes.

Question 2 is a binary classification problem, built from the same cut-off date: a customer who spent nothing in those ninety days is a 1, everyone else a 0. Start with logistic regression from Session 9.

Question 3 is a clustering problem, and it is the standard example. For each customer, calculate three numbers: how recently they last bought, how often they buy, and how much they have spent. Scale them, then use k-means from Session 11. Look at the average customer in each group. That will tell you what to name each group.

The data

Online Retail II, from the UCI repository. One million and sixty-seven thousand rows, every transaction of a UK-registered online gift wholesaler between 1 December 2009 and 9 December 2011, from 5,943 customers. Invoice number, stock code, description, quantity, invoice date, unit price, customer ID, country.

Constraints on this brief

There is no target column, and that is the main task. Nothing in this table says "churned" or "will spend". You choose a cut-off date. You build every feature from transactions before that date, and you build both answers from transactions after it. The date you choose changes your results a lot. Say what date you chose and why. Also say what happened when you tried a different date.

One row per transaction is not one row per customer. You have a million transaction rows. Turning them into a customer table with the features you want is the main work here. It is also the most common task an analyst gets in a job. Group, aggregate, and think carefully about which summaries of a customer's history are really useful for prediction.

Split by customer, and by time. A customer must never appear in both your training and your test data. Also, your test period must come after your training period. A model that learned from Christmas 2010 to predict Christmas 2010 has learned nothing.

Decide what a cancellation is. Some invoice numbers begin with a C and have negative quantities. Some rows have no customer ID at all. Some unit prices are zero, and a few are negative. For each of these you must make a decision, and each decision changes the result. You get marks for stating your decision clearly, not for getting the same answer as everybody else.

Spend is extremely uneven. A few customers bring most of the revenue. The distribution has a long tail, and that tail will give a simple regression very poor results. Most of the thinking in this brief is about what you do with this tail, and how it affects your choice of error measure.

What is hard about this one

Percentage error. A business person will ask for this measure by name. On this data it gives misleading results. Suppose you predicted forty rupees and the customer spends four hundred. That is a nine hundred percent error. A customer who spends nothing gives you an infinite error. So you must decide what to report to a non-technical manager instead. You must also explain why you are not giving them the percentage they asked for. This is a real part of the job.

The second hard thing is this. "Will not buy in ninety days" is not the same question as "has left forever", and the marketing person does not know the difference. You will have to explain it to them in the memo.

  3. Before you write any modelling code, write the one-page cost note that stage 1 asks for and commit it. The timestamp on that commit is worth ten marks.
  4. Download the data with code, in a notebook, and answer your eight questions about it.
  5. Split the test set off the way this brief tells you to, and do not look at it again until the end.
