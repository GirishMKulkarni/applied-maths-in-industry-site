P2 · Where the Marketing Budget Goes

The situation

A wholesaler sells occasion gifts online, mostly to small shops that buy in bulk and resell. Two years of every transaction it has ever made sit in one table. Until now nobody has done anything with them.

The company has just hired its first marketing person. They have a budget for one campaign a quarter, and about six thousand customers. They cannot contact six thousand. They want to know who to spend it on, and they have asked you three questions in the language they actually used.

"Who is about to stop buying from us?" Winning a customer back is cheap if you catch them early and nearly impossible once they have found another supplier.

"Who is worth chasing?" Not everyone who is leaving is worth the postage. The marketing person wants an amount, per customer, not a ranking.

"What kinds of customer do we even have?" Nobody in the company can answer this. There is a feeling that there are the big regulars, the seasonal ones who only appear before Christmas, and a long tail of shops that bought once. Nobody has checked.

The three questions

In the marketing manager's own words.

1. "How much is each of these customers going to spend with us over the next three months?"

2. "Which of them are about to stop buying from us altogether?"

3. "What kinds of customer do we actually have, and what should I say to each kind?"

Stuck at the very start? Open this.

The first move only, so nobody loses a week on the wrong shape of problem.

Question 1 is a regression problem, and the awkward part is that nothing in the file is the answer. You make the answer. Pick a date, treat everything before it as what you know, and add up what each customer actually spent in the ninety days after it. That sum is your target. Start with linear regression from Session 7. Spend is very lopsided, a few customers are most of the money, so also try predicting the logarithm of spend and see what that does.

Question 2 is a binary classification problem, built from the same cut-off date: a customer who spent nothing in those ninety days is a 1, everyone else a 0. Start with logistic regression from Session 9.

Question 3 is a clustering problem, and it is the classic one. For each customer work out three numbers: how recently they last bought, how often they buy, and how much they have spent. Scale them, then k-means from Session 11. Look at the average customer in each group and that is where the names come from.

The data

Online Retail II, from the UCI repository. One million and sixty-seven thousand rows, every transaction of a UK-registered online gift wholesaler between 1 December 2009 and 9 December 2011, from 5,943 customers. Invoice number, stock code, description, quantity, invoice date, unit price, customer ID, country.

Constraints on this brief

There is no target column, and that is the brief. Nothing in this table says "churned" or "will spend". You choose a cut-off date, build every feature from transactions before it, and build both answers from transactions after it. Which date you choose changes your results substantially. Say what you chose, why, and what happened when you tried a different one.

One row per transaction is not one row per customer. Getting from a million transaction rows to a customer table with the features you want is the core work here, and it is the single most common thing an analyst is asked to do in a job. Group, aggregate, and think hard about which summaries of a customer's history actually carry a signal.

Split by customer, and by time. A customer must never appear in both your training and your test data. And your test period must come after your training period, because a model that learned Christmas 2010 to predict Christmas 2010 has learned nothing.

Decide what a cancellation is. Some invoice numbers begin with a C and carry negative quantities. Some rows have no customer ID at all. Some unit prices are zero, and a few are negative. Every one of those is a decision with a consequence, and the marks are in stating the decision, not in getting the same answer as everybody else.

Spend is savagely skewed. A handful of customers are most of the revenue, and the distribution has a tail that will wreck a naive regression. What you do about that, and what it does to your choice of error measure, is most of the thinking in this brief.

What is hard about this one

Percentage error. It is the metric a business person asks for by name, and on this data it is a trap: a customer who spends four hundred rupees when you predicted forty gives you a nine hundred percent error, and a customer who spends nothing gives you infinity. Working out what to report to a non-technical manager instead, and how to explain why you are not giving them the percentage they asked for, is a real part of this job.

The second hard thing is that "will not buy in ninety days" is not the same question as "has left forever", and the marketing person does not know the difference. You will have to explain it to them in the memo.
