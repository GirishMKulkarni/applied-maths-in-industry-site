O3 · The Chemist Shop

The situation

Mr. Shaikh runs a medical store in Solapur, next to a busy clinic. He has three problems.

The evening queue. Between 6 and 9 in the evening, when the clinic's patients come out with their prescriptions, a customer walks in on average every 2.5 minutes. Serving one customer takes on average 2 minutes. Only one pharmacist works at the counter. People complain about the wait, and some walk across the road to the new pharmacy. A second pharmacist for the evening would cost ₹600 an evening.

Empty shelves. His biggest seller is paracetamol. He sells on average 30 strips a day, some days more and some days less. When he orders, the supplier delivers two days later. Every order costs ₹150 for delivery. Keeping one strip on the shelf for a day costs about 20 paise, counting the money locked up in stock and the space. When a customer asks for paracetamol and he has none, he loses about ₹5 of profit, and the customer may not come back.

Customers going away. He thinks of his customers in three groups. Regulars buy every month. Occasional customers buy sometimes. Lost customers now buy elsewhere. From his billing records he has worked out how people move between these groups from one month to the next:

This month \ Next month Regular Occasional Lost
Regular 0.85 0.10 0.05
Occasional 0.20 0.60 0.20
Lost 0.05 0.10 0.85

Today he has 600 regular and 300 occasional customers. A regular customer brings him about ₹400 of profit a month. An occasional one brings about ₹100.

He is thinking about starting free home delivery. He thinks it would make regulars much less likely to leave: 0.92 stay regular, 0.05 become occasional and only 0.03 are lost each month. Home delivery would cost him ₹15,000 a month for a delivery boy and fuel.

The three questions

In Mr. Shaikh's own words.

1. "In the evening rush, how long do my customers really wait? Is a second pharmacist worth ₹600 an evening?"

2. "When should I order paracetamol, and how much, so that I almost never say 'not available'?"

3. "After one year, how many regular customers will I still have? Is home delivery worth ₹15,000 a month?"

Use a Poisson distribution with an average of 30, as in class. The lead time is 2 days. Add the ₹150 delivery charge to the cost every time an order is placed. The class code does not have this cost, so you must add it. Try many pairs of s and S. Keep the cheapest pair that meets demand on at least 95% of the strips asked for.

Rules for this brief

Fix your seeds, and do not trust one run. Questions 1 and 2 use random numbers. Set a seed so that we get the same numbers as you. But base your answer on many runs, not one. For Question 1 use at least 100 evenings. For Question 2 run each policy for at least 300 days.

Say what "almost never" means. Mr. Shaikh said "almost never". You turn that into a number, such as 95% or 99% of the strips asked for. Choose one, say why, and then show what the stock and the cost would be if you had chosen the other one.

Show the waiting in minutes, not only the average. An average of 9 minutes can hide some people waiting 40 minutes. Draw a histogram of the waiting times for one counter and for two counters, side by side.

Remember that the matrix is his guess. Mr. Shaikh worked out the first table from records. The home delivery numbers are only his hope. In your note, say how much better than today the home delivery numbers must be for it to pay for itself.

What is hard about this one

Each of the three questions has an answer that is a number with some randomness in it. The average waiting time changes from one evening to the next. The best (s, S) pair changes a little if you change the seed. A good answer says this honestly: "with one pharmacist, the average wait is around so many minutes, and on a bad evening it goes up to so many".

The other hard part is putting everything in rupees, so that Mr. Shaikh can compare. Waiting time is not in rupees. You will have to make a sensible assumption, for example that a customer who waits more than 10 minutes goes to the other shop, and lose ₹400 a month if that customer was a regular. Write your assumption down clearly, and check if your answer changes when the assumption changes.
