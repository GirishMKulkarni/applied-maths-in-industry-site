O2 · The Fertiliser Money

The situation

Mrs. Wankhede farms four and a half acres near Akola. This season she has three crops: cotton on two acres, soybean on one and a half acres, and tur on one acre. She has ₹60,000 to spend on fertiliser and spray for all three.

Every year she splits the money equally, ₹20,000 for each crop. This year she went to the Krishi Vigyan Kendra, and the agriculture officer gave her a rough rule for her own fields. For each crop:

extra income from the crop (in ₹ thousand) = k × √(money spent on it, in ₹ thousand)

Crop k
Cotton 12
Soybean 8.5
Tur 5

So, for example, ₹16,000 on cotton gives 12 × √16 = 48, which is ₹48,000 of extra income. ₹36,000 on cotton gives 12 × √36 = 72, which is ₹72,000. The money went up by more than double, but the income went up by only half. This is what the officer meant when he said "after some point, more fertiliser does not help much".

She has also been offered a crop loan of ₹20,000 from the bank at 12% a year. She would repay it after the harvest, in six months.

The three questions

In Mrs. Wankhede's own words.

1. "How should I split my ₹60,000 between cotton, soybean and tur? How much more will I earn than with my equal split?"

2. "The government pulse scheme wants me to spend at least ₹10,000 on tur. What does that rule cost me?"

3. "Should I take the ₹20,000 loan and put it into fertiliser too?"

Question 1 is Session 22, the advertising budget in your class code. It is the same problem. TV, Instagram and Google become cotton, soybean and tur. The money spent is a cvxpy variable with three entries, all at least zero, adding up to 60. The income is the sum of k times the square root. Solve it and then work out the income of the equal split by hand, to compare.

Question 2 is Session 22 again. Add one more rule: the money on tur is at least 10. Solve again. The fall in income is the cost of the scheme's rule.

Question 3 is the equal-marginal rule from Session 22. At the best split, the last ₹1,000 earns the same amount in every crop. Print that number. If the last ₹1,000 earns less than ₹1,000 plus the interest, borrowing more to spend on fertiliser loses money. Then solve again with ₹80,000 and compare the extra income with what she must pay back to the bank.

Rules for this brief

Work in ₹ thousand. Write 60, not 60000. The numbers stay small, and that is easier for the solver.

Check the equal-marginal rule. After every solve, print what the last ₹1,000 earns in each crop. The formula is k / (2 × √spend). At the best answer these numbers are equal for every crop that gets money. If they are not equal, something is wrong in your model.

Draw one picture. For each crop, plot income against money spent, from 0 to 60. Mark the amount you chose on each curve. Mrs. Wankhede will understand the answer from this picture faster than from any table.

Remember that the rule is rough. The officer's formula is an approximation. It does not know about rain or pests. In your note, say how much your answer would change if k for cotton were 10 instead of 12.

What is hard about this one

The first idea most people have is "cotton earns the most, so put everything in cotton". Try it and see what happens. Because the income grows like a square root, the first few thousand rupees on any crop earn a lot, and the later ones earn little. So the best answer gives some money to every crop, but not equal amounts.

Question 3 needs care. ₹20,000 more fertiliser will bring in more income. That part is true. But she has to pay the loan back with interest. The question is not "does income go up?". The question is "does income go up by more than she repays?". Explain this to her in one simple sentence.
