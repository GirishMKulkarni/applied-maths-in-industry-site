F2 · Can a Computer Beat the Market?

The situation

Mr. Agarwal runs a small stock-broking office in Indore. He has about four hundred customers. Most of them are shopkeepers, teachers and retired people who trade a little on the side.

In the last two years the same questions keep coming to his office. A customer saw an advertisement for "AI trading software". Another one is in a WhatsApp group that sends a buy signal every morning. A third one watched a YouTube video about a moving-average rule that "never fails".

Mr. Agarwal does not want to sell anything to them. He wants to give them honest answers, with numbers, that he can show on his computer screen. He has asked you for three things.

The three questions

In his own words.

1. "Can a computer tell me where the Nifty will be next week?"

2. "This YouTube video says: buy when the 20-day average goes above the 50-day average, sell when it goes below. After brokerage, does it make money?"

3. "One customer says 10 and 30 work much better than 20 and 50. Is he right, or was he just lucky?"

This tells you which class notebook to start from. It does not give the answer.

Question 1 is Session 15. Use the Nifty closing value. Fit ARIMA and the three exponential smoothing models from the class code. Forecast five trading days ahead. Now the important part: compare every model against the simplest forecast of all, "next week will be the same as today". Call it the naive forecast. Measure all of them with RMSE on the checking period. Do it many times, not once: stand on each Friday of the checking period, forecast the next five days, and average the errors. Session 14 tells you what to expect here, from the ACF of the returns.

Question 2 is Session 19, notebook 01 and the cost part of notebook 02. Run the 20/50 rule on the Nifty and on each of the five shares. Trade on the next day, with shift(1). Take off 0.1% on every buy and every sell. Compare with simply buying and holding: final value of ₹1, Sharpe ratio, worst drawdown, and number of trades.

Question 3 is Session 19, notebook 02, trap 2. Try many pairs of averages on the learning period only. Pick the best pair. Then run that same pair on the checking period and see what happens. Do the same for 10/30 as the customer asked.

The data

Daily closing prices from Yahoo Finance:

Rules for this brief

Two periods, and keep them apart.

  • The learning period is 1 January 2015 to 31 December 2021. Fit your models and choose your rule settings using only this period.
  • The checking period is 1 January 2022 to 31 December 2025. Use it at the end to see how your choices really do.

Today's signal is traded tomorrow. The 20-day average for today is known only after the market closes today. So you can act on it only tomorrow. If you forget shift(1), your rule will look far better than it really is. Show us once, on purpose, what happens to the result when you forget it.

Every buy and every sell costs 0.1%. That is a rough number for brokerage, taxes and charges together. Then try 0.2% and say if your answer changes.

Always compare with buy and hold. A rule that turns ₹1 into ₹1.50 sounds good. If just holding the share turned ₹1 into ₹2, the rule lost money for you.

What is hard about this one

The hard part is not the code. The code is short, and most of it is in your class notebooks. The hard part is being honest about the result.

On the class data from Session 19, the 20/50 rule on HDFC Bank turned ₹1 into about ₹1.05 from 2019 to 2025, before any costs. Simply holding the share turned ₹1 into about ₹1.97. Your job is to find out whether that was bad luck with one share, or whether it happens for the Nifty and the other shares too.

For Question 3, remember the 200 random rules from Session 19. If you try enough settings, one of them will look very good on the past just by chance. The only fair test is new data that was not used to choose the setting. When you explain this to Mr. Agarwal, do not use the word "overfitting". Explain it the way you would explain it to his customers.

For Question 1, a result of "no model beats the naive forecast" is a correct and useful answer. Do not hide it. Explain why in simple words, using what you saw in the ACF of the daily returns.

Extra, only if you finish early

Session 19 also did pairs trading on HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank. Mr. Agarwal's customers have heard of it too. Pick one other pair of companies in the same business, for example TCS and Infosys. Test whether the gap between them stays steady (the cointegration test), and run the z-score rule with costs. This is not marked, but you can show it in your talk.
