Financial Maths projects

These three projects use only what we did in the finance module, Sessions 14 to 19. Prices and returns, forecasting, building a portfolio, VaR and CVaR, options and backtesting. Nothing new to learn. The new part is that nobody tells you which step comes next.

Each project is small. It has one person with a real money problem, three questions in that person's own words, and data you can download in two lines. The code you need is already in your class notebooks. Your job is to put the right pieces together and then explain the answer to someone who does not know any finance.

How this works

  1. Work in your project team. Each team picks one of the three projects below.
  2. Read your brief fully before you write any code.
  3. Answer the three questions. One notebook per question is fine.
  4. If you do not know where to start a question, open the help box in your brief. It tells you which session and which notebook to start from.
  5. Write a short note for the person in your brief. Two pages, plain words, numbers in rupees, no code.

You have about two weeks. This is much smaller than your ML project, and it is marked out of 50, not 100.

Choose one

F1 The Retirement Money A retired school teacher · Personal finance and portfolios F2 Can a Computer Beat the Market? A small stock-broking office · Trading and forecasting F3 Insurance for Your Shares A parent who must pay college fees next month · Options and risk

What you hand in

  1. A GitHub repo with your notebooks. Every notebook must run from top to bottom on Google Colab. It must download its own data. Fix the random seed so we get the same numbers as you.
  2. A note of two pages for the person in your brief. They are not a finance person. Tell them what to do, how much money is at stake, and what could go wrong. If the note needs a formula or a line of code to make sense, rewrite it.
  3. A five-minute talk in class. Show one chart and give your answer to each of the three questions.

Three rules for all three projects

Fix your dates. Every brief gives you a start date and an end date. Use those dates, not period="3y". Otherwise your numbers change every day and nobody can check them.

Do not use the future. If a brief splits the data into a learning period and a checking period, choose everything (weights, settings, models) using the learning period only. Then look at the checking period once, at the end, and report what you see, good or bad. In a backtest, today's signal is traded tomorrow. This is the shift(1) from Session 19.

Talk in rupees. "VaR is 2.1%" means nothing to the people in these briefs. "On one bad day in twenty you can lose ₹21,000 or more" means something.

How it is marked

Out of 50.

Marks What gets the marks
Question 1 12 Correct method, correct code, and the answer stated in one plain sentence
Question 2 12 Same
Question 3 12 Same
The note 10 Two pages that the person in your brief could read and act on, with rupee numbers and one honest paragraph on what could go wrong
The notebooks run 4 We open them on Colab and run everything. They download their own data and finish without any help from us

Minus 10 for using the checking period to make a choice. For example, choosing weights or rule settings by looking at 2024 and 2025 results.

We do not mark how much money your portfolio or rule makes. A team that finds its rule loses money, and shows clearly why, can get full marks.
