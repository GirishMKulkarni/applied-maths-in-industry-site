P4 · Tomorrow's Air

The situation

You are working with a city administration. On bad-air days schools are supposed to move games indoors, construction is supposed to pause, and people with asthma are supposed to be told to stay in. None of that can be arranged on the morning of, because by the morning of, the children are already at school and the construction crew is already on site.

The administration needs to know the evening before.

At the moment it does not. It has an air quality board that reports what the air is like right now, which is useful for deciding whether to go for a walk and useless for planning anything. The health department wants a warning issued at about six in the evening for the following day, and it wants that warning in the same language the public already knows: Good, Satisfactory, Moderate, Poor, Very Poor, Severe.

They have also asked a question the environment department cannot answer. Delhi and Chennai both have bad days, but they are not bad in the same way, and the measures that work in one may be pointless in the other. Nobody has grouped the cities by what actually pollutes them.

The three questions

As the health and environment departments asked them.

1. "Tell me this evening what the air is going to be like tomorrow."

2. "And tell me in the words the public already knows: is tomorrow Good, Satisfactory, Moderate, Poor, Very Poor or Severe?"

3. "Delhi and Chennai both have bad days. Are they bad in the same way, or do we need different measures in each city?"

Stuck at the very start? Open this.

The first move only.

Question 1 is a regression problem, and there is a trap in it that will cost you the project if you walk into it. The AQI number in each row is calculated from the pollutant numbers in that same row. Predict today's AQI from today's pollutants and you will get an almost perfect score, and you will have built a calculator, not a forecast. You are predicting tomorrow, using only what you could know this evening. That means building lagged columns: today's PM2.5, yesterday's, the average of the last week. Once those exist, start with linear regression from Session 7.

Question 2 is a multi-class classification problem. Six classes instead of two, and the AQI_Bucket column already gives you the label. Start with logistic regression from Session 9, which handles more than two classes without any extra work. The classes are ordered, Severe is further from Good than Moderate is, and your first model will not know that. Notice it.

Question 3 is a clustering problem. One row per city, the columns being that city's typical levels of each pollutant, then k-means from Session 11. A city choked by vehicle exhaust and a city choked by dust should not land in the same group.

The data

Air Quality Data in India, 2015 to 2020, collected by the Central Pollution Control Board and published on Kaggle by Rohan Rao. Two files matter: city_day.csv and station_hour.csv. The columns are City, Datetime, PM2.5, PM10, NO, NO2, NOx, NH3, CO, SO2, O3, Benzene, Toluene, Xylene, AQI and AQI_Bucket.

It downloads without a Kaggle account:

https://www.kaggle.com/api/v1/datasets/download/rohanrao/air-quality-data-in-india

Constraints on this brief

The index is calculated from the pollutant columns in the same row. Read that sentence again, because it is the whole brief. AQI is not measured, it is computed from PM2.5, PM10 and the rest by a published formula. A model that uses today's pollutants to predict today's AQI will score an R squared of about 0.99 and has learned arithmetic, not air quality. It cannot be deployed, because at six in the evening you do not have tomorrow's pollutant readings.

You are forecasting tomorrow from today and earlier. Only. Every feature you build has to be something a person could actually know at six o'clock on the evening before.

Test on 2020, by date. Then look up what happened in India in the last week of March 2020, and write honestly about what it does to your test scores. Some teams will find their model looks better in 2020 and some will find it looks worse, and both need explaining. A model evaluated on a period unlike anything it trained on is a genuine industrial situation, not a spoiled experiment.

Your metric must know the classes are ordered. Calling a Severe day Satisfactory is not the same size of mistake as calling it Poor, and plain accuracy, plain F1 and the ordinary Cohen's kappa all treat those two errors as identical. Something in your evaluation has to understand the ordering, and choosing what is part of the work.

Missing readings are everywhere, and they are not missing at random. Some stations do not measure some pollutants at all. Some go offline for months. Sometimes a station is offline precisely because conditions were extreme. How you fill those gaps is a modelling decision that changes your answer, and it belongs inside the pipeline, with the reasoning written down.

What is hard about this one

Two things fight each other. The costs are wildly asymmetric, because telling a city it is fine when it is Severe hurts children, while a false alarm costs a morning of inconvenience. But the Severe class is also the rarest, so it is the one your model will be worst at, and the one every default setting in scikit-learn will quietly ignore. Reconciling those two facts is the project.

The second is seasonality. North Indian winter air is a different phenomenon from the rest of the year. A model trained across all months will be mediocre in both regimes, and a model per season has less data to learn from. There is no clean answer, and we want to see you choose one and say what it cost.
