P4 · Tomorrow's Air

The situation

You are working with a city administration. On bad-air days, schools are supposed to move games indoors, construction is supposed to stop, and people with asthma are supposed to be told to stay at home. None of this can be arranged on the same morning. By then the children are already at school and the construction workers are already on site.

The administration needs to know the evening before.

Right now it does not. It has an air quality board that shows what the air is like at this moment. That helps you decide whether to go for a walk. It does not help you plan anything. The health department wants a warning at about six in the evening for the next day. It wants the warning in the same words the public already knows: Good, Satisfactory, Moderate, Poor, Very Poor, Severe.

They have also asked a question the environment department cannot answer. Delhi and Chennai both have bad days, but the causes are not the same. A measure that works in one city may be useless in the other. Nobody has grouped the cities by what actually pollutes their air.

The three questions

As the health and environment departments asked them.

1. "Tell me this evening what the air is going to be like tomorrow."

2. "And tell me in the words the public already knows: is tomorrow Good, Satisfactory, Moderate, Poor, Very Poor or Severe?"

3. "Delhi and Chennai both have bad days. Are they bad in the same way, or do we need different measures in each city?"

Stuck at the very start? Open this.

This only helps you get started.

Question 1 is a regression problem, and it has one mistake that can make your whole project fail. The AQI number in each row is calculated from the pollutant numbers in that same row. If you predict today's AQI from today's pollutants, you will get an almost perfect score. But you will have built a calculator, not a forecast. You are predicting tomorrow, using only what you can know this evening. So you need to build lagged columns: today's PM2.5, yesterday's, the average of the last week. Once you have these, start with linear regression from Session 7.

Question 2 is a multi-class classification problem. There are six classes instead of two, and the AQI_Bucket column already gives you the label. Start with logistic regression from Session 9, which handles more than two classes with no extra work. The classes are in order. Severe is further from Good than Moderate is. Your first model will not know that. Notice it.

Question 3 is a clustering problem. Make one row per city. The columns are that city's typical levels of each pollutant. Then use k-means from Session 11. A city polluted mostly by vehicle smoke and a city polluted mostly by dust should not land in the same group.

The data

Air Quality Data in India, 2015 to 2020, collected by the Central Pollution Control Board and published on Kaggle by Rohan Rao. Two files matter: city_day.csv and station_hour.csv. The columns are City, Datetime, PM2.5, PM10, NO, NO2, NOx, NH3, CO, SO2, O3, Benzene, Toluene, Xylene, AQI and AQI_Bucket.

It downloads without a Kaggle account:

https://www.kaggle.com/api/v1/datasets/download/rohanrao/air-quality-data-in-india

Constraints on this brief

The index is calculated from the pollutant columns in the same row. Read that sentence again. The whole brief depends on it. AQI is not measured. It is computed from PM2.5, PM10 and the rest using a published formula. A model that uses today's pollutants to predict today's AQI will get an R squared of about 0.99. It has learned arithmetic, not air quality. It cannot be used in practice, because at six in the evening you do not have tomorrow's pollutant readings.

You forecast tomorrow using only today and earlier days. Nothing else. Every feature you build has to be something a person could actually know at six o'clock on the evening before.

Test on 2020, by date. Then find out what happened in India in the last week of March 2020. Write honestly about how it affects your test scores. Some teams will find their model looks better in 2020 and some will find it looks worse. Both need explaining. In real industry, a model is often tested on a period very different from its training data. This does not mean your experiment has failed.

Your metric must know the classes are ordered. Calling a Severe day Satisfactory is a bigger mistake than calling it Poor. But plain accuracy, plain F1 and the ordinary Cohen's kappa all treat these two errors as the same. Something in your evaluation has to understand the order. Choosing that metric is part of the work.

Missing readings are everywhere, and they are not missing at random. Some stations do not measure some pollutants at all. Some go offline for months. Sometimes a station is offline exactly because conditions were extreme. How you fill these gaps is a modelling decision, and it changes your answer. Do it inside the pipeline, and write down your reasons.

What is hard about this one

Two facts pull in opposite directions. First, the two kinds of mistake have very different costs. If you tell a city the air is fine when it is Severe, children get hurt. A false alarm only costs one morning of trouble. Second, the Severe class is also the rarest. So your model will be worst at it, and every default setting in scikit-learn will mostly ignore it. Handling both facts together is the project.

The second difficulty is the seasons. In North India, winter air behaves very differently from the rest of the year. A model trained on all months together will be only average in both seasons. A separate model for each season has less data to learn from. There is no perfect answer. We want to see you choose one and say what you lost by choosing it.
