P6 · The Number on the Screen

The situation

You are working for a broadcaster covering the IPL. During the second innings the production team wants two numbers in the corner of the screen, updating every ball.

A projected final score, so the commentary has something to argue about.

And a win probability for each side, because that is the number viewers now expect and a rival broadcaster already has one.

The head of production has been clear about one thing. The graphic will be on screen in front of tens of millions of people, and it cannot embarrass them. If it says a side has a seventy percent chance and that side loses, that is fine. If it says seventy percent about a hundred different situations and the side wins forty of them, the number is a lie and people on the internet will work that out within a season.

The commentary team have asked for something else while you are at it. They want three or four types of batter, with names they can use on air, worked out from what players actually do rather than from reputation.

The three questions

As the production team asked them.

1. "It is the end of over twelve. What score are they going to finish on?"

2. "And what is the chance each side wins from here?"

3. "Give the commentary team three or four types of batter, with names they can say on air."

Stuck at the very start? Open this.

The first move only.

Question 1 is a regression problem. Your rows are individual deliveries, but the thing you predict from is the state of the match at the end of an over, so your first job is to group the deliveries into one row per over: runs so far, wickets down, balls remaining, and the final score of that innings as the target. Then linear regression from Session 7.

Question 2 is a binary classification problem, one side wins or does not, and the winner is already in the data. Start with logistic regression from Session 9. Here is the thing that makes this brief different from the others: you do not really want the label, you want the probability, because that is what goes on the screen. Logistic regression is a good starting point precisely because the number it gives you is meant to be read as a probability.

Question 3 is a clustering problem. Build one row per batter out of their own deliveries, with a few numbers describing how they score, then k-means from Session 11. Set a minimum number of balls faced before you include a player, or somebody who faced four balls in 2011 will get their own cluster.

The data

One CSV, ball_by_ball_ipl.csv, one row per delivery for every IPL season from 2008 to 2023. Each row already carries the match it belongs to, the innings, the over and ball, the batter, the bowler, the runs scored, the wicket if there was one, the venue, the two teams, the target, and the eventual match winner.

It downloads without a Kaggle account:

https://www.kaggle.com/api/v1/datasets/download/jamiewelsh2/ball-by-ball-ipl

The data derives from Cricsheet, which publishes under the Open Data Commons Attribution License 1.0, so credit Cricsheet clearly in your repo and your memo.

You still have to turn deliveries into the thing you actually predict from, which is the state of the match at the end of an over: runs so far, wickets down, balls left, runs required. That is a group-and-aggregate on the ball rows, and it is modelling work rather than plumbing.

Constraints on this brief

Split by match, never by ball. Every delivery in a game shares that game's outcome, its pitch, its weather and its two teams. Put some balls from a match in training and some in testing and your model has effectively seen the answer. This is the single most common mistake made on this dataset, and it is invisible in the scores, which is what makes it dangerous. Later seasons should be your test set.

You are marked on calibration, not on accuracy. Report the Brier score and plot the reliability curve. Bucket your predictions by the probability you gave and show what fraction actually won in each bucket. A model with worse accuracy and honest probabilities beats a confident, badly calibrated one, because the first can go on television and the second cannot.

One model over all overs will disappoint you. The state at the end of over two and the state at the end of over eighteen are different problems. Runs required, balls remaining and wickets in hand interact completely differently at the two ends of an innings. Noticing this, and deciding what to do about it, is the modelling judgement in this brief.

Decide what you do about rain. Some matches are shortened and resolved by the Duckworth-Lewis-Stern method, some are abandoned, some have revised targets mid-innings. Every one of those distorts the relationship you are trying to learn. Drop them, model them or flag them, but say which and why.

What is hard about this one

The temptation is to add everything: player identities, venue, toss, head to head. Some of that helps and some of it is a route to memorising which teams were good in 2014. The test set is later seasons, and squads change completely between them, so a model that leans on player identity ages badly in a way your cross-validation will not show you until the final test.

The second difficulty is the one the production head named. Being accurate and being honest about your uncertainty are different properties, most classifiers optimise the first, and the fix is not more searching. Session 9 gave you the tools to see the problem. What you do about it is yours to work out.
