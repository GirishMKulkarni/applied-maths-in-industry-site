P6 · The Number on the Screen

The situation

You are working for a broadcaster covering the IPL. During the second innings, the production team wants two numbers in the corner of the screen. Both numbers update after every ball.

A predicted final score, so the commentators have something to discuss.

And a win probability for each side. Viewers now expect this number, and a rival broadcaster already shows one.

The head of production has said one thing very clearly. Tens of millions of people will see this graphic, so it must not make the broadcaster look foolish. If it gives a side a seventy percent chance and that side loses, that is fine. But suppose it says seventy percent in a hundred different situations, and the side wins only forty of them. Then the number is wrong, and viewers on the internet will notice it within one season.

The commentary team has also asked for one more thing. They want three or four types of batter, with names they can use on air. These types should be based on what players actually do on the field, not on their reputation.

The three questions

As the production team asked them.

1. "It is the end of over twelve. What score are they going to finish on?"

2. "And what is the chance each side wins from here?"

3. "Give the commentary team three or four types of batter, with names they can say on air."

Stuck at the very start? Open this.

This only helps you get started.

Question 1 is a regression problem. Each row is one delivery. But you predict from the state of the match at the end of an over. So your first job is to group the deliveries into one row per over: runs so far, wickets down, balls remaining, and the final score of that innings as the target. Then use linear regression from Session 7.

Question 2 is a binary classification problem, one side wins or does not, and the winner is already in the data. Start with logistic regression from Session 9. This brief is different from the others in one way. You do not really want the label. You want the probability, because that is what goes on the screen. Logistic regression is a good place to start, because the number it gives you is meant to be read as a probability.

Question 3 is a clustering problem. Build one row per batter from their own deliveries, with a few numbers that describe how they score. Then use k-means from Session 11. Set a minimum number of balls faced before you include a player. If you do not, somebody who faced four balls in 2011 will get their own cluster.

The data

One CSV, ball_by_ball_ipl.csv, one row per delivery for every IPL season from 2008 to 2023. Each row already has the match it belongs to, the innings, the over and ball, the batter, the bowler, the runs scored, the wicket if there was one, the venue, the two teams, the target, and the final match winner.

It downloads without a Kaggle account:

https://www.kaggle.com/api/v1/datasets/download/jamiewelsh2/ball-by-ball-ipl

The data comes from Cricsheet, which publishes under the Open Data Commons Attribution License 1.0, so credit Cricsheet clearly in your repo and your memo.

You still have to turn the deliveries into what you actually predict from. That is the state of the match at the end of an over: runs so far, wickets down, balls left, runs required. You get it by a group-and-aggregate on the ball rows. This is real modelling work, not just data handling.

Constraints on this brief

Split by match, never by ball. Every delivery in a game shares that game's result, its pitch, its weather and its two teams. If some balls from a match are in training and some are in testing, your model has already seen the answer. This is the most common mistake made on this dataset. It does not show up in the scores, and that is why it is dangerous. Later seasons should be your test set.

You are marked on calibration, not on accuracy. Report the Brier score and plot the reliability curve. Group your predictions by the probability you gave, and show what fraction actually won in each group. A model with lower accuracy but honest probabilities is better than an overconfident, badly calibrated one. The first can go on television. The second cannot.

One model for all overs will not work well. The state at the end of over two and the state at the end of over eighteen are different problems. Runs required, balls remaining and wickets in hand work together very differently at the start and at the end of an innings. You have to notice this and decide what to do about it. That is the modelling judgement this brief tests.

Decide what you do about rain. Some matches are shortened and decided by the Duckworth-Lewis-Stern method, some are abandoned, and some have targets changed in the middle of an innings. Each of these changes the relationship you are trying to learn. You can drop them, model them or flag them. Say which you chose and why.

What is hard about this one

You will want to add everything: player identities, venue, toss, head to head. Some of that helps. Some of it only makes the model memorise which teams were good in 2014. The test set is later seasons, and squads change completely between seasons. So a model that depends on player identity gets worse as the years pass. Your cross-validation will not show you this. You will see it only in the final test.

The second difficulty is the one the head of production pointed out. Being accurate and being honest about your uncertainty are two different things. Most classifiers try to improve the first one. Searching over more models or settings will not fix this. Session 9 gave you the tools to see the problem. You have to work out what to do about it.

What to do next

  1. Read the method. It lists the eight things every submission contains, whichever brief you chose. These are rules to follow, not suggestions.
  2. Read the rubric so you know where the hundred marks are, and which four mistakes lose them.
  3. Before you write any modelling code, write the one-page cost note that stage 1 asks for and commit it. The timestamp on that commit is worth ten marks.
  4. Download the data with code, in a notebook, and answer your eight questions about it.
  5. Split the test set off the way this brief tells you to, and do not look at it again until the end.
