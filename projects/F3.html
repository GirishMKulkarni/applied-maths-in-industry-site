F3 · Insurance for Your Shares

The situation

Mr. Deshmukh works in a bank in Nashik. Years ago he started buying Reliance shares a few at a time, and today he holds 500 of them. His daughter got admission to an engineering college. The first-year fees and hostel deposit are due in about one month, and he plans to sell the shares to pay for it.

The fees, the hostel deposit and the first year's expenses come to about ₹7 lakh. Today his shares are worth a little more than that. He is worried. If Reliance falls 10% before he sells, he will have about ₹75,000 less than he needs, and the college will not give him more time.

He has received two suggestions.

His broker says: "Buy a put option. It works like insurance. If the price falls below a level you choose, the put pays you the difference."

His colleague says: "Don't pay for insurance. Sell a call option instead. You get money today. You only lose some of the profit if the price goes up a lot."

He does not understand either suggestion properly, and he does not want to simply believe one of them. He has asked you to explain it with numbers.

The three questions

In his own words.

1. "What is a fair price for one month of insurance on my shares? Is the price on the NSE website too high?"

2. "If I do nothing, buy the put, or sell the call, how much money will I have in my hand at the end of the month? Show me the good case and the bad case."

3. "Looking at the last three years, would this insurance really have been worth the money?"

Question 2 is Session 18, notebook 01. You already drew the profit charts for the share alone, the protective put and the covered call. Now draw them for Mr. Deshmukh's real numbers: 500 shares, the strike and premium you chose, and the money he needs for the fees. Make one simple table: Reliance at expiry 15% down, 10% down, 5% down, unchanged, 5% up, 10% up, and the rupees he gets in each plan.

Question 3 is Session 17 thinking with Session 18 tools. Take every 21-day window in 2023 to 2025. For each window, imagine he bought the put at the start at the Black-Scholes price, and see what he had at the end. Do the same with no insurance and with the covered call. Now you have hundreds of possible months for each plan. Find the average, the worst 5% (this is VaR from Session 17) and the average of the worst 5% (CVaR). Also count in how many months the put paid back more than it cost.

The data

Reliance daily closing prices from Yahoo Finance:

import yfinance as yf

reliance = yf.download("RELIANCE.NS", start="2023-01-01", end="2026-01-01",
                       auto_adjust=True, progress=False)["Close"]

Use auto_adjust=True. Reliance gave one bonus share for every share held in October 2024. Without adjusted prices you will see a 50% fall that never happened.

The class file from Session 18 has the same prices, if Yahoo Finance does not work:

https://girishmkulkarni.github.io/applied-maths-in-industry-site/sessions/S18/data/reliance_2023_2025.csv

The real option prices you note down yourself. Open the option chain for Reliance on the NSE website (nseindia.com, then Option Chain). Choose the expiry nearest to one month away. Write down, in a small table in your repo:

  • the date and time you looked
  • Reliance's price at that moment
  • the expiry date
  • the strike prices and the last traded price (LTP) of the put and the call at each strike, for at least five strikes around the current price
  • the lot size for Reliance options on that day

Take a screenshot of the page and put it in your repo too. This is real market data, and the date matters, because the prices change every minute.

Rules for this brief

Use 6% a year as the interest rate. That is roughly what a one-year government treasury bill pays.

Check the lot size. NSE does not sell options for any number of shares you like. They come in lots. If one lot is not exactly 500 shares, say what Mr. Deshmukh can actually buy and what part of his shares stays uninsured.

Count the cost every month. Insurance is not bought once. If he did this every month for a year, how much would he pay in total premiums? Put that number next to the losses the insurance saved him in Question 3.

The price on the screen is not the price you pay. There is brokerage and tax on options too. Take 0.1% of the premium for each trade, and say if it changes your answer. It usually does not change much here, but check.

What is hard about this one

The question "was the insurance worth it?" has two honest answers, and you have to give both.

Most months, the put expires worthless and the premium is lost. If you only count rupees on average, insurance usually looks like a bad deal. That is how all insurance works. Your car insurance also "loses money" in every year you do not have an accident.

But Mr. Deshmukh's problem is not the average month. His problem is one month in which the fees must be paid. Going 10% short in that one month hurts him much more than a small premium does. So Question 3 is really about the bad months, not the average. Use the worst 5% and the CVaR to show him this.

The other hard part is volatility. Black-Scholes needs a number for future volatility, and you can only measure past volatility. If your price and the NSE price do not match, that is not a mistake in your code. The difference tells you what the market expects. Explain that to him in plain words.
