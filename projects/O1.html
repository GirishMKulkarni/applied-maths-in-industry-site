O1 · What to Make from the Milk

The situation

A milk co-operative near Kolhapur collects about 2,000 litres of milk every morning from the farmers of four villages. It can sell the milk in four ways: as packed milk, as curd, as paneer or as ghee.

Mr. Patil, the secretary of the co-operative, decides every morning how much milk goes into each product. Today he does it by habit. Half the milk goes into packets, because that is how it was always done, and the rest is divided between the others.

The co-operative has eight workers, each working eight hours, so 64 working hours a day. It has one paneer machine. Each product sells only up to a certain amount a day. The shops will not take more than that.

Here are the numbers. "Profit" is what the co-operative keeps after paying the farmers for the milk and paying for packing.

Product Milk needed Workers' time Profit Most that sells in a day
Packed milk 1 litre per litre 0.01 hours per litre ₹6 per litre 1,200 litres
Curd 1 litre per kg 0.05 hours per kg ₹12 per kg 400 kg
Paneer 5 litres per kg 0.3 hours per kg ₹90 per kg 150 kg
Ghee 25 litres per kg 1 hour per kg ₹200 per kg 20 kg

The paneer machine can make at most 100 kg of paneer a day.

The three questions

In Mr. Patil's own words.

1. "From 2,000 litres, how much packed milk, curd, paneer and ghee should we make every day to earn the most?"

2. "Farmers from the next village want to sell us 300 more litres a day, but they want ₹5 a litre more than we pay now. Should we take it?"

3. "A second paneer machine will cost us ₹1,500 a day in loan instalments. Should we buy it? And what if we also hire one more worker for ₹500 a day?"

Question 1 is Session 20, notebook 02, the cheapest thali. It is the same kind of problem turned the other way. In the thali you chose how many katoris of each food to make the cost as small as possible. Here you choose how much of each product to make the profit as big as possible. One variable per product. One rule for milk, one for workers' hours, one for the paneer machine, and one for each product's demand. Solve, read the status, then check every rule by hand.

Question 2 is Session 20 again. Change the milk from 2,000 to 2,300 litres and solve again. How much did the profit go up? Divide by 300. That is what one more litre of milk is worth to the co-operative. Compare it with the extra ₹5 a litre the farmers want.

Question 3 is Session 21. Buying the machine is a yes/no decision, so it is a binary variable, like opening a dark store. If you buy it, the paneer limit goes from 100 kg to 200 kg and you pay ₹1,500 a day. Write the paneer rule so that it uses the yes/no variable. Then solve the same model again with 72 working hours instead of 64, and take off ₹500 for the new worker.

Rules for this brief

Write the problem in words first. Before any code, write three short lists in your notebook: what you decide, what you make as big as possible, and every rule that must be followed. Mr. Patil should be able to read those lists and say "yes, that is my dairy".

Check which rules are "full". After you solve, find which rules are used up completely (all the milk used, all the hours used, the machine at 100 kg) and which still have room. This is the most useful thing you can tell Mr. Patil, because it tells him what is really holding the dairy back.

Compare with what he does today. Work out the profit of his habit: 1,000 litres into packets and the rest split evenly by milk between the other three products, within their limits. Show how many rupees a day, and a month, your plan adds.

What is hard about this one

Paneer earns the most per litre of milk. So the first idea is to make as much paneer as possible. But paneer also needs a lot of workers' time and it needs the machine. When one of those runs out, making more paneer is not possible, no matter how profitable it is.

The same thing happens in Question 3. A second machine sounds like an easy "yes", because paneer makes so much money. Find out what actually stops the dairy from making more paneer. If it is not the machine, the machine will not help on its own.

In Question 2 there is a second lesson. Extra milk is worth something, but not the full profit of any one product, because the extra milk has to go to whatever is still possible to make. Explain this to Mr. Patil without using the words "shadow price".
