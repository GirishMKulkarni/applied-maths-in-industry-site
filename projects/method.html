The method

Eight stages. Every submission has all eight, whichever brief you took. This is the specification your work is marked against, so read it before you write a line of code, and read it again before you submit.

1. Write down the decision, and what a mistake costs

Before you open the data. In the first hour.

One page. What decision does this model feed? Who acts on it? What happens when the model is wrong in each of the two directions, and what does each direction cost in rupees? For a delivery estimate: what does an hour of underestimate cost against an hour of overestimate. For a wafer test: what does a missed failure cost against a false alarm.

Commit this page to git before you commit anything else. The timestamp is part of the evidence. Everything in stage 7 refers back to these numbers, and a cost table written after you saw your results is worth nothing.

2. Get the data, and look at it properly

Your brief gives you a download that needs no account. Write the download as code in a notebook, not as a manual step, and make it work on a machine that has never seen the file. The data arrives as CSV files you can read straight into pandas. There is no database to set up and nothing to install.

Then look at it before you model it. Answer at least eight questions about the table, and not the questions we would ask: the ones you personally need answered before you would trust this data enough to build on it. How many rows are actually usable. What the missing values look like and whether they are missing at random. What the target's distribution is. Which columns are near-duplicates of each other. What the oldest and newest rows are.

Show the answers as numbers and charts, and write one paragraph on what you found that surprised you. Every one of these six datasets has something in it that does not match what the brief led you to expect.

3. Split the test set off, and lock it

Your brief tells you how. It is never a random shuffle. It is by date, or by match, or by customer, and there is a reason in every case.

Do this before feature engineering, before scaling, before you plot anything that includes the test rows. Then leave it alone until stage 7. You get one look at it, at the end, and you report what you saw whether it flatters you or not.

Say in writing how many rows and what date range are in each part, and what would leak if you had split the easy way instead.

4. Build the features, inside a pipeline

Every transformation that learns anything from the data goes inside a scikit-learn Pipeline or ColumnTransformer. Imputation, scaling, encoding, selection, the lot. If you fit a scaler on the whole dataset and then split, you have leaked, and the mark for this stage is zero regardless of how good the rest is.

Hunt for leakage on purpose and write up what you found. Every one of these six datasets contains at least one column that will not be available at the moment the prediction is actually needed, and at least one that quietly contains the answer. Finding them is a graded part of the work, not a preliminary.

5. Do the unsupervised pass

Before the supervised models, not after, and it is not decoration.

Reduce the dimensions and say how much of the variation you kept. Cluster, and choose the number of clusters from evidence, not by eye: report the elbow, the silhouette score, Davies-Bouldin and Calinski-Harabasz, and say what you did when they disagreed, because they will. Try a method that finds round clusters and one that does not, and one that can say "this point belongs to nothing". Look for the points that belong to nothing.

Then do two things with what you found. Give every group a plain-English name and a one-line description a non-technical manager would recognise. And feed the group label back in as a feature in stage 6, then report whether it helped. Both answers are interesting. "It made no difference" is a finding, if you show it.

6. Search the models

You are comparing families of model, not tuning one. Every submission covers all of these, or explains in writing why one does not apply to its data:

For a number: a baseline that ignores the features, ordinary least squares, Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet, polynomial features with a penalty, k-nearest neighbours, a support vector regressor, a single decision tree, Random Forest, Extra Trees, gradient boosting, XGBoost or LightGBM, and a small neural network.

For a category: a baseline that always guesses the common class, logistic regression with both penalties, k-nearest neighbours, a support vector classifier with more than one kernel, naive Bayes, a single decision tree, Random Forest, Extra Trees, gradient boosting, XGBoost or LightGBM, and a small neural network.

Now the part people get wrong. You cannot try every hyperparameter of every one of those. A full grid over that list is millions of fits and several days of Colab, and chasing it is not thoroughness, it is a way of avoiding the thinking. What we want instead is a budget you can defend, spent in three stages:

Stage A, screen everything. Every family above, randomised search, a fixed number of candidates each, cross-validated. Roughly fifteen hundred fits, a few minutes. This tells you which families are even in the running.

Stage B, refine the top three. A tight grid around where stage A landed. Say how you chose the bounds of that grid.

Stage C, one honest number. Nested cross-validation on the winner. The inner loop picks the hyperparameters, the outer loop measures. Report both this number and the stage B number, and explain the gap between them, because there will be one, and it is the single most useful thing in your report.

Log every run to a leaderboard: model, parameters, cross-validated score, the spread across folds, and how long it took. The leaderboard is a deliverable. A model that scores 0.02 better and takes forty times longer to train is not obviously the winner, and we want to see you make that call.

Write down your total fit count and your reasoning for spending it the way you did. The smaller your dataset, the larger that budget should be, and one of the six briefs is small enough that you really can search hard. Notice if it is yours.

7. Compute every metric, then defend one

Compute the whole panel. All of it, in a table, for your final model.

For a number: mean absolute error, root mean squared error, median absolute error, mean absolute percentage error, symmetric MAPE, R squared, adjusted R squared, explained variance. If your cost from stage 1 is asymmetric, a pinball loss at the quantile that matches it.

For a category: accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, recall, F1 in macro, micro and weighted forms, Matthews correlation, Cohen's kappa, area under the ROC curve, area under the precision-recall curve, log loss, Brier score, and the confusion matrix itself.

Then argue, from the rupee costs you committed in stage 1, which single number decides your winner. This paragraph is worth more marks than the models. Say what each of the others would have chosen if you had used it instead, and why that would have been the wrong call here. On at least one of these datasets, accuracy and precision-recall area disagree completely about which model is best, and if you cannot say which to believe you have not finished.

Two things everybody forgets. If you predict a category, 0.5 is not your threshold. Choose it from the cost matrix and show the curve of expected cost against threshold. And if you report a probability, check it is honest: plot the reliability curve and report the Brier score, because a model that says 70% and is right 40% of the time is a worse product than a less accurate model that means what it says.

8. Say where it breaks, then write the memo

Pull out the fifty worst predictions and look at them. What do they have in common? Answer this in sentences, not with a plot. This is what separates somebody who ran a library from somebody who understands their problem.

Check the model does not behave very differently for one group than another, the way you did in Session 10. Say what you checked and what you found.

Then the memo. Three pages, no more. Written for the manager in your brief, who will not open a notebook. What you would deploy, what it is worth in rupees a month, what it gets wrong, what would have to be true for it to keep working, and what you would do next with another month. If your memo needs a single line of code to make sense, rewrite it.
