The method

Eight stages. Every submission has all eight, whichever brief you took. Your work is marked against this list. So read it before you write any code, and read it again before you submit.

1. Write down the decision, and what a mistake costs

Do this before you open the data, in the first hour.

Write one page. Which decision will this model help with? Who takes that decision? The model can be wrong in two directions. What happens in each case, and what does each one cost in rupees? For a delivery estimate: what does one hour of underestimate cost, compared with one hour of overestimate? For a wafer test: what does a missed failure cost, compared with a false alarm?

Commit this page to git before you commit anything else. The timestamp is part of the evidence. Everything in stage 7 depends on these numbers. If you write the cost table after you have seen your results, it gets no marks.

2. Get the data, and look at it properly

Your brief gives you a download link that needs no account. Write the download as code in a notebook. Do not do it by hand. It must work on a machine that has never had the file. The data comes as CSV files that you can read directly into pandas. There is no database to set up and nothing to install.

Then study the data before you build any model. Answer at least eight questions about the table. Do not just answer the questions we would ask. Answer the ones you yourself need answered before you can trust this data and build on it. How many rows can really be used? What do the missing values look like, and are they missing at random? What is the distribution of the target? Which columns are almost copies of each other? What are the oldest and newest rows?

Show the answers as numbers and charts. Then write one paragraph on what surprised you. Each of these six datasets has something in it that is different from what the brief made you expect.

3. Split the test set off, and lock it

Your brief tells you how. It is never a random shuffle. It is by date, or by match, or by customer. In every case there is a reason.

Do this before feature engineering, before scaling, and before you plot anything that includes the test rows. Then do not touch it until stage 7. You get to look at it only once, at the end. Report what you saw, even if the result is bad for you.

Write down how many rows and which date range are in each part. Also write what would have leaked if you had split the easy way instead.

4. Build the features, inside a pipeline

Every transformation that learns anything from the data goes inside a scikit-learn Pipeline or ColumnTransformer. This means imputation, scaling, encoding, selection, all of them. If you fit a scaler on the whole dataset and then split, you have leaked. Then the mark for this stage is zero, however good the rest of your work is.

Search for leakage on purpose, and write down what you found. Each of these six datasets has at least one column that will not be available at the time the prediction is needed. Each also has at least one column that secretly contains the answer. Finding these columns is a marked part of the work. It is not just a warm-up step.

5. Do the unsupervised pass

Do this before the supervised models, not after. It is a real part of the work, not just for show.

Reduce the dimensions, and say how much of the variation you kept. Then cluster. Choose the number of clusters from evidence, not by looking at a picture. Report the elbow, the silhouette score, Davies-Bouldin and Calinski-Harabasz. These will not all agree, so say what you did when they disagreed. Try one method that finds round clusters and one that does not. Also try one that can say "this point belongs to no group". Look for those points that belong to no group.

Then do two things with your groups. First, give every group a simple English name and a one-line description that a non-technical manager would understand. Second, use the group label as a feature in stage 6, and report whether it helped. Either answer is useful. "It made no difference" is also a result, if you show it.

6. Search the models

You are comparing families of models, not tuning just one model. Every submission covers all of these. If one does not suit your data, explain why in writing:

For a number: a baseline that ignores the features, ordinary least squares, Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet, polynomial features with a penalty, k-nearest neighbours, a support vector regressor, a single decision tree, Random Forest, Extra Trees, gradient boosting, XGBoost or LightGBM, and a small neural network.

For a category: a baseline that always guesses the common class, logistic regression with both penalties, k-nearest neighbours, a support vector classifier with more than one kernel, naive Bayes, a single decision tree, Random Forest, Extra Trees, gradient boosting, XGBoost or LightGBM, and a small neural network.

Now the part where many students make mistakes. You cannot try every hyperparameter of every one of those models. A full grid over that list means millions of fits and several days of Colab. Trying to do that is not careful work. It only lets you avoid thinking. Instead, we want a budget that you can justify, spent in three stages:

Stage A, screen everything. Every family above, randomised search, a fixed number of candidates for each, cross-validated. About fifteen hundred fits, a few minutes. This tells you which families are worth looking at further.

Stage B, refine the top three. A tight grid around where stage A landed. Say how you chose the bounds of that grid.

Stage C, one honest number. Nested cross-validation on the winner. The inner loop picks the hyperparameters, and the outer loop measures. Report both this number and the stage B number. There will be a gap between them. Explain it. That explanation is the most useful thing in your report.

Record every run in a leaderboard: model, parameters, cross-validated score, the spread across folds, and how long it took. The leaderboard is something you must submit. Suppose one model scores 0.02 better but takes forty times longer to train. It is not clear that it is the winner. We want to see you decide, and give your reason.

Write down your total number of fits, and why you spent them the way you did. The smaller your dataset, the larger this budget should be. One of the six briefs has data small enough that you really can search a lot. Check whether it is your brief.

7. Compute every metric, then defend one

Compute the whole panel. All of it, in a table, for your final model.

For a number: mean absolute error, root mean squared error, median absolute error, mean absolute percentage error, symmetric MAPE, R squared, adjusted R squared, explained variance. If your cost from stage 1 is asymmetric, a pinball loss at the quantile that matches it.

For a category: accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, recall, F1 in macro, micro and weighted forms, Matthews correlation, Cohen's kappa, area under the ROC curve, area under the precision-recall curve, log loss, Brier score, and the confusion matrix itself.

Then use the rupee costs you committed in stage 1 to argue which single number decides your winner. This paragraph carries more marks than the models. For each of the other metrics, say which model it would have chosen. Then say why that choice would have been wrong here. On at least one of these datasets, accuracy and precision-recall area give completely different answers about the best model. If you cannot say which one to believe, your work is not complete.

Students often forget two things. First, if you predict a category, 0.5 is not your threshold. Choose the threshold from the cost matrix, and show the curve of expected cost against threshold. Second, if you report a probability, check that it is honest. Plot the reliability curve and report the Brier score. Suppose a model says 70% but is right only 40% of the time. It is a worse product than a less accurate model whose probabilities are correct.

8. Say where it breaks, then write the memo

Take out the fifty worst predictions and study them. What is common to them? Answer this in sentences, not with a plot. This step shows whether you really understand your problem, or only know how to call a library.

Check that the model does not behave very differently for one group compared with another, as you did in Session 10. Say what you checked and what you found.

Then write the memo. Three pages, no more. Write it for the manager in your brief, who will not open a notebook. Say what you would deploy, how much it is worth in rupees per month, and what it gets wrong. Say what conditions must stay true for it to keep working. Say what you would do next if you had one more month. If the memo needs even one line of code to be understood, rewrite it.
