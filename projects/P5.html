P5 · The Plant's Electricity Bill

The situation

A steel plant buys its electricity on an industrial tariff. One part of the bill is for the energy it uses, and the plant cannot avoid that. The other part is a demand charge, based on the highest load the plant reaches during the billing period. This second part can be controlled. Right now nobody controls it, because nobody knows a peak is coming until it has already happened.

The plant runs day and night, in shifts. Some of the work is light finishing work. Some is heavy melting, which uses far more power. The operations team can move some of the heavy work to another time, within limits, if they know early enough.

The energy manager wants two things, and the plant engineer wants a third.

The energy manager wants a forecast of consumption, so the plant can plan its purchases and see a peak coming.

She also wants a warning given in the words the plant uses. The operations team does not talk in kilowatt hours. They talk about whether the plant is on a light, medium or maximum load. A warning must come in those words, or nobody will act on it.

The plant engineer has a different question. He thinks the plant really has only about four kinds of day, and that the shift roster does not match them. He wants to know if he is right.

The three questions

As the energy manager and the plant engineer asked them.

1. "What are we going to draw in the next fifteen minutes, and am I heading into a peak?"

2. "Warn me in our own words. Is the plant going into light, medium or maximum load?"

3. "I think this plant really has only about four kinds of day. Am I right, and does the shift roster match them?"

This only helps you get started.

Question 1 is a regression problem, predicting a number of kilowatt hours. Start with linear regression from Session 7. Before that, handle time properly. The plant follows a daily and weekly pattern. If you give a model an hour column that goes from 0 to 23, you are telling it that 23:45 and 00:00 are as far apart as possible. Session 12 shows you how to fix this.

Question 2 is a multi-class classification problem with three classes, and the column is already there. Start with logistic regression from Session 9, then try the trees from Session 10, which usually do well on this kind of data.

Question 3 is a clustering problem with a tricky first step. Before you can cluster days, you have to decide how to turn a "day" into a row. One simple choice is to turn each date into a row of ninety-six numbers, one for each fifteen minute reading. Then each row shows the shape of that day. Then use k-means from Session 11. Should you scale each day first? That is a real decision and it changes the result. You must make it yourself.

The data

Steel Industry Energy Consumption, from the UCI repository. Thirty-five thousand and forty readings, taken every fifteen minutes through the full year 2018 at a single plant. Consumption in kWh, reactive power both leading and lagging, the power factors, carbon dioxide, the number of seconds from midnight, the week status, the day of week, and the load type.

Constraints on this brief

Time is circular and your model does not know that. In the plant, fifteen minutes before midnight and fifteen minutes after midnight are next to each other. In an integer column they look twenty-three hours apart. The same is true of Sunday and Monday, and of December and January. If you give a model an hour column that goes from 0 to 23, you have told it something false. Fixing this properly is the main feature engineering lesson of this brief. Do it before anything else, because it changes every result after it.

Test on the last two months, in time order. The plant in November does not behave like the plant in March.

Decide what you actually have at forecast time, and explain why. Reactive power, power factor and carbon dioxide are all measured at the same instant as the target. If you are forecasting the next interval, you do not have them yet. If you are estimating the current interval from cheaper instruments, you might. These are two different products, and each has its own set of allowed features. Choose one, say which one, and stick to it. If a team uses columns from the same instant to predict that same instant without saying so, it will get a very high score and no marks.

The three load classes are not equally common, and not equally important. Missing the start of a maximum-load period costs the demand charge for the whole billing period. A false alarm costs one unnecessary schedule change. These two costs are not equal, so your threshold should not treat them as equal.

What is hard about this one

This is the easiest of the six briefs, and that brings its own danger. The scores come out so high that you may feel the work is done. It is not. The marks here are not for the score. They are for the four decisions above. Each of them can be made badly and still look fine on a leaderboard.

The really difficult part is the clustering. You have three hundred and sixty-five days. Each day is a curve of ninety-six readings. You have to decide what it means for two days to be similar before you can cluster them at all. Take two days with the same shape, but one is shifted by an hour. Are they the same kind of day, or not? The data does not give a right answer. There is an answer that is useful to the plant engineer, and you have to give reasons for it.
