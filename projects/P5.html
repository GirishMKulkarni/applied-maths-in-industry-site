P5 · The Plant's Electricity Bill

The situation

A steel plant buys its electricity on an industrial tariff. Part of the bill is the energy it uses, which is unavoidable, and part is a demand charge based on the highest load it hits during the billing period. That second part is the one that can be managed, and at the moment nobody manages it, because nobody knows the peak is coming until it has come.

The plant runs continuously, in shifts, with a mix of light finishing work and heavy melting that draws enormously more power. Operations can move some of the heavy work, within limits, if they know far enough in advance.

The energy manager wants two things, and the plant engineer wants a third.

The energy manager wants a forecast of consumption, so the plant can plan its purchases and see a peak coming.

She also wants a warning classified in the plant's own language. Operations do not talk in kilowatt hours, they talk about whether the plant is on a light, medium or maximum load, and a warning has to arrive in those words to be acted on.

The plant engineer has a different question. He thinks the plant really only has about four kinds of day, and that the shift roster does not match them. He wants to know if he is right.

The three questions

As the energy manager and the plant engineer asked them.

1. "What are we going to draw in the next fifteen minutes, and am I heading into a peak?"

2. "Warn me in our own words. Is the plant going into light, medium or maximum load?"

3. "I reckon this plant really only has about four kinds of day. Am I right, and does the shift roster match them?"

The first move only.

Question 1 is a regression problem, predicting a number of kilowatt hours. Start with linear regression from Session 7. Before you do, deal with time: the plant runs on a daily and weekly rhythm, and if you hand a model an hour column running 0 to 23 you have told it that 23:45 and 00:00 are as far apart as it is possible to be. Session 12 is where the fix for that lives.

Question 2 is a multi-class classification problem with three classes, and the column is already there. Start with logistic regression from Session 9, then try the trees from Session 10, which tend to do well on this kind of data.

Question 3 is a clustering problem with an awkward first step. You have to decide what a "day" is as a row before you can cluster days at all. One natural choice is to turn each date into a row of ninety-six numbers, one per fifteen minute reading, so that a row is the shape of that day. Then k-means from Session 11. Whether you should scale each day first is a real decision with a real consequence, and it is yours to make.

The data

Steel Industry Energy Consumption, from the UCI repository. Thirty-five thousand and forty readings taken every fifteen minutes through a full year of 2018 at a single plant. Consumption in kWh, reactive power both leading and lagging, the power factors, carbon dioxide, the number of seconds from midnight, the week status, the day of week, and the load type.

Constraints on this brief

Time is circular and your model does not know that. Fifteen minutes before midnight and fifteen minutes after are adjacent in the plant and twenty-three hours apart in an integer column. The same is true of Sunday and Monday, and of December and January. If you hand a model an hour column running 0 to 23 you have told it something false. Fixing that properly is the feature engineering lesson of this brief, and it is worth doing before anything else, because it changes every result that follows.

Test on the last two months, chronologically. The plant in November is not the plant in March.

Decide what you actually have at forecast time, and defend it. Reactive power, power factor and carbon dioxide are all measured at the same instant as the target. If you are forecasting the next interval, you do not have them yet. If you are estimating the current interval from cheaper instruments, you might. Those are two different products with two different sets of legal features. Choose one, say which, and be consistent. Teams that quietly use same-instant columns to predict the same instant will get a beautiful score and no marks.

The three load classes are not equally common, and not equally important. Missing the onset of a maximum-load period costs the demand charge for the whole billing period. A false alarm costs an unnecessary schedule change. Those are not the same number, and your threshold should not pretend they are.

What is hard about this one

This is the most tractable of the six, and that is a trap of a different kind. The scores come out high enough that it is tempting to stop. The marks here are not in the score, they are in the four decisions above, each of which can be made badly in a way that looks fine on a leaderboard.

The genuinely difficult part is the clustering. You have three hundred and sixty-five days, each of which is a curve of ninety-six readings, and you have to decide what it means for two days to be similar before you can cluster them at all. Two days with the same shape shifted by an hour: same kind of day, or not? There is no right answer in the data. There is an answer that is useful to the plant engineer, and you have to argue for it.
