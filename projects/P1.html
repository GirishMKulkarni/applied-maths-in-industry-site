P1 · The Delivery Promise

The situation

You work in the analytics team of an online marketplace. It does not hold stock. Thousands of small sellers list their goods on it. A buyer places an order, and the seller ships it through a courier that the platform has arranged.

At checkout the site shows the buyer an estimated delivery date. It must do this, because nobody buys without one. When the parcel arrives after that date, the buyer gives a one or two star review. That review appears on the seller's page. It does not appear on the courier's page or the platform's page. Sellers who get bad reviews because of late couriers leave the platform. Getting a new seller costs far more than keeping an old one.

Three people have asked you for something this quarter.

Operations wants a better estimate. The current one is too generous. It is often wrong in both directions, and nobody knows by how much.

The seller-support team has time to phone about fifty sellers a month. Right now they choose by guesswork. They want to know which fifty to call.

The head of the marketplace wants to know which orders will get a bad review, even before the parcel is shipped. Then something can be done while there is still time.

The three questions

These are the questions in the words the people actually used.

1. "If someone orders this today, when will it really arrive?"

2. "Which of the orders going out this week will get an angry review?"

3. "We can phone fifty sellers a month. Which fifty?"

Stuck at the very start? Open this.

These are not answers. They only tell you where to start, so that nobody wastes a week trying to decide what kind of problem this is.

Question 1 is a regression problem. You are predicting a number: how many days the delivery takes. You have the purchase timestamp and the actual delivery timestamp. So you can calculate that number for every past order and learn from it. Start with plain linear regression from Session 7. Use a few obvious columns and get one complete result first. Then compare it with the platform's own estimate, which is already in the data. You have to beat that estimate, not zero.

Question 2 is a binary classification problem. Every past order either got a review of one or two stars, or it did not. So make that column yourself: 1 for a bad review, 0 for everything else. Start with logistic regression from Session 9. About fifteen in a hundred orders are bad. So the classes are unbalanced. This matters a lot when you measure how good your model is.

Question 3 is a clustering problem. Nobody has labelled any seller, so this is Session 11. First build one row per seller. Each row summarises that seller's orders in a few numbers. Then run k-means on those rows. Use PCA to bring the data down to two dimensions. Then you can see whether the groups are real.

The data

The Brazilian E-Commerce Public Dataset by Olist. Plain CSV files, about one hundred thousand orders placed between 2016 and 2018, fifty-two columns in all.

Five of the files have everything you need, and they join on simple keys:

  • olist_orders_dataset.csv is the main table, one row per order, with the purchase timestamp, the estimated delivery date and the actual delivery date
  • olist_order_items_dataset.csv joins on order_id and gives price, freight and the seller
  • olist_order_reviews_dataset.csv joins on order_id and gives the star rating
  • olist_products_dataset.csv joins on product_id for weight, size and category
  • olist_customers_dataset.csv and olist_sellers_dataset.csv join on customer_id and seller_id and give the postcode of each

That is five pd.merge calls, and we are not grading that part. There is also a geolocation file of postcodes with latitude and longitude, if you want distance.

You can download it without a Kaggle account:

https://www.kaggle.com/api/v1/datasets/download/olistbr/brazilian-ecommerce

Constraints on this brief

These are requirements, not suggestions. The rubric gives marks for them.

Split by purchase date. Your test set is the last three months of orders. Not a random sample. A marketplace in December is different from the same marketplace in June. A random split lets your model learn about December from other December orders.

Everything must be known at checkout. The prediction is made at the moment the buyer clicks pay. At that moment the review score, the delivery timestamps, the date the parcel was given to the courier and the freight actually charged do not exist yet. More than half of the fifty-two columns fail this test. Finding which ones fail is a graded part of the work. It is not easy, because some of the failures are hard to spot.

You must build distance yourself. The geolocation file gives you seller and buyer coordinates by postcode. Nothing in the data tells you how far apart they are. Distance is one of the few really useful features you can add.

What is hard about this one

The honest answer is that the best possible score is lower than you expect. Delivery time depends on the courier network, the weather and the season. Almost none of that is in your table. Teams that try to build a nearly perfect model will waste four weeks. Some teams find out early how much of the variation can be predicted from these columns at all. They say so clearly, and then get the most out of what is there. Those teams do well.

The bad-review target has its own difficulty. Late delivery causes bad reviews. So your delivery-time model is almost a bad-review model. You must decide whether to feed the first model's output into the second. You must also think about what happens when the first model is wrong. Make that decision and defend it.

What to do next

  1. Read the method. It lists the eight things every submission contains, whichever brief you chose. It is the specification, not advice.
  2. Read the rubric so you know where the hundred marks are, and which four mistakes lose them.
  3. Before you write any modelling code, write the one-page cost note that stage 1 asks for and commit it. The timestamp on that commit is worth ten marks.
  4. Download the data with code, in a notebook, and answer your eight questions about it.
  5. Split the test set off the way this brief tells you to, and do not look at it again until the end.
