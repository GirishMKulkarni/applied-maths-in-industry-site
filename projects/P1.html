P1 · The Delivery Promise

The situation

You work in the analytics team of an online marketplace. It does not hold stock. Thousands of small sellers list their goods, a buyer orders, and the seller ships through a courier the platform arranged.

At checkout the site shows the buyer an estimated delivery date. It has to: no one buys without one. When the parcel arrives after that date the buyer leaves a one or two star review, and that review lands on the seller's page, not the courier's and not the platform's. Sellers who collect bad reviews for late couriers leave the platform, and replacing a seller costs far more than keeping one.

Three people have asked you for something this quarter.

Operations wants the estimate to be better. The current one is generous and often wrong in both directions, and nobody knows by how much.

The seller-support team has capacity to phone about fifty sellers a month. Right now they pick by gut. They want to know which fifty.

The head of the marketplace wants to know, before a parcel has even shipped, which orders are heading for a bad review, so something can be done while there is still time to do it.

The three questions

Asked the way the people who need the answers actually asked them.

1. "If someone orders this today, when is it really going to turn up?"

2. "Which of the orders going out this week are heading for an angry review?"

3. "We can phone fifty sellers a month. Which fifty?"

Stuck at the very start? Open this.

These are not answers. They are the first move, so that nobody loses a week working out what kind of problem they are looking at.

Question 1 is a regression problem. The thing you are predicting is a number: how many days the delivery takes. You have the purchase timestamp and the actual delivery timestamp, so you can compute that number for every past order and learn from it. Start with plain linear regression from Session 7, on a handful of obvious columns, just to get an end-to-end result on the board. Then compare it against the platform's own estimate, which is already in the data. That comparison, not zero, is the score you have to beat.

Question 2 is a binary classification problem. Every past order either got a review of one or two stars, or it did not, so make that column yourself: 1 for a bad review, 0 for everything else. Start with logistic regression from Session 9. About fifteen in a hundred orders are bad, so the classes are unbalanced, which matters more than you think when you come to judge it.

Question 3 is a clustering problem. Nobody has labelled any seller, so this is Session 11. Build one row per seller first, summarising that seller's orders into a few numbers, then run k-means on those rows. PCA down to two dimensions will let you see whether the groups are real.

The data

The Brazilian E-Commerce Public Dataset by Olist. Plain CSV files, about one hundred thousand orders placed between 2016 and 2018, fifty-two columns in all.

Five of the files carry everything you need, and they line up on obvious keys:

  • olist_orders_dataset.csv is the spine, one row per order, with the purchase timestamp, the estimated delivery date and the actual delivery date
  • olist_order_items_dataset.csv joins on order_id and gives price, freight and the seller
  • olist_order_reviews_dataset.csv joins on order_id and gives the star rating
  • olist_products_dataset.csv joins on product_id for weight, size and category
  • olist_customers_dataset.csv and olist_sellers_dataset.csv join on customer_id and seller_id and give the postcode of each

That is five pd.merge calls and it is not what we are grading. There is also a geolocation file of postcodes with latitude and longitude if you want distance.

Constraints on this brief

These are requirements, not suggestions. The rubric marks them.

Split by purchase date. Your test set is the last three months of orders. Not a random sample. A marketplace in December is not the marketplace in June, and a random split lets your model learn December from December.

Everything must be knowable at checkout. The prediction is made the moment the buyer clicks pay. The review score, the delivery timestamps, the carrier handover date, the freight actually charged: none of that exists yet. Rather more than half of the fifty-two columns fail this test. Working out which is a graded part of the work, and it is harder than it sounds, because some of the failures are subtle.

Distance is yours to build. The geolocation file gives you seller and buyer coordinates by postcode. Nothing in the data tells you how far apart they are, and it is one of the few genuinely predictive things you can add.

What is hard about this one

The honest answer is that the ceiling is lower than you expect. Delivery time depends on the courier network, the weather and the season, and almost none of that is in your table. Teams that go looking for a model that gets close to perfect will waste four weeks. Teams that work out early how much of the variation is even knowable from these columns, say so, and then squeeze what is there, do well.

The bad-review target has its own trap. Late delivery causes bad reviews, so your delivery-time model is nearly a bad-review model. Whether you feed one into the other, and what that does when the first model is wrong, is a decision you have to make and defend.

What to do next

  1. Read the method. It lists the eight things every submission contains, whichever brief you chose. It is the specification, not advice.
  2. Read the rubric so you know where the hundred marks are, and which four mistakes lose them.
  3. Before you write any modelling code, write the one-page cost note that stage 1 asks for and commit it. The timestamp on that commit is worth ten marks.
  4. Download the data with code, in a notebook, and answer your eight questions about it.
  5. Split the test set off the way this brief tells you to, and do not look at it again until the end.
