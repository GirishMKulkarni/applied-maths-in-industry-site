Projects

Everything up to here has been a lab. The data was small, it was already clean, and the answer was somewhere on the page. A project is not that.

You get a situation, a dataset nobody tidied for you, and three questions a real team would actually be asked. Nothing else. There is no starter notebook, no hint about which model to use, and no worked example to lean on. That is the point. By now you have met every tool you need, in sessions 7 to 13B. The work left is deciding which of them to reach for, and being able to say why.

You will spend about eight weeks on this, alongside the finance and optimization modules. You present it in Session 29 and show it working in Session 30.

How this works

  1. Read the six briefs below and pick the one you want. Your team is three or four people, fixed in class.
  2. Open your brief. It gives you a real situation, three questions in the words the client actually used, and the data.
  3. If you cannot tell what kind of problem a question is, open the nudge box in your brief. It tells you which of the three kinds each question is and which model to start with. Nothing more, on purpose.
  4. Follow the method. Eight stages, the same for all six briefs.
  5. You are marked by the rubric. Read it in week one, not week eight.

Choose one

Six briefs, below. Pick the one you want. Teams are three or four people, fixed in class.

If two teams pick the same brief, that is fine, but you may not share code, features or notebooks between teams. The datasets have public solutions online. You may read them the way you would read anything else, but a submission that is somebody else's notebook with the names changed gets nothing, and it will be obvious, because the parts we grade hardest are the parts nobody publishes: your cost matrix, your search budget, your error analysis.

P1 The Delivery Promise An online marketplace · E-commerce and logistics P2 Where the Marketing Budget Goes An online gift wholesaler · Retail and customer analytics P3 The Lot That Fails Final Test A semiconductor fabrication plant · Manufacturing and process control P4 Tomorrow's Air A city administration · Environment and public health P5 The Plant's Electricity Bill A steel plant · Heavy industry and energy P6 The Number on the Screen A sports broadcaster · Sports analytics and live media

Every brief asks for the same three kinds of question, because your submission has to cover the whole module: something you predict as a number, something you predict as a category, and something you find without any labels at all.

All six arrive as CSV files you read straight into pandas. Nothing to install, no database to set up, no account to register for. The download is two lines and your brief gives you the address.

What you hand in

Read The method. It lists the eight things every submission contains, whichever brief you took. It is the specification, not advice, and the rubric marks against it directly.

In short: a GitHub repo with notebooks that run top to bottom on a fresh machine, a leaderboard of everything you tried, and a memo of no more than three pages that a manager could act on without opening a single notebook.

Two rules that decide most of the marks

Split the test set off first, and do not look at it again. Not after feature engineering, not to check a hunch, not once. Every brief tells you how to split, and it is never a random shuffle. In a 2025 study of students doing exactly this exercise, 40.5% preferred the model with the higher score on shuffled data, without noticing the score was higher only because the model had seen the future. Do not be in that 40.5%.

Choose your metric before you see any results. Write down what an error costs, in rupees, in the first hour of the project, and commit it to git so the timestamp proves when you wrote it. Then compute the full panel of metrics at the end and argue from your own cost numbers for the one that decides the winner. A metric picked after the results is not a metric, it is a justification.
