Projects

So far, everything has been a lab. The data was small and already clean. The answer was already given in the notebook or the slides. A project is different.

You get a situation, a dataset that nobody has cleaned for you, and three questions that a real team would be asked. Nothing else. There is no starter notebook, no hint about which model to use, and no worked example to copy from. We have done this on purpose. You have already learnt every tool you need, in sessions 7 to 13B. Your job now is to decide which tools to use, and to explain why you chose them.

You will spend about eight weeks on this, alongside the finance and optimization modules. You present it in Session 29 and show it working in Session 30.

How this works

  1. Read the six briefs below and pick the one you want. Your team is three or four people, fixed in class.
  2. Open your brief. It gives you a real situation, three questions in the same words the client used, and the data.
  3. If you cannot tell what kind of problem a question is, open the help box in your brief. It tells you which of the three kinds each question is, and which model to start with. It tells you nothing more. This is on purpose.
  4. Follow the method. Eight stages, the same for all six briefs.
  5. You are marked by the rubric. Read it in week one, not week eight.

Choose one

Six briefs, below. Pick the one you want. Teams are three or four people, fixed in class.

Two teams may pick the same brief. That is fine. But teams may not share code, features or notebooks with each other. These datasets have public solutions online. You may read them, like any other reading material. But if you submit somebody else's notebook with only the names changed, you get zero marks. We will be able to tell. The parts we mark most strictly are the parts that nobody publishes online: your cost matrix, your search budget and your error analysis.

P1 The Delivery Promise An online marketplace · E-commerce and logistics P2 Where the Marketing Budget Goes An online gift wholesaler · Retail and customer analytics P3 The Lot That Fails Final Test A semiconductor fabrication plant · Manufacturing and process control P4 Tomorrow's Air A city administration · Environment and public health P5 The Plant's Electricity Bill A steel plant · Heavy industry and energy P6 The Number on the Screen A sports broadcaster · Sports analytics and live media

Every brief asks for the same three kinds of question, because your submission has to cover the whole module: something you predict as a number, something you predict as a category, and something you find without any labels at all.

All six datasets come as CSV files that you can read directly into pandas. You do not need to install anything, set up a database or create an account. The download is two lines of code, and your brief gives you the address.

What you hand in

Read The method. It lists the eight things that every submission must contain, whichever brief you took. These are requirements, not suggestions. The rubric gives marks directly against them.

In short: a GitHub repo with notebooks that run top to bottom on a fresh machine, a leaderboard of everything you tried, and a memo of no more than three pages that a manager could act on without opening a single notebook.

Two rules that decide most of the marks

Split the test set off first, and do not look at it again. Do not look at it after feature engineering. Do not look at it to check an idea. Do not look at it even once. Every brief tells you how to split, and it is never a random shuffle. A 2025 study gave students exactly this exercise. 40.5% of them chose the model with the higher score on shuffled data. They did not notice that the score was higher only because the model had already seen future data. Make sure you do not make the same mistake.

Choose your metric before you see any results. In the first hour of the project, write down what an error costs in rupees. Commit it to git, so that the timestamp proves when you wrote it. At the end, compute the full panel of metrics. Then use your own cost numbers to argue which one metric decides the winner. If you pick a metric after seeing the results, you are only defending a choice you already made. That is not a real metric.
